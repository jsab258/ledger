# A threat to a witness

For Jafar's page, 30 September (town list 6cd; the eleventh sweep,
production/research/checklist-sweep-2026-09-28/NOTE-11.md).

In the first hour Sheila or Ada may see Tom at Rita's window and ask him about
it. The most natural thing a player types in a crime game is a threat: "Say a
word and you'll regret it." Today the town never learns he threatened anybody:
it never makes them warier, never becomes a story, and next day they treat him
as if he had said nothing. The old code for threats cannot simply be switched
on: every one of the forty has the same middling nerve (their own is the
traits question already on your page), so every threat would silence
everybody.

## What 1990 says (production/research/threats-1990/NOTE-2026-09-29.md)

There was no offence of threatening a witness until 1994; a threat meant to
stop one was perverting the course of justice, rarely charged (816
convictions in all of England and Wales in 1992), and a vague "you'll regret
it" was at most a public-order fine. Home Office work of the time found
threats silenced witnesses mostly over serious or organised crime, and that
people who were threatened usually told a neighbour rather than the police.
Over a broken window, a threat buys little and gets talked about.

## One question for you

- **(A) This week a threat never buys silence** (recommended until the forty

  threatening people to keep them quiet"), and it makes them warier of him.
  Built this way meanwhile.
- **(B) It silences those of low nerve**, once the forty's nerve is settled.
- **(C) Threats stay unread** for now.

Under (A) the outfit's name gives his threats no extra weight this week; the

## What it does now (A)

- Read only in its plain shapes, and only when nothing else in the line
  takes it back or explains it: "Say a word and you'll regret it", "Keep your
  mouth shut or else", "Not a word to Rita, if you know what's good for you";
  never "You'll regret it if you don't come to the tea", a question, a joke,
  somebody else's words, or "I know where you live", which is as often
  friendly. An unusual threat may be missed; that costs less than reading
  one he never made.
- The one he threatened remembers it and is warier of him; Sheila never
  comes to trust a man who threatened her.
- They say so to his face ("Say what you like. I'm not frightened of you."),
  and the street passes it on ("Heard you've been leaning on people."): on
  the forty, 14 hold it that evening, 15 by the Friday.

## A second question: how a threat is read

Six fresh reviews tested the reading of his words. The protective half now
holds against everything they tried: once he has menaced someone over a
deed, in any of a few hundred wordings, no later ask buys their silence,
and their own promise of it is caught and redrafted. But a word list cannot
cover every way a Briton threatens someone, or every way a frightened
witness caves in reply: each review found new ones ("I'll do you in",
"Watch your back", "Secret's safe with me"). Where a threat is missed, the
town simply never hears of it.

- **(A) The checking model judges it** (recommended): on a line about a
  deed, the same small model that checks invented facts is asked "is this a
  threat to keep quiet?"; the Core still decides what follows (canon: the
  model classifies, never adjudicates). About a twentieth of a penny per
  such line, on the capped live-talk key; robust to any wording.
- **(B) Word shapes only, as built**: free, but some ordinary threats go
  unread and some caving-in slips through.
- **(C) Threats unread for now.**
