# The week's end

For Jafar's page, 30 September (town list 6ca; the first hour you approved:
"The week ends on day 7 ... Sheila, over the real book, 'So which is it going
to be?'"; the outline: wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say, and the
street learns the answer).

## How it goes

- **When:** day 7 is a Sunday, Sheila's day off, so she comes in to the office
  for it, at ten, and waits till twelve; once she has asked, she stays till
  six for his answer, "before the day's out". The
  first time he talks with her at the office from then on, she puts the
  question. If he never comes on the Sunday, she asks the next time he talks
  with her there.
- **With talk off** it all still works: her lines here are fixed, not written
  by the model.
- **Over which book:** Mickey's real book if she trusts him (the question
  above), the day-book if not.
- **His answer is two steps**, as his no to Ron is, because it is for good: a
  line that sounds like one answer gets her plain question back, and only his
  plain yes to that is his answer. A line that mixes two answers, jokes,
  supposes or puts it off is no answer.
- **Refusing to say:** said plainly ("None of your business"), or the day
  she asked ends unanswered. A put-off ("I'll let you know") is no answer.
- **The street learns it:** she holds it first-hand, and anybody where she is
  then (after her hours, nobody else); how far it goes is her discretion and
  the town's talk.
- **What each answer does beyond that** is Act II's.

## Her words

Putting it, over the real book: "That's Mickey's real book. Everything he ran,
in his own hand. You've had your week. So which is it going to be?"

Over the day-book: "That's the day-book. The other one stays where it is.
You've had your week. So which is it going to be?"

On the Sunday, first: "I don't come in Sundays."

Asking plainly:
- "Wind it down, then? Mickey's arrangements finished, and this a cab firm and
  nothing more. Say yes and I'll close the book on it."
- "Take it over, then? Mickey's arrangements and everything that comes with
  them, yours. Say yes and it's your book."
- "You won't say, then? Say yes and I'll take that as your answer."

To his yes:
- "Right. I'll close the book on it."
- "Right. Then it's your book."
- "Suit yourself. The street will decide for you, then."

With talk off, to anything else while it stands: "You heard me. Wind it
down, take it over, or won't you say?"

What the street says it heard:
- "The new owner told Sheila he's winding Mickey's business down."
- "The new owner told Sheila he's taking Mickey's business on, all of it."
- "Sheila asked the new owner what he means to do with Mickey's business, and
  he wouldn't say."

## The reviewers' remaining notes

The fourth fresh review passed it: it tried about 170 lines that are not a
plain yes and none was taken as one. Its narrow notes: a hesitant "Yes,
well." counts as his yes; "Right." or "Fine." alone does not, and he then
has to give his answer again; a few plain answers she does not pick up
("I'll carry on with things", "Actually, I'm taking it over") she answers
in her own words, and a refusal she misses still ends as "won't say" at
midnight.

A third found two more ("Yes, well no." and brush-offs such as "OK, OK." or
"Aye, right." taken as his yes), fixed, with narrower ones. A second fresh
review found four more ("Yeah, yeah." and "Yes?!" taken as
his yes; a second clear answer ignored; her leaving the office the moment
she asked; the answer heard at the office when given elsewhere), all fixed.
The first found five faults (trust earned the same day she asked; "Yes,
take it over." not taken as his yes; asked away from the office; jokes and
put-offs read as answers; a brush-off with talk off), all fixed. Left as
they are:
- Whoever holds his answer treats him a little more warily for it, as with
  word of the police asking.
- A first-hand answer can outrank an older story they hold about a deed, so
  they remark on the answer first.
