# Fit check: a GTA player reads LEDGER

A player who has put hundreds of hours into GTA V and follows GTA VI closely, from the outside only, reads the one-shot description once and says what he would try, what he fears, and what he expects but cannot find.

## The first evening

1. The first thing he would do after the ferry is ignore Darren and Sheila and just run down the quay to see how far the town goes. The playable map is "the Hook plus its working waterfront". He is afraid he hits the edge inside five minutes and the place feels like a stage set, not a town.

2. He would drive car two straight out to the west road to see what is past it. The description says a fare there "comes back as time passed and money paid". He fears that means a fade to black at an invisible line, which in a driving game feels like a wall.

3. He would walk to the quay edge and try to fall in, then drive at it. "Thirty real minutes along the quay edge on foot and in the car, and Tom never falls in" sounds like an invisible kerb, and invisible kerbs are the first thing players laugh at in clips.

4. He would try to jump a bollard, climb a fence that looks low, and hop a wall that is not on the list. "No jump key", "nothing else can be vaulted" and set vault places only: he fears a town full of 1.3 m walls he cannot cross and a hero who moves like he is on rails.

5. He would run someone over at 60 mph on purpose to see what happens. "Nobody dies at any speed" and "a broken leg at speed" will look absurd the first time a body flies off the bonnet and gets up later. He wants to know what the body does on screen, not just what the record says.

6. He would punch the beat constable. The description covers running from him, standing, a word and a bribe, but never what happens when Tom hits a policeman. If the answer is "the input does nothing" or a quiet caution, the police will feel toothless.

7. He would try to start a fight with a random man in the street. "No random street fights" is about the town starting them, but can Tom swing at anybody he likes? If most people are covered by Tom's line, by talk-only prompts or by nothing happening, the street stops feeling like a sandbox.

8. He would type something rude or off-topic into live talk with Darren, then something clever, to see if the AI holds up. "A reply is heard within 2 s" and "a line slower than 8 seconds is dropped with an in-character brush-off": he fears a lot of brush-offs at busy hours and characters that go bland because the claim check stops them saying anything interesting.

9. He would notice Tom is not voiced. Every other character speaks and Tom types or picks four-word lines. In a game where talk is where consequences come back, a silent typed hero next to voiced people may feel like a chat window, not a scene.

10. He would sit through the first fare and ask himself if he would do a second one for fun. "1 to 2 real minutes" and "80p plus distance" with no music in the car: the radio is Pauline, not songs. A 1990 Britain game with no period car radio feels like a missed open goal.

## The first week

11. He would count how many times he hears the same street line. "About thirty of their own street lines" per regular and "at least six lines per situation" for the crowd sounds fine for a week, but over "15 to 20 hours" he expects to hear the same greeting dozens of times.

12. He would walk Quay Street every day and wonder if anything new is there. One main street, "six-bay parade", a caff, a pawn, a few shops: the description says "one port, dense, known by heart", but he fears known by heart turns into bored of it by week two.

13. He would count the street happenings. Six kinds are listed (a doorstep row, a broken van, a shoplifter, a lost man, a loose dog, a woman locked out) and "none comes twice in thirty minutes or twice in a week on one street". With essentially one street, that is a short list for seven game weeks of Act II.

14. He would try to find all the side stories. "25 to 40, found on foot with no markers" and "each 3 to 5 steps": fine if they are good, but with no marker and no map pin he fears missing most of them or bumping into the same three.

15. He would test whether a chase can ever feel big. "It always ends, caught or lost, within 90 real seconds" for every foot and car chase, and "one patrol car works the Hook". He fears chases that feel timed and polite, with no build-up, no roadblocks and no reason to plan an escape route.

16. He would crash at speed to see the damage. "From about 35 mph in one hit the car will not drive on" means one bad corner ends the getaway. He likes consequences, but a car that dies at 35 mph in a chase that must end inside 90 seconds may feel fragile rather than tense.

17. He would look for anything to spend money on. Coats, a torch, a crowbar, a key cut, the barber, a third car, a share of the launderette. "No garage of cars", "no car tuning", three outfits across the game: he fears money stops meaning anything after week two.

18. He would ask how the money actually feels. Starting at "£23.50", rent "£18" a week, fares at 80p: the opening sounds right, but the rise to "running the town" in pounds is never shown in numbers he can feel, and a Money page in words may not give the buzz of seeing a stash grow.

19. He would try the fights again and again to see if they stay fun. The timings are good on paper ("jab 0.25 to 0.40 s", "tells 0.45 to 0.80 s"), but he fears the same jab, guard, slip and counter against "a few kinds" of fighter for 15 hours, and "two against one is deadly" making every fight with two men a forced loss.

20. He would want the escalation to come. Guns are "rare and late", "no crosshair", "no ammunition count", "no gunfights with cover", and a firm of fists is "a real way to play the whole game". He expects one big loud moment at least; if the war is mostly armed police cars and talk, the climax may feel muted.

21. He would test what happens if he goes on a rampage in week one: beats several people, smashes windows, takes a car. No game over, arrest costs "about six hours" for a window and "twelve for a robbery" with "no court scene and no prison". He fears the punishment is a time skip and a Ledger line, which a player learns to shrug off.

22. He would try to break the clock. "A game hour is 45 real seconds, so a game day is 18 real minutes". Landings open "from ten till one", Ada's pot at nine: fine, but over seven game weeks of Act II that is a lot of 18-minute days that could start to feel like a routine loop.

23. He would look at the watch over and over. "The hour shows on his watch face for 2 s" with no screen clock while the whole game runs on windows and appointments. He expects to press N every few seconds, which is a meter by another name.

24. He would try to tail someone and get bored. A target who "looks back about every 20 to 40 seconds" and a tail that has "no hidden distance fail" may be very forgiving or very slow; GTA tail missions are already the ones players skip.

25. He would open the paper map to find something and wish for a pin. No waypoints and landmark directions are lovely in the first hour, but "a newcomer reaches any named place unaided within 3 real minutes" is a big promise on fog-of-war paper.

26. He would wonder where Act II's crime actually happens on screen. The "eight verbs" of sending are "an order given and a line written; a beating is never shown at length". He fears the rise turns into giving orders on digit keys and reading the Ledger while the good stuff happens off screen.

27. He would ask how many proper set-piece jobs there are. The outfit's asks come on "nights one, three, five and seven" in week one, then beats "fire on states". He expects a spine of memorable missions like a heist; the description has systems and beats but no list of big jobs he can look forward to.

28. He would notice the town never surprises him twice. "The real town code uses no chance: the same week plays the same way twice." Great for testing, but on a second playthrough or after a reload he will learn the clockwork and the town will feel scripted.

29. He would feel the weather never changes much. "Never sunshine": grey, drizzle, rain for nine weeks. Moody for an evening, but he expects a clear cold morning now and then to make the wet nights land.

30. He would feel the British port town is oddly dry. Pubs "stand as places only", boarded or serving food. He accepts the rule, but a 1990 dock town with nowhere to go at night may feel thin in its night life, and he expects other places that fill that gap: a snooker hall, a café that stays open late, a chippy queue.

31. He would want to buy and show off something. A third car, a decent jacket and an overcoat, the flat stops being Mickey's. He expects to own a place he can walk into and see changed by his success; "the office fills" is the only property change named.

32. He would wonder how crowded the streets are. No crowd numbers are given beyond "the tuning table's hours". If the Hook has a dozen people on screen at noon, the "whole street watches" pitch falls apart.

33. He would ask what there is to do after the ending. "Play returns in the town at the ending's state within 10 s, with no new main beats" and "No new game plus". He fears a dead town after the credits, with a handful of side stories and fares only.

34. He would worry about the 15 to 20 hours themselves. Act I is "about two hours", Act II "11 to 14 hours" on one small map. If the content is a loop of fares, collections and asks, that is a long time to fill without the map growing.

35. He would test whether the town's watching is actually readable. The whole game rests on "who can see him, how far did that carry", and the description promises a player can answer "eight times in ten" from picture and sound alone. If that fails, being caught will feel random, and random punishment is the fastest way to lose a GTA player.
