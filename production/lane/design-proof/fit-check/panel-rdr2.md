# Fit check: a Red Dead Redemption 2 player reads LEDGER

Read only the one-shot prompt. Worries and questions for a first evening and a first week, as a player who has put a few hundred hours into RDR2.

1. The clock. "A game hour is 45 real seconds, so a game day is 18 real minutes." RDR2's day is about 48 minutes and even that feels quick at dusk. At 18 minutes the ferry arrives, dusk falls and the lamps come on while I am still learning the steering; will I ever get to just stand on the quay and watch the light go, or does the whole town feel like it is on fast forward?

2. Routines at that speed. If Brenda goes to the fish dock at dawn and the street empties at night, every routine runs in a few real minutes. I would follow one person for a whole game day to see if their life holds together; I fear I will see people marching briskly from point to point rather than living.

3. The map is one port. "Not a hundred square miles: one port, dense, known by heart." I like dense, but I will drive to the edge in the first evening and find "the soft edge" where fares "come back as time passed and money paid". After a week of fifteen to twenty hours, is Quay Street plus the docks going to feel like a film set I have walked every inch of?

4. The soft edge itself. Hitting an invisible "the fare is done" at the west road is the opposite of RDR2's feeling that the road keeps going. I would try to keep driving past it on day one; what actually happens to the car and the camera at that line?

5. Interiors. RDR2 lets me walk into most shops, the saloon, the general store, the doctor. Here rooms are enterable "where jobs and stories go". I will try every door on Quay Street in the first evening; how many say "locked" and break the illusion?

6. Shops. The spending list is short: newsagent, caff, ironmonger, outfitter, second-hand shop, car lot, barber, solicitor. With no hunger, no wear, no fuel and no crafting, what do I actually spend money on in week one besides rent and a torch? Money with nothing to buy feels empty fast.

7. No camp and no home life. RDR2's camp gives me people who comment on what I did, chores, songs and somewhere to come back to. Mickey's office and Sheila are the nearest thing; will the office ever feel like a place I want to return to, or only a menu with a coat rack?

8. Ron as Arthur's horse. The one companion who is with me all the time seems to be Ron "at about 2 m". Can I talk to him on a drive, does he have things to say on his own, does he react to the weather, the dark, what I just did? If he only answers orders on the digit keys he will feel like a pet.

9. Ambient life. RDR2's world is full of small unprompted stuff. Here "no random street fights or random encounters" and street happenings come "never twice in thirty minutes or twice in a week on one street". Six happenings are listed. Over a week on one street that sounds like I will see each one once and then the street goes quiet.

10. Street lines. About "thirty of their own street lines" per regular and "a person's own line at most once a day". Over nine game weeks that is a lot of days; I expect to hear Ron's lines recycled by week two, and repetition is the fastest way to kill the spell.

11. Side stories. "25 to 40" across fifteen to twenty hours on one small map, each "3 to 5 steps". RDR2's strangers are memorable because they are odd and long. Will these be found, or will I tick through all the ones on Quay Street in a single evening?

12. Live AI talk. Typing a line to Darren is the boldest thing here. I would type something rude, something about 2026, something about drink, and something about Mickey's death, in the first ten minutes. If a reply takes up to "8 s" before a brush-off, or I get the brush-off often, it will feel broken rather than alive.

13. Tom is not voiced. "Tom's lines are text; he is not voiced." Arthur's voice is half of RDR2. A silent typed hero next to fully voiced Darren and Sheila may feel like I am a ghost in my own story, especially in the cab with Alison.

14. Fare length. "A fare lasts one to two real minutes." That is barely enough time to settle into the car and listen to the passenger. Does a fare ever feel like a journey, or is every drive a sprint between two kerbs?

15. Driving feel. RDR2's horse is about weight and bond; here the car is the horse. If the cab is a "driving study" port that still needs retuning next week, the thing I will touch most in the first hour may feel unfinished. I would test a handbrake turn, kerb bumps, rain and a pole in the first five minutes.

16. Crashes. "From about 35 mph in one hit the car will not drive on" and the firm fetches it. In the first evening I will hit things by accident at 35. If I am walking every time I clip a lamp post, the most-used verb starts punishing me for playing.

17. Fights. The melee sheet looks careful, but RDR2 fist fights get samey fast. With only a few fighter types ("a boxer who guards, a brawler who rushes, a big man slow to drop, a man with a knife") will the fifth fight in a week feel any different from the first?

18. Two against one "is deadly". Fine as a rule, but if I lose every two-man fight with no way to read it coming, losing becomes a cutscene. I would try to fight the two dockers by car two alone on purpose; is there a skill route through it, or is it scripted to lose?

19. No game over and no reload pressure. I like that, but RDR2's honour and bounty give risk a bite. Here an arrest is "about six hours", a beating is under "60 real seconds" to recover. If every failure costs a few game hours, I will stop caring about being seen by about Thursday.

20. Hiding with only the move keys. No crouch, standing still in a doorway "at the doorway's light (0.12)". I would test it the first night: stand in a doorway in front of a watcher at 9 m. If it feels like the town cannot see a man standing still in plain sight, it stops feeling grounded; if it catches me anyway, it feels arbitrary.

21. Reading the town without any meter. "A player can answer from picture and sound eight times in ten." That is a big promise. In RDR2 I still rely on the minimap's witness icons. If head turns and windows are the only clue, I fear I will feel blind and blamed for things I could not have known.

22. The paper map. It marks "only places Tom has been or been told of", and there is no waypoint. Landmark directions from Pauline are lovely, but on a dark rainy night in week one, I expect to get lost and frustrated, then get Tom's own line "once". What about the second and third time?

23. Witness walks. "At least 30 real seconds" for her to reach the phone box. I would break a window and then just wait to see whether she actually goes, whether she takes a sensible path, and whether she ever gets stuck on a lamp post. One witness walking into a wall would undo the whole system for me.

24. Gossip shown, not told. "A deed seen by one of the principals reaches about five of the nine on its first day and then stops." If I cannot see that chain happening, the only evidence is Darren's line the next day. I worry the town's memory will feel like the game simply remembering a flag, not people talking.

25. Hats, coats and disguise. RDR2 lets me change outfits and bandanas freely. Here it is "three outfits across the game" plus a dark coat, a barber "for a week", a scarf and hat. Is that enough wardrobe to feel like my Tom by week three?

26. The rise. Rungs are felt "never counted". I like that in principle, but after eight hours I will want to know whether I am getting anywhere. Will a door held open and "Tommy" from "two or three" people be enough to feel the climb, or will week three feel like week one with more cars on a chalkboard?

27. The fleet without a garage. "No garage of cars", "no car tuning". Buying a third car at the lot feels like it should be a big moment. If it is a line on the board and a man who drives it off screen, the power fantasy is thin.

28. Guns arrive late and rare. I respect that, but RDR2 players will try to get a gun in the first evening. "Before a person brings one, there is no gun to be had" is fine, but what does the game say when I ask Darren or the docks for one on Monday night? A shrug, or a proper in-world no?

29. Animals and nature. RDR2 is full of them; here I see one dog in a yard and "a dog loose on the quay". No gulls on the fish dock, no cats on the walls, no rats by the bins? A port in 1990 with no birds would feel dead.

30. Weather variety. "Dry overcast, drizzle, rain, never sunshine." Grey noir is the tone, but nine weeks of the same three greys with no fog, wind or a cold clear morning might feel flat. I would want the sea and sky to change more than that.

31. Endings. Three shown endings plus "better and plainer" versions sound thin against RDR2's epilogue. After the credits, play returns "with no new main beats". What is there to do in the town after that besides fares?

32. Act lengths. Act I "about two hours", Act II "11 to 14 hours" over "about seven game weeks", Act III "one week, 2 to 3 hours". Act II is where I am afraid of repetition: the same fares, the same three rival ladders, the same rounds. Fourteen hours on one street needs a lot more authored content than this list shows.

33. The VAT inspection as a finale. A detective in RDR2 terms would be the Pinkertons closing in. An inspector asking for "one item each morning at nine" is clever but could feel like paperwork as a climax. Will the last act have any tension with the hands, or is it all talk and Ledger pages?

34. Performance and polish. "60 frames a second, never under 30, in rain and at night" on a wet, reflective street full of lit windows and a crowd. If puddles, rain and lamplight are the look of the game, I expect them at RDR2's level and will notice any pop-in, clipping crowd or stiff animation immediately.

35. Animation and faces. RDR2's people sell everything through faces and posture. Here "posture first" carries all of the town's knowledge: a look held too long, two heads together. If those animations are generic or rare, the whole memory system is invisible, and the town will feel like mannequins that occasionally say something knowing.
