# LEDGER, as the player plays it

This is the game. Every line below is something the player sees, does, hears or meets, and what the town does back. A rule that the player never meets in play is not built, however well it tests. Where a number is given it is the number to build to; every number lives in the one tuning table so that the browser game and the Unreal game are tuned from one home. Canon outranks this page and the owner's rulings stand over everything else in it.

## 1. The premise

Britain, Monday 15 October 1990, about twenty to four in the afternoon. A grey sky. The ferry comes into Meridian, a fictional port town, and Tom Nowak, 32, walks down the gangway with one suitcase and a letter. He is the son of a Polish post-war settler and of Mickey's sister. His uncle Mickey, Michael Suddaby, has died of his heart at the office early one morning; Ron found him, and nobody thinks otherwise. Mickey has left Tom a minicab office on Quay Street in the Hook, the old port: two tired cars, a radio, a bookkeeper who will not use his name, an old docker who minds the door and the rank, a book of five debts nobody can collect, a half-dead criminal arrangement, and an envelope expected at the ferry landing after ten. Tom has £23.50 in his pocket and the rent, £18, is due on Friday. Nobody in the Hook has ever met him. He is known only as Mickey's nephew by name, and his life before the Hook is never asked about and never told.

Fifteen to twenty hours later the port does not move without him. His cars stand on every rank, his men stand at the dock gate, the cold store and the landing, and people step aside for him on the pavement. That is the whole arc: from a minicab office to running the town, felt and never counted.

The minicab firm is the criminal machine, and the player feels all four sides of it. It is cover: nobody looks twice at a minicab waiting with its sign lit at two in the morning. It is eyes: drivers cross the town all day, passengers talk in the back, and the radio hears everything, and anything said on the radio is heard by the office and by every cab. It is an alibi and a wash: every fare goes in the book, and night money can hide only behind fares that were really driven. It is the risk: the firm's name is on the roof, and what a cab does, the firm did.

The town is Meridian. The playable map is the Hook plus its working waterfront, the docks: Quay Street with Mickey's on it, the six-bay parade on its east side, shops on the west block across from the north half of the parade, plain terraces on the west block across from Mickey's at the quay end, the yards and lanes behind them, the quay, the ferry landing, the Old Basin, the fish dock, the cold stores, the dock gate, customs and the Harbour Board. The rest of the town (Copper Row, the Exchange, the Parade, Fairview, Ironside, Gullwing) is where fares go and letters come from, reached at the soft edge of the map.

## 2. The tone and the bar

Grounded noir, wet, overcast and grimy, with dry British wit and a little seaside-postcard smut, sparingly, so that the noir holds. Never American satire. Full period swearing is allowed and there are no slurs of any kind. Late analogue: landlines, phone boxes, answering machines, pagers for dealers and fixers, cash and paper, CCTV only at the bank, no mobiles in ordinary pockets, no internet.

The bar has two halves and the game must meet both. Kingdom Come: Deliverance II for immersion: the town lives by its own clock with Tom or without him, nothing breaks the spell, there are no markers, meters or minimap, and he learns its streets, hours and people the way a newcomer does. Grand Theft Auto for why people play for hours: there is always something to do with the hands, and every verb feels good the moment it is touched.

The pillar is verbs first, talk as the consequence. The player mostly acts: jobs done with the hands, break-ins, fights, driving, working the street. The living town watches and reacts. Talk is where the consequences come back and where they are dealt with, and the Ledger keeps track. The owner's words: "we don't want a gossip simulator. We want an exciting, immersive, novel game." The town's memory and gossip are the engine under the hood, never the show. The player never manages them. He meets them as a look held too long, a fare refused, a constable at the door, a rival who knows too much. What the player feels is tension, risk and a rise to power, and every job is tense because the town is watching. The player's questions are always the same three: who can see him, how far did that carry, and who is walking to the phone box.

LEDGER is not a gossip simulator, not a taxi simulator, not an economy game, not a shooter, not a driving game and not a story on rails. Not a hundred square miles: one port, dense, known by heart.

A moment worth retelling is the measure of every evening. A plan meets a person: the player chose it, it could have gone another way, somebody with a name was there, and it came back later. The friend's retelling the game is built for: he was walking the envelope down the quay when the constable turned round, so he cut through Mickey's yard, and next morning the woman at the window asked what he had been doing in her alley. The first thirty minutes hold at least one such moment, and every hour after holds two.

A tense moment is a stretch in which at least one of nine sources is live and the player has a choice. The nine: eyes (who can see him now); ears (how far that carried); the load (the envelope, a holdall, blood, a weapon: found is worse than used); the window (the landing open from ten till one, the customs lamp out at eleven, Ada's pot on at nine); the walk (a witness on the way to a phone); the law near (a beat, a car, Ellis on the street); the other man's nerve (will he give way or stand?); the odds (two against one is deadly); the two lives (the tea and the landing on the same night). Every thirty minutes hold at least twelve tense moments, from at least four sources, none of them more than half. An evening has a shape: an easy day, a tightening dusk, the job, the relief of getting home, the count, and the sting or the reward next morning. Tension that never lets go is as flat as none; the day shift is the rest between.

## 3. What is never in the game

- No stats, levels, skills, perks or skill trees. Tom rises in position (money, respect, crew, property, clothes, access), never in numbers. He never gets better at fighting or driving; the player does.
- No number of standing, loyalty, fear, heat or reputation anywhere on screen, ever. No rumour screen, no who-knows-what meter. No faction is a number; the town has no opinion, only people.
- No minimap, no waypoints, no objective markers, no marks over heads, no timers on screen, no health bar, no screen clock, no ammunition count, no crosshair (an optional small dot is an assist, off by default, once guns exist), no inventory grid, no weapon wheel.
- No game over. Tom cannot die. Every failure ends in a state the town can talk about, and control returns within 2 real minutes. A mistake becomes a story or a choice, never a reload. Reloading is never stopped either; the game says what is permanent before it happens, and a choice that cannot be undone asks twice.
- No jump key, no climbing walls, no crouch, no swimming, no lockpicking or safe-dial game, no crafting, no hunger, tiredness, fuel or wear, no racing, no car tuning, no garage of cars, no boats or buses beyond scenery, no multiplayer, no typed commands as a way to act (typing is for talk only), no lock-on, no finishers, no combat music, no random street fights or random encounters, no price tables, stock or supply chains, no prison terms and no fines as a system, no long cutscenes and no watched opening, no silenced .22, no scene traces such as prints or heel marks, no gunfights with cover, no wanted level that clears.
- The content rule, from canon, everywhere, in image, speech, crowds and animation names: no alcohol and no gambling, never shown, served, drunk or spoken of (pubs may stand as buildings, boarded or serving food, never entered for drink); tobacco stays; violence stays, including blood and light gore, with no torture and no cruelty as spectacle (a beating is never shown at length); killing is possible, rare, permanent, and remembered by the town forever; a car can hurt a person badly and never kills; drugs exist only as an off-screen economy other people run, never shown, used or a verb; no prostitution and no sexual content; NO CHILDREN ANYWHERE: none rendered, none in the crowd, none in dialogue; the school stands closed and empty; racism and sectarianism exist only as facts about characters, never voiced as slurs, never rewarded; religion is part of life, never mocked, never a mechanic; police are corruptible as individuals, never as a thesis; every brand, band, club, product, weapon and vehicle is fictional, and no real person, event or news is named. Nobody can bet on anything, anywhere, including at the boxing club.

### What the screen does show

Nothing Tom could not know. A prompt names what he can use or the person ahead and in view, held 0.2 s before it changes, and everything usable has a prompt; when two things share a key, one prompt asks which, and a person with something to say is offered before a door. A subtitle appears only with its audio, large, never overlapping and never leaving the screen at any window size, nothing under 24 units. A line low on the screen, the belief line, says when Tom comes to believe something new ("Ada saw you at the yard gate, he thinks"). Money is said in words, never a figure in a corner. The light cue is the picture itself: the corners of the frame darken and lift with the same light value the watchers use, so a player can feel he is in shadow without any meter. The sound cue is the mix: his own footsteps are as loud as their reach, and a street that has noticed him goes quiet. Switches turn off prompts, first hints and street-chatter subtitles. A photosensitivity line shows once guns flash.

The only difficulty is assists, each changing only its own thing, all off by default: the seeing outline (outlines only the people Tom can see who are facing him, never a figure he could not see and never what anyone knows); slower fights (wind-ups readable 25% longer, at most one man swinging at a time); driving help (steering eased more on keys, stronger brakes); longer walks (a witness's walk to tell lasts 50% longer); hints again (first hints shown again from the Ledger); and sound shown, for deaf and hard-of-hearing players: a short word at the screen's edge when Tom makes a loud noise ("door forced", "glass", "running on cobbles"), when the street goes quiet, or when a shout or a car is heard. It shows what Tom hears, never what anyone knows. With every assist on, an impatient player finishes the first hour.

Controls. Keyboard with mouse and a pad each reach every verb, and prompts follow the device last used; keys can be rebound, and held or repeated inputs can be toggles. Every action answers within 100 ms, a press 100 ms early still counts, movement on keys is eased, and mouse look never jumps. On top of the prototype's movement and use keys, the game's verbs sit where the prototype already binds them: Z held while moving walks slowly; M street manners, picked by the stick or mouse; B sit, lean or smoke; N raises Tom's watch; O throws a stone or rattles a bin; I the torch; semicolon held draws a weapon; the digits give crew orders; Comma opens the paper map; Full stop gives Tom's reading of how things stand; Tab shows suggested lines in talk. At the wheel: H the horn, B held looks back through the rear window, R has Pauline say the way again. In any panel: up, down and Enter.

Menus and saving. It starts from its shortcut, shows progress while it prepares, opens at a sensible size, offers quality presets, then brightness and scale, and offers New game, Continue and Quit. Esc pauses everywhere. No destructive choice sits one press from Resume. Continue returns Tom exactly where he left. The game autosaves after every deed, every consequence and every change of a job step, holds the save during a fight and never refuses it; it saves on quit and when Tom sleeps; the menus add manual saves in several slots. A small sign shows while it saves. A reload at any moment gives the same next hour: the town is part of the save and plays on exactly. Nothing about a person's memory is ever lost to a save, a load or skipped time.

One clock. A game hour is 45 real seconds, so a game day is 18 real minutes; the value lives once in the tuning table and every system reads it. Tom's walking speed and every timing in this page are independent of the frame rate.

## 4. The first hour, as staged

The first hour is the owner's approved first hour, restaged to the pillar: hands first, and the speeches spread across the things they are about. Every hint fires the first time it can mean something or when the player hesitates, never on the clock, each once, never two within 12 s; the interface appears piece by piece as each part is first used; Ron or Pauline nudge after a minute of standing about. Every scene is skippable and at most 60 s; under 5% of the hour is watched, and there is never a stretch over 3.5 minutes without control. Something new happens at least every 3 minutes.

0:00. The ferry comes in under a grey sky. One shot, 20 s, skippable.

0:20. Control. Tom walks down the gangway with the suitcase and up the quay, and people look: a dock hand's head turns, two women by the landing pause. If he stands still for 4 s, one hint names the way to Quay Street by its landmark.

1:30. Quay Street. Darren Milner is on the pavement, friendly and owing. Ron Kirby is on the rank and calls him "boss". Ada is at her window. June Suddaby, Mickey's estranged daughter, still in town and wanting nothing from the office, passes with a line. Each is a line in passing. From here Tom can speak to anyone; the AI notice shows once, before the first talk.

1:45. Sheila Dunn's walk-round: her six approved lines, said walking with him through the office and its things. She does not use his name. At the radio Pauline Kitching has a fare and no car.

2:30. The first verb with the hands. Car two, a fare to the priest's house by the chapel, Pauline giving the way by landmarks over the radio. Dusk falls and the lamps come on as he drives. The fare goes in the book: his first clean money.

4:30. Back at the office Sheila finishes at the things themselves: the fare book, the locked door of Mickey's inner office, the flat upstairs and the debt book: five names, £156 between them. Rent is due on Friday.

5:30. The first choice and the first deed. Darren owes £22 and is standing right there. Tom can front him up, take what he offers instead (what he hears), or strike the debt out. Somebody sees, whichever it is.

7:00. After dark Ron brings the outfit's first ask: a dark coat and an envelope for the ferry landing after ten. Go, say no (asked twice), or simply stay away.

9:00. The carry. Three ways to the landing and the player reads them himself: the lit quay is quick and seen; the yard and the lane behind the warehouses are dark and slower; the cab is quickest and known. The constable is on his beat. If he calls out, Tom stands and is searched (and the envelope is under the coat) or runs.

11:30. The landing. A man in the dark says "Mickey's?" The hand-over, and Tom's cut comes back. Home by the yard. The cut goes in the tin. Sleep. The game saves.

13:00. Tuesday. It comes back: Darren says it to Tom's face, whichever it was, the debt or the landing. A look held too long by somebody at the caff. The Ledger opens for the first time, with what Tom knows.

14:00. The count with Sheila: how much of the tin the fare book can hide.

15:00. The day shift: two fares, each a person with something going on (a talker who says something true about the fish dock; a parcel for Rita's side door, "no questions"), then the packing shift from the cold stores.

19:00. Wayne Harrison's £30 at the pawn's back room: goods or money, and the first haggle.

20:30. Lee Drewery's £15 on the quay. He stands, with the docks behind him. It comes to blows, and people stop to watch.

23:00. A side story found on foot, light in tone.

25:00. A night with no ask: Darren's tip (a first break-in) or the late fares (a runner out of the door without paying, and a chase).

29:00. Wednesday. If a witness named him for the fight, a constable comes at ten: go with him, or run. Hours lost, never the game.

31:00. Ada asks him to tea at nine. Alison Sedman takes his cab to the warehouse row and asks about the fire on the way. Julie Kettlewell's £9: mercy or shame.

39:00. Ron with the second envelope at eight; Ada's pot at nine. The tea, the landing, or both, with Ada's window looking on the street he must cross.

45:00. Thursday. Both nights come back. Somebody calls him "Nowak" for the first time.

47:00. Dennis Iveson's £80 at the yard: front up his yard hand, come back for the hut at night, or lean on what he bought out of the burned warehouse.

53:00. If the street is loud, DS Carol Ellis is on Quay Street, asking.

56:00. After dark, two men from the docks wait by car two, for Lee. Back off, run through the yard, or have Ron there. Tomorrow is rent day.

Against the numbers: control at 0:20 (within 1.5 minutes); the first verb with the hands at 2:30 (within 3); talk possible at 1:30 (within 3); a choice and a deed at 5:30 (within 20, aim 6); about thirteen distinct activities by minute 30; the town visibly knows him at minute 13; the retellable moments are the carry and the fight with Lee; a search, a lost fight and an arrest each cost under 2 minutes.

## 5. The town and its people

### Who lives here

The principals are canon's cast, each a whole person with a routine, a memory and live talk: Ron Kirby (58, a docker until 1989, kept on by Mickey for the door and the rank), Sheila Dunn (53, the bookkeeper, at Mickey's since she was 22), DS Carol Ellis, Darren Milner (25, the street hustler), Ada, June Suddaby, Father Brendan Walsh (the Irish-born parish priest, Father Walsh to the street), Alison Sedman (a journalist), Philip Danby (a teacher by trade, never seen at work because the school is closed), Keith Garbutt (the Fixer, broker between all three rivals), and the three rival heads: Geoffrey Agar (old money, lawyers and stamps), Maureen Jensen, the Widow (the docks, muscle and smuggling) and Danny Cammack (the new crew, loud and reckless).

The firm's people run the business whether Tom drives or not: Pauline Kitching on the radio, Kevin Needham driving days and Terry Hodgson driving nights, Ron on the door and the rank, Sheila at the book, and Darren running messages and selling what he hears.

The thirty regulars stand as written, each with a routine, a temper and about thirty of their own street lines (Sheila, Alison and Ada keep shared lines; Ron, Darren, Father Walsh and June have their own). Among them: Brenda Laverack (to the fish dock at dawn), Wayne Harrison (the pawn's back room), Lee Drewery, Julie Kettlewell, Dennis Iveson and his yard hand, Ian Megginson (knows every lock on the street), Norman Dent (nights on the warehouse row), Karen Grummitt (a view of the ferry), Shaun Hewson (paint), Susan Coverdale, Rita Welburn (the back room that fences), Hal (his shop), Grace Rinaldi (whose kitchen a beaten man may wake in). New people introduced by the design (the trainer at the boxing club, the man at the car lot, a second constable, the retired doctor, the other firm's owner, the inspector) are minor and named within canon. Only regulars who knew Mickey describe him, and only in the two approved lines.

The crowd fills the street between them: adults only, in period clothes, generated under the content rule, each kind of speaker with at least six lines per situation (greetings by regard: afraid, angry, knows and likes him, his appearance, neutral; bumping into someone; neighbours greeting each other; talking to oneself at work; rain; a body or a man down). The crowd keeps clock windows by hour (the dock shift at dawn, shoppers through the day, the street emptying at night), and it walks round people and things without clipping or jamming, looks before stepping into the road, and uses the crossings.

### The town lives without him

Everyone named keeps a routine, hour by hour: where they are, what they do, which door they use. Each place in a routine has a cap and an owner, so no doorway jams. People sleep at night; their windows go dark. Lit windows follow routines, so a lit window means somebody is home. The town runs whether Tom is watching or not: a week plays out the same, deed for deed, watched or unwatched, and unseen people go on seeing, remembering and talking. The town code the prototype now runs is the game's own, compiled for the browser, and it uses no chance: the same week plays the same way twice.

Weather and the hour change who is out. Weather comes as weighted profiles (dry overcast, drizzle, rain), never sunshine. One wetness value drives the look (wet street dark and glistening, mirroring sky and buildings, puddles that are water), what is seen and heard, and who is out: rain cuts sight and sound, empties the street, halves the voices and makes the road slippery. The same seed played dry, in drizzle and in rain gives three different streets, the same way every time. Day turns to night with dusk and the lamps coming on; at night there are pools of lamp light with darkness between and lit windows, never one orange wash.

The town keeps a calendar from Monday 15 October 1990 for about nine game weeks. Thursday is market day in Copper Row, which empties the Hook's shops. Saturday's match fills the street at three and at five. On Sunday the shops shut and the chapel is busy. Bonfire Night brings a display on the quay for adults. Remembrance Sunday is kept at the chapel. Routines change on those days as written, and every calendar day's lines and crowds pass the content gate. Nobody speaks of real people or real news; the invented local poll-tax campaign may be talked about and is on the posters.

### Each person their own temper

Every named person has their own nerve, loyalty and readiness to talk. So the same deed goes differently with different people there: one neighbour walks straight to the phone box, another pulls her curtain and tells nobody but her sister, a debtor with nerve stands while another pays at once, a witness with loyalty to Mickey's people keeps quiet. The player learns people, not a system.

### What a person catches

Every act exposes seven slots a watcher may or may not catch: the precursor (what came before: the torch beam in a window, a man hanging about), the draw (a weapon coming out), the act itself, the victim, the actor, the flight, and the aftermath (the broken glass, the body). A person who caught only the aftermath knows only that something happened.

What they knew of who did it climbs five rungs: someone; a shape; a mark (the coat, the car, a limp, a bruise); a face; recognition. Recognition is gated by knowing him, never by distance and light alone: a stranger who saw his face clearly at 5 m can describe a face, never name Tom.

A witness is only as sure as what they caught. A noise or a shape is suspicion, never "he did it". A shopkeeper who saw only a man in a dark coat says "a man in a dark coat, could have been anyone", even if Tom was the only man in the street.

Memory is permanent and per person. Nothing is ever wiped: not by time, not by a save and load, not by skipped hours. Remediation is behavioural: a man can be talked round, paid, frightened or befriended, but he still remembers what he saw.

### The paper and things to read

The local paper ("the paper"; its name is owed by the brand bible) comes out twice a week. Its local page carries what Alison Sedman knows and chose to write, so a story about Tom is only as sure as what she was told. It lies on the caff's counter and in people's hands; people who read it know what it says at the strength she wrote it. Letters arrive in hand: the solicitor's letter from Agar's side, the VAT letter in Act III, notes left at the office. Each opens on screen as a page Tom can read and goes into the Ledger. No real people, events or brands are in print.

## 6. Moving on foot

Tom walks, runs and turns with weight: a man in boots on wet cobbles, not a skater. Turning is quick, starting and stopping have a short ease, and running is plainly louder and plainly more noticed than walking. Walking slowly (Z held, or a light touch on the stick) halves the reach of his footsteps; there is no crouch.

The street camera is at the Between picture's height with an 86-degree lens, pulled back 3.1 m so his feet are always fully in frame and his head level with the far end of the street. It changes by situation, in blends of a second or less: closer in rooms and for talk (Tom 1.5 m from the speaker, both facing), wider in a fight so both men's feet and the watchers are in frame in at least 90% of frames, and its own row in the car. It never ends inside a wall, a body, a car, a lamp post, a shelter or a van; it comes in front of thin things rather than through them. Shake obeys the Reduce motion setting.

Doors, stairs and thresholds just work: a door opens with the use key and Tom walks through without catching; stairs are walked, not snagged on; a locked door says it is locked. Mickey's front has a visible door and nothing inside the office ever holds him against a wall. A door's sound tells the street something: doors creak, hit and latch, and a forced door is the loudest thing in a quiet street. Rooms change the echo at the doorway. Never stuck: from any state the player can move on within 5 seconds by walking away, getting out or backing out, and if anything traps him a short in-world reset puts him on clear ground.

Carrying is felt and seen. A holdall in the hand slows him a little and can be seen; a thing under the coat cannot be seen but can be found in a search. What he carries raises what a witness catches: a man with a holdall on the quay at midnight is a man with a holdall.

Vaulting. At set places only (low walls, yard fences and bins under about 1.2 m, marked in the map data), running at one carries Tom over it in about 0.8 s. Nowhere else: no jump key and no climbing walls. A pursuer vaults the same places, slower, so a chase through Mickey's yard and over the back fence is a real way to gain ground. Every set place can be vaulted and nothing else can.

The water's edge. Tom never walks or runs into the water: the quay edge stops him like a kerb that cannot be stepped off. Cars cannot leave the quay: bollards and the edge stop them, and a car stopped there backs out. In a fight the quay edge is one of the street's places: a man put in the water climbs out at the nearest ladder within a minute, soaked and humiliated, and the town talks about it. Water never kills. Tom, put in by others, climbs out the same way. Things can be thrown in: a weapon, goods, a bag. What goes in is gone, and anybody who saw it keeps it.

Using the world: every object a job or a story needs is used with the one use key and a prompt naming it (a drawer, the kettle, the radio, the fare book, the tin, a phone box, a bench).

## 7. Being seen, and hiding

The first question of every minute is who can see him, and the player answers it from the picture and the sound alone, never from a meter.

Sight. A watcher sees a shape at 35 m, a mark at 18 m and a face at 8 m, and recognises Tom at 25 m only if they know him. Light scales all of it: daylight 1, a lamp 0.7, an unlit street 0.25, a doorway 0.12. A glance takes 0.35 s and knowing who it was 1.2 s; running doubles how fast he is noticed. The watcher's head turns at the start of that 1.2 s, never at the end, so the player always gets at least 0.8 s of warning before he is known. It is symmetrical: if Tom can tell a person is facing him and he is in light, that person can see him.

Reading watchers. The world shows it in bodies: a head turns, a longer look, a pause in a walk, two heads together, a step back at night, a figure at the glass of a lit window. A window is a watcher only when somebody is at it, and then a head and shoulders show at the glass; Ada in her dark front room is the one exception, and the player learns it once. Asked at any moment "is he seen, how far did that carry, who noticed", a player can answer from picture and sound eight times in ten, with the sound off, and with the light cue off.

Light and dark as cover. The light value the watchers use is the light the player sees: the corners of the picture follow it. Dark is readable, never blind: Tom and the ground 5 m round him are always visible above a brightness floor.

Hiding is what a man in a street can really do, with only the move keys. Standing still in shadow is the hiding: a still figure in a doorway counts at the doorway's light (0.12) and is caught at most as a shape, and only beyond 8 m; moving through the same doorway counts at the street's light. Turning his back to a watcher means his face cannot be caught, though the coat still can. Among a group of three or more people within 2 m, a watcher's glance at him takes twice as long (0.7 s). Behind a parked van, a stall or a corner there is no line of sight and nothing is caught, by the same line-of-sight test the watchers use. Nothing on screen says "hidden". A newcomer asked where to stand to be least seen gets it right four times in five.

What gives him away. Each give-away makes a witness catch more than the same act without it: running; the same coat as last night; blood, until washed with water in private (the launderette will wash a coat too, and remember it); dirt; a weapon in the hand (watchers scatter once one is out); clothes wrong for the door he knocks at; bruises, a cut lip or a limp; a holdall; the car. The coat buys doubt about the face and none about the car: a deed done from the car always names the car. CCTV is rare, the bank's among the few, its tape recycled weekly.

## 8. Street manners, stopping, ground rules

The smallest acts are verbs. Street manners sit on one key (M), picked by the stick: a nod, a greeting, a hard look, a shove, and a call ("Oi!") that stops a walker for about 2 s and turns him round, which is how Tom stops a witness on her walk before talking to her. Each is an act with the seven slots, answered in body or voice within 1 s and remembered like any act. A greeting raises nothing; a hard look and a shove are rungs 1 and 4 of the ladder.

Stopping and blending in (B): sit on a bench, a wall or a step; lean in a doorway; light a cigarette. A stopped man who belongs (sitting, smoking, waiting at the bus stop) is caught as a shape like anyone and draws no second look; a man standing about at night without a reason draws one. A seated or smoking Tom is never caught more than a walking one at the same spot, so minding a door or watching a house feels like play: Tom on the wall opposite with a cigarette.

Ground rules. Every place carries a label: public, open by hours, private, forbidden. Public is free. Open by hours is free in its hours and private out of them. Private: the owner or a neighbour warns, warns again, then calls someone or acts, within half a minute at worst; the warnings reset, the sighting is kept for good. Forbidden places (the customs shed, the Harbour Board's back office) skip the first warning.

## 9. Fists and the ladder

A fight is a ladder the whole street watches, good to do and costly to have done. Eight rungs: stare, square up, threaten, shove or grab, strike, weapon out, weapon used, kill. Most confrontations end on rungs 1 to 4: a stare held, a man squared up to, a threat said, a shove into a wall, and somebody gives way. Each rung is an act with the seven slots, so the town treats each like any deed. Threatening is rung 3, said in talk or shown.

Blows and contact, to the melee sheet. A blow starts within 0.1 s of the button. A jab lands in 0.25 to 0.40 s; the committed heavy blow in 0.63 to 0.85 s and cannot be cancelled, which is what makes it readable. Contact is sold by a short freeze, a brief camera close-in inside the wider fight view, a small shake and four layers of sound. A struck man reels to the side he was hit.

Reading and answering blows. Every incoming blow can be read 0.45 to 0.80 s before it lands, in the other man's shoulders, feet and breath, never an icon. At most two men swing at once, 0.4 s apart. Guard (a held key) makes a blocked fist do little, a blocked heavy blow stagger Tom a little, and a swung weapon break through. A tap with a direction slips a blow (a step and a duck, about 0.3 s) from the same tell. A blow thrown inside the first part of the other man's recovery after a slip or a block is a counter: it lands as a heavy blow at a jab's speed. No defence cue is ever drawn. Breath, not a bar: mashing empties Tom in 6 to 10 jabs and he takes 4 to 8 s to get his wind back; the player hears the breathing and sees the arms drop. Skill beats mashing: a patient fighter wins most fair one-on-ones, a masher fewer than 3 in 10.

Targeting without lock-on. Tom slides to about 1 m in the wind-up and hits the man the stick points at. Nobody is hit through a wall. With three men round him, he hits the one he points at.

The grab, at rung 4, holds a man for about 2 s: push him into one of the street's places, take what is in his hand (a knife, a bat), or let go.

The street as a weapon. Walls, doorways, railings, car bonnets, steps, kerbs and the quay edge, seven places first, each a paired move fitted by height: a man shoved against railings, a head against a bonnet, a man put over the quay edge into the water. Nothing shown at length.

Things to hand and carried weapons. Things to hand break: a chair after about 2 blows, a brick 3, a dustbin lid 4, a wheel brace never, and they are left behind. Carried weapons (cosh, knuckleduster, knife, bat) can be seen on him, are found in a search, and must be got rid of. A weapon comes out only on a held input (semicolon), chosen from the two things under the coat, and a weapon drawn cannot be undrawn unseen. What the town makes of a blow rises with the weapon; no number is shown. The quiet blow: a cosh or a fist from behind on a man who has not seen Tom knocks him out for 10 to 30 s; it is a strike with the seven slots and the man may have caught a shape. No prompt and no finisher.

Condition without bars. No health bar, no combat music, no arena. Condition shows in posture, breath, a dropped guard, blood and a limp; a player asked "who was winning?" can say. Fists knock a man out and never kill on their own. A fall against a kerb, a wall or a step within 3 s of a blow can kill, rarely, and the town treats it as a killing. There are no heights to fall from.

Odds, crowds and nerve. A fair one-on-one is roughly even and skill tips it. Two against one is deadly; three is a beating. Other men have nerve: a man who is watched stands longer, and so does a man who is cornered; alone, he gives way sooner. Blood on Tom lowers a man's nerve to stand up to him. Watchers keep about 3 m and scatter once a weapon is out. A man at Tom's side takes the second man, so two against two is fair.

How a fight ends: someone backs off, someone runs (a chase through alleys and yards), someone goes down, the police arrive, or people step in. Backing off and running are inputs at every moment. Tom cannot die. Beaten, he comes round on the pavement, in Grace Rinaldi's kitchen or in hospital, sometimes robbed, with a street that knows he lost, and he is in control again within 60 real seconds with the cost said. Hospital costs days, never money, and plants a witness. Finishing a man who is down is a separate decision, made in the quiet afterwards, never one press. A man down can be searched (a few seconds, seen like any act).

Fighter types. Every fight's men are written as one of a few kinds: a boxer who guards, a brawler who rushes, a big man slow to drop, a man with a knife who keeps his distance. No two fights in a row use the same mix, so Lee on the quay, Iveson's yard hand and the two dockers by car two each feel different.

Tom's own line. Tom never lays hands on a woman, on anyone of sixty or over, or on the priest. It is his line, not the town's: the input does nothing and he says so. It holds for the boxing club and for hold-ups.

Marks that last. A beaten Tom carries bruises and a cut lip for three game days and a limp for one. Others see them (a give-away) and say so. The retired doctor in a terrace by the quay stitches without asking questions, for money, and remembers; the hospital costs days.

Death, bodies and the missing. A killing is rare, permanent and remembered forever. A body never cools in talk and is never wiped. A body left where a routine passes is found by that routine. A man who stops keeping his routine becomes the town's question before anyone finds him: his landlady asks, his mates at the dock gate ask, Ellis asks. A body hidden or sunk is never found, and the missing man stays the town's question for good.

## 10. Driving and the cab

Fun first, as open-world games that are not simulations do it; 1990 by sound, sway, look and relative speed; an automatic gearbox; real figures a floor. Not a driving game: driving stays under a quarter of play, and no drive runs longer than 2 real minutes without a decision, a person or a sighting.

Car handling. On keys the steering eases to lock in 0.2 to 0.35 s and centres itself in 0.4 to 0.9 s; a lane change at 60 mph on keys swings the tail under 15 degrees and settles within 1 s; the pad is analogue; a set course takes the same time within 10% on keys or pad. A handbrake turn works. Rain lowers grip through the one wetness value. The handling never changes without a cause the player can see or hear.

The car camera sits about 5.8 m back and widens with speed. B held turns it to the rear window, for the car in the mirror. It never enters a wall, a car or a post.

Getting in and out takes under 2 s from either side; a blocked door never traps him; a locked cab says so.

Crashes and damage come in stages by closing speed: a light touch does nothing (a touch under 10 mph is forgiven); then dented; then crumpled; and from about 35 mph in one hit the car will not drive on. It says so at once, Tom gets out and walks, and the firm fetches the car: it is off the road for a day. A dent stays until the firm mends it at the yard overnight. A car wedged on a pole or a wall always backs out; after any crash the car moves or can be left within 5 s. A car can ram another car, both take damage by closing speed, and nobody inside is killed.

The cab in the town. It is ordinary: a cab waiting with its sign lit draws no look, by day or night. It is known: every sighting of a deed done from it names the car even when the face is not known, and the roof sign and the plate make it the firm's. It is loud: heard within about 44 m by day, much further at night. The horn (H) is a noise at about 60 m by day, further at night, less in rain: a short toot outside a door brings out a fare or a man Tom is waiting for within 10 s if he is within 15 m; a long blast in a quiet street at night is remembered as a noise and draws a look from lit windows; pedestrians in the road step aside; it never alarms the police on its own.

Traffic drivers obey lights and lanes, flash a car on the wrong side, and always brake for people. Only the player's car can strike anybody. A crewman told to drive is an AI driver and always brakes too.

The soft edge. A fare that leaves the Hook is driven to the west road or the north road and comes back as time passed and money paid; nothing fails for leaving, and there is no loading at the edge.

Driving with passengers. Passengers talk while the car moves: Alison asks about the fire from the back seat on the way to the warehouse row; a fare says "left here" a junction before as well as Pauline's line; a sit-down can happen in the back of his own cab, driving while they talk.

Riding as a passenger. Tom can be driven by Kevin, Terry or a crewman: he says where, by landmark, and can skip the ride, which passes exactly its drive time on the clock. He can talk with the driver on the way.

Plates and paint. A witness notes the plate as a mark. Shaun Hewson's paint makes a car not look like a cab: the roof sign off and a new colour, after which earlier sightings of the cab no longer name this car.

## 11. Cars and people, and the choice after a hit

People look before they step out, so nobody steps out inside the stopping distance of a car doing 30 mph or less, and at town speeds a hit is the player's doing. A hit knocks a person down and can hurt them badly. It never kills. How bad scales with speed and intent: a clip at walking pace is a bruise and a mouthful; a hit at speed is a broken leg; a car aimed at a man is an attack with a weapon (rung 7). A car strike is never a blow under the fall rule: whatever the person lands on, a car never kills.

Then the choice, put within 2 s, covering everybody down: stop and help, or drive off. Stop and help costs minutes and puts Tom's face beside the cab, seen by everyone who stopped to look; the victim and the street remember that he stopped. Help is a call from the nearest phone box or the office (a minute or two of walking and talking), or a neighbour who rings; the ambulance comes in about ten game minutes and takes the hurt person to hospital, where they stay for the days their hurt sets. Drive off is a hit-and-run: the car is described, the police look for it, a police car or a witness who sees it gives chase, and "a Mickey's cab knocked her down and never stopped" goes round. Either way the cost lands on the firm's name and its fares, and the person who was hit never forgets. Neither ends the game.

After a drive-off, people ring the other firm for a while: one other minicab firm works the Hook, a small outfit with two cars and an owner of its own. It takes the fares people stop giving Mickey's and gives them back as the talk fades. It is a business, never a rival and never a crime faction.

## 12. Fares, few and chosen

Fares are the town's daily life made playable, and they are few and chosen: each one a named person with something going on, feeding the crime. This is not a taxi simulator. Kevin and Terry drive the ordinary fares off screen whether Tom drives or not, so the book fills slowly without him; that is the lazy path, and a player who only drives fares can still pay the rent.

Pauline calls the fares Tom is offered. Each is a named person going where their routine takes them at that hour: Brenda Laverack to the fish dock at dawn, the packing shift from the Old Basin cold stores at half past five, a lodger for the last ferry, Alison to the warehouse row. A fare lasts one to two real minutes and pays 80p plus distance. Driving them teaches the map and the hours with no tutorial.

Every fare after the first carries one wrinkle, a small decision, and no wrinkle comes twice running: the talker, who says something true about somebody; the parcel ("take this too, no questions"); the tail (a car in the mirror, or "follow him"); the runner, out of the door without paying; the wait ("keep the engine running"); the hurry (the last ferry in four minutes); the face (somebody who matters gets in).

The radio is the guide and the leak. Pauline gives the way by landmark ("past the chapel, second left, the house with the slippers on the step") and says it again on R. The way she gives is always right: a driver following her words alone arrives. Nothing said on the radio is private: the office and every cab hear it, and so does anyone who sits in a cab.

## 13. Jobs

A job is offered by a person, on a state, never by a marker or by the clock alone. It reaches Tom by a channel that names a giver and a place: in person at the office counter, on the street or at the rank; a message Sheila or Pauline took ("a man came for you, said he'd be at the caff at six"), written in the Ledger; the office phone when he is there, or a phone box call he makes back; a man waiting by the car at night. A tip-off from Darren is a channel too.

Every job is a small graph of saved steps. Each step has a place named by a landmark, who is there, a window on the town's clock, the act the town can perceive, two or more outcomes where it decides, a Ledger line of about 22 words, two or more sources of direction, and a skip the tester can call. The laws the player feels: three keys to every lock, so no job needs talk, a fight or a car to finish; windows, not minutes, with no lateness fail inside a window of at least 2 game hours and a "wait until" for every known appointment; failure continues, so late, caught, wrong man hurt or refused each ends in a state the town can talk about and nothing restarts; every job authored, never rolled; no set piece twice in thirty minutes. Steps sleep while he is away and settle by the clock. Planning is the minute before: which car, who comes, which way in, chosen at dusk from what casing and tails wrote in the Ledger, with no planning screen.

The families are the verbs: fares, carries (move a thing through watched ground; the outfit's envelope is the first), collections, break-ins, lifts, tails, minding, sending (Act II) and sit-downs.

Tails. A tail is the town's eyes in reverse: the target is a watcher who looks back now and then, about once every 20 to 40 s on foot, and at every corner and stop. Being made uses the same ladder as any watcher: a shape is nothing; a mark is a doubt, and he speeds up or changes route (the doubt always shows first, a look back held or a change of pace); a face he knows ends the tail, and he turns and says so, or runs. There is no hidden distance fail. Losing sight is not failing: the target keeps his routine and Tom can pick him up at the next place on it, which the Ledger notes once Tom has heard it. By car the same, with the car as the mark: a cab behind him twice is a doubt, and the firm's roof sign makes it worse. Being tailed is a fare's wrinkle: a car in the mirror. A tail ends in a Ledger line naming what was learned: a place, an hour, a person met.

Casing is a tail on a place: standing a while across the street or sitting in a parked car learns its hours, who holds the key and which way is left open, each written in the Ledger as Tom sees it, matching the town's real routines. It is done close, which is its risk.

Minding: stand at someone's door, sit in the car, be seen at someone's shoulder. The town records who stood with whom ("Tom and Ron were at the gate" is a sighting), and what minding pays is set in each job. Escorting someone out of danger is minding.

Tails, minding and lifts are authored instances, each with its outcomes, like every job.

Side stories: 25 to 40, found on foot with no markers, mostly light or grey against the main story, each 3 to 5 steps and ending in something the town knows. Some are offered only after earlier steps. They run beside the acts from week one.

Street happenings are small authored scenes, placed by the town's state and the hour, never by dice: a row on a doorstep between two people who have reason; a van broken down across the lane; a shoplifter running from the grocer (the grocer open and a known thief out); a lost man with a suitcase; a dog loose on the quay; a woman locked out. Each takes under two minutes, offers a choice with the hands (help, ignore or harm; watch, join or break up a row), and leaves something the town knows. None comes twice in thirty minutes or twice in a week on one street. A stranger Tom helped can turn up days later with a small favour: a word, a lift, a warning.

Finding the way without markers. Every door that matters has a number and every street a name plate at its ends. A job, a fare and a letter give an address and a landmark. People asked the way give it by landmark if they know it and say "no idea, try the caff" if they do not; shopkeepers and the constable always know. Lost (90 real seconds without progress towards the place in the Open list, moving away or circling), Tom's own line notes the landmark again, once ("the chapel, then second left"). After three minutes, the next person he passes within 5 m offers the way. A newcomer reaches any named place unaided within 3 real minutes.

## 14. Lifts, the fence and the shops

Lifts. Goods carry a hot mark that fades with value and distance, while the owner never forgets them: he recognises his own goods in a shop window. Goods off a van or a lorry are a lift.

Lifting from a pocket. Beside or behind a person in a crowd, a queue or a packed caff, one held press of about 2 s takes the one thing worth taking (authored: a key, a letter, a wallet). Whether he feels it follows fixed limits, never a roll: an attentive man, good light or fewer than three people near means he feels it; a distracted man in a crowd of three or more in poor light does not. Before he feels it his head turns, and Tom can stop. Felt, it is a deed with the seven slots and he turns on Tom there and then. A lifted key is missed by morning, so the owner knows when it went and suspects only the people he noticed near him.

Purses, tills and shops. Everybody's money is somebody's circumstance: a debtor, a shopkeeper or a fence pays only what is in the purse or the drawer. Shops keep hours from their people and doors and restock weekly; Thursday's market empties them. A shop buys, haggles or refuses, and remembers: a shopkeeper remembers a robber, a man who walked out of a haggle, and a man who robbed the shop next door. A haggle is three offers in talk at most: the buyer names a price, Tom asks more, takes it, or walks. The buyer's answers come from his purse, how hot the goods are and how he regards Tom; his face and words show how close Tom is, and no number shows except the prices spoken. Walking away is remembered: he may call Tom back, or not next time.

Fencing. Rita's back room fences at about 0.4 of an honest price, and opens at rung 2. Rita refuses or lowballs goods whose deed is still hot in talk. Wayne's pawn takes goods too.

Spending. The newsagent and tobacconist (cigarettes, the paper), the caff (a meal, a place to sit), the ironmonger (a crowbar, a torch, a key cut), the outfitter and the second-hand shop (coats), the car lot by the north road (a car), the solicitor (a brief on call), the barber, and bribes. Each shop sells only its list, by its hours, from its purse.

## 15. Finding a deed nobody saw

Every unseen deed leaves its traces: a forced door, glass, a missing thing, a body. Whoever's routine next brings them past finds it: the owner on his return, the cleaner at six, the first dock worker. The finder is a witness of the aftermath only, and reports or tells as a witness would. Nothing is found by a chance roll, only by routines. A body left where a routine passes is found that way; a body hidden or sunk is never found, and the missing man becomes the town's question instead. The player learns that where he leaves a thing matters as much as who saw him leave it.

## 16. Witnesses: the walk, and reaching them first

Being seen is not failure; it is the fork. A witness who means to tell sets off, to a friend, a phone box or the station, and is seen going: a woman pulls on her coat and walks briskly towards the phone box at the corner. No mobiles in 1990, so the walk is the timer, and when it starts in Tom's sight it lasts at least 30 real seconds (50% longer with the assist).

Tom can reach her first: call "Oi!" to stop her, then talk her round, pay, threaten, help, or let her go. A threat can stop a report to the police, never the street's talk: she may not sign, but she will tell her sister. Paid, she remembers being paid. Each answer changes the report and the talk differently, and her temper decides how hard she is to pay or frighten. A neighbour who sees him put a window in reports him unless she is on his side (Ada's tea, a friend) or talked round. Mickey's people never go to the police about one of their own. Some never go because of their trade: Rita, Wayne, Hal, the scrap dealer, the customs officer, the outfit's man. A call from the phone box brings the constable to the spot that night; the signed statement is made at the station at nine next morning, and until she has signed, Tom can still reach her.

The crowd after a deed. People within a few metres look; at least one gathers and drifts back; witnesses talk quieter for a game hour; bystanders who saw a weapon flee.

A street on edge. Three seen deeds on one street within two game days set it on edge: doors locked earlier, windows watched longer, witnesses setting off to tell sooner, shops shut early, and the constable walking it more often. It eases as the talk cools; the memories stay. One deed alone does not do it. It shows in people and doors, never a meter.

## 17. Statements, arrests and charges

Talk alone brings questions, never an arrest. An arrest needs a signed statement from someone who knew it was him, or an officer who saw it. An unprovoked blow is a deed like any other.

An arrest costs hours, never the game, and under 2 real minutes of play: a constable walks up (go with him, or run), the station, the duty solicitor (free), the hours pass. About six hours for a window (two and a caution if he owns up), eight for a wounding, twelve for a robbery, twenty-two for a killing and then bail. The coat is kept if he wore it. He is never taken twice for one deed; each new deed is its own matter. The street sees him go and says so.

An arrest ends in a caution, or a charge and bail. A charge joins Ellis's case and is read at the ending; there is no court scene and no prison. Once Ellis reaches investigating she may search the office once, always with notice the evening before (a constable's word at the door, Sheila's warning), and what is stored there counts for the case.

## 18. The police

The police are a pressure Tom can read and answer, never the end of the game. Corruptible as individuals, never as a thesis.

The beat. A constable walks Quay Street and the quay at night. He is always heard or seen at least 3 s before he can challenge: footsteps, a torch, a cough, the shape under a lamp. He challenges on suspicion: a man carrying at night, running, bloodied, or where he should not be. He calls out first, always.

The challenge has three answers. Stand, and he searches: he finds exactly what is under the coat and in the hand, and what he finds he keeps. Run, and it is a chase. Or a word: talked round if he has no cause; a note offered if he is one of the bought men; offered to an honest one, the note is a crime he saw.

Police after a deed. Once a deed is reported, the beat constable (or a car at a manhunt) goes to the spot and stays a while; a body brings tape, a torch at night and Ellis next morning. After a lost chase he walks to the last place he saw Tom and looks round there for about a game hour (half a real minute at most on the spot), so the law stays near. The spot is watched ground for the rest of the night: going back there is the risk. Nothing on screen marks where he searches; Tom reads it from the torch, the voice and the parked car. Seeing Tom again starts a new chase, called out first.

DS Carol Ellis and the four stages. Ellis is already on the warehouse fire. She comes to Quay Street for a body, for a detective's crime once reported (a robbery, a wounding, a hold-up), and from the second day for talk, once three people of Tom's day world pass on talk of his nights; for a reported window or door, she comes a morning after the statement, with a constable at ten the morning after that. People she asks remember being asked and tell Tom. Her interest has four stages, each with its sign, and no stage is skipped unseen: nothing; asking round (her name in the street's talk, "a lady detective was asking about you"); investigating (an officer at his door, the office search with notice); a manhunt (a car on the street and his description said aloud). Even after a witnessed killing, the manhunt's sign shows at least one game hour before an arrest can land.

Traffic police and stops. One patrol car works the Hook by day and night on a route; more only at the dial's War. Reckless driving in its sight starts a stop: a hit, driving on the pavement, a red light crossed at speed in sight, or a speed far over the game's band. Ordinary fast driving is never stopped. The car flashes and sounds; pull over and the constable looks at the car and the driver (a search if he has cause), notes the plate and gives a caution, under 2 real minutes; refuse and it is a chase. The plate noted joins what the police know of the car.

Bought men. Two named officers in the Hook can be paid or leaned on, each with a price and a limit: one looks away from a car; the other loses a statement once. Buying one is an act a colleague may see, and that colleague keeps it. The station as a whole never changes. Ellis herself is not among them.

Armed response. At dial state Fired, armed officers come from the station for gun calls, slowly. At War the police bring in armed cars, stop and search on the street, and everyone is jumpier.

## 19. Chases on foot and by car

Fleeing on foot. Run from a constable and it is a chase through the Hook's back ways: ginnels, yards, bollarded lanes a car cannot follow, the vault places. It is lost once Tom is unseen for 7 s or 70 m, and it always ends, caught or lost, within 90 real seconds. A player asked "how did you lose him?" can say: "over the yard wall and into the lane".

Pursuers give up at the rule and never cheat: no pursuer sees through a wall, knows where Tom went without seeing, or catches by a speed Tom cannot match on a straight. After losing him, the search of the last place seen.

Pursuing on foot. Somebody runs from Tom: a runner fare, a man from a fight, a witness heading for the phone. He runs at Tom's run speed less about 5%, so a straight chase closes slowly and a clever line closes fast; he takes the routes of his own routine, through his yards and alleys. Catching him is a hand on the collar when Tom is within 1 m behind, then the choice: let go, talk, or the lower rungs. Breaking sight for 7 s or 70 m loses him, and he goes home or to a friend, where he can be found later. Every foot chase ends within 90 real seconds.

Car chases. A hit-and-run seen by an officer, a refused stop, or a car that saw the deed: the patrol car gives chase with its bell and lights. It ends caught or lost within 90 real seconds, lost by breaking sight (down a lane, round the basin, behind the cold stores) for 7 s or 70 m. Caught, it is a stop or an arrest, never a game over. Nobody shoots from a moving car.

## 20. Money in words, and the Ledger

Two pots. Clean money is in the fare book and can be spent anywhere. Night money is in the tin, kept at the office, and cannot be explained. Tom starts with £23.50; the rent, £18, is due every Friday; drivers' wages come out of the book weekly. Doing nothing misses Friday's rent; a week of fares alone pays it. The opening is hard and never a dead end.

No money from nowhere: every pound in or out is a flow from a person, a purse, a fare, a till, a wage or a bribe, and every pound balances. The firm's takings are said when they come in.

The Ledger is Tom's notebook of people and events, opened in 120 ms by key or pad, with nothing under 24 units, and any person found in three inputs. It is the one place the player sees where he stands, and it holds only what Tom saw, heard or was told, with how sure he is from how he learned it: said to his face is sure; saw them looking is "thinks so"; two heads together is "guesses". Nothing is erased: a wrong belief is struck through with the day and the reason. Every perceivable change (a job failed in front of him, a door shut in his face, money lost, a witness he could see or hear, an officer coming into view) is shown by the world within 3 s and written in the Ledger within a game minute; a change he could not perceive never appears on screen or in the Ledger and surfaces only through the world, later.

Its pages:
- People: what Tom knows of each, and what he believes they know of him. Never another mind's numbers.
- The Day book: events in order, each job step done or failed a line of about 22 words.
- Open: jobs, debts, promises and appointments, each naming a person and a landmark; what Tom carries, in words. A newcomer can answer "what next, and where" from it alone.
- Money: in words. "£31 in your pocket. The tin has about £40 in it. Rent, £18, Friday. Darren still owes £12." Asked what he has, the player can say.
- The paper map: a drawn map of the Hook, not a list, marking only the places Tom has been or been told of. Opening it (Comma) stops Tom but pauses nothing.

## 21. The count and the wash

At the count, with Sheila, once a day and in twenty seconds: the tin on one side, the fare book on the other. The fare book can hide £10 plus 80% of the fares really driven since the last wash, by Tom and by the firm's drivers. Over that the books are off. Sheila says so before she does it ("That's more than the book will stand, love. You sure?"), thinks less of him for it, and the inspection in Act III reads every such week. A front's till raises what can be washed by a set amount a week. A fare written for an hour Tom was somewhere else is an alibi the police can check, and a lie in the book that somebody may one day have to hold up. This one rule ties the day to the night: more real fares, more night money made clean. Never a spreadsheet.

## 22. The crew, orders and men at his side

Tom's power is people. Crew are named residents with a routine, a need, a nerve and a loyalty with a ceiling, and they act on orders.

Recruiting comes from the thirty regulars by one of two roads: meet a need, which is slow and holds (Julie Kettlewell, laid off, wants work and can drive); or spend a hook, which is quick and brittle. Each recruit brings a use the firm lacks: Ian Megginson's locks, Lee Drewery's fists, Norman Dent's nights on the warehouse row, Karen Grummitt's view of the ferry, Shaun Hewson's paint. Men who train at the boxing club can be recruited there.

Orders take two inputs at most (a digit, then a target) and are answered in voice or body within 1 s; a man who cannot do it says why: with me; wait here; watch that; fetch him; take this there; drive; lean on him. Crew never do what cannot be undone unless told.

A man at his side follows at about 2 m, waits at doors, rides in the car, and stands in a fight, taking the second man. He never blocks a door or a lane, never lags more than 10 s, and never fails a job by being slow. He is a witness the town sees with Tom. With Ron at his side, the two dockers by car two are a fair fight.

Sending, from Act II, the eight verbs, each through a named man: have a named man hurt through a named man; point a plan at a person; frame someone through what the town believes; lean on a witness to change what they say; move or hide a body; dock, expel or beat one of his own; send men at a rival's property; have someone vouch for him. An order is given and a line is written; a beating is never shown at length. Each sent verb leaves a chain the town can trace, and the man sent knows.

Nerve, loyalty and breaking. The accomplice is the risk, not the alibi: each man sent is a second person who knows. Past his nerve he talks, to a rival or to the police, and the sign always comes first: he looks away, he asks for his cut early, he is not at his post.

Crew pay and the cut. A driver gets a weekly wage from the fare book; drivers leave if unpaid or frightened. A man on a night job gets a cut agreed in a line of talk before it, paid from the tin. Going alone keeps the whole take but brings no second man. Paid short or late, a man's loyalty falls and he asks for it first, the sign before breaking; paid fairly, he holds longer.

Crew fates. Crew can be poached (the Widow takes his least loyal man), frightened off, arrested, docked or expelled by Tom, and killed for good. Each fate is a town fact.

Getting ready, at dusk at the office: the coat (the dark coat goes on with one key), two things under the coat (a weapon, the envelope, a key, a tool, the torch), one thing in the hand or a holdall, which car, who comes. The rest lives at the office: a locked drawer for small things, the yard shed for big ones, and the tin. A constable's search finds what is under the coat and in the hand; the office search finds what is stored there. What is stored survives save, load and skipped time. Getting ready takes a minute, never a screen.

Getting rid of things: dropping a weapon down a drain, throwing a bag in the dock (gone, and remembered by anyone who saw), washing blood off with water and privacy, or the launderette, which cleans the coat and plants a witness. A body can be moved by Tom or by a sent man.

## 23. The board, the fleet and the ranks

The board is a chalkboard in the office with a line for each car and driver, placed by the last radio call, and the week's fares. It is where a car is put on a rank or a shift. A car on a rank is the firm's eyes there: its driver's sightings reach the radio, and a cab placed where Tom will be gives him an alibi or a witness. A minute a day at most; if he never touches it, Pauline runs it.

The fleet grows by people, never a shop screen. A car is bought from a man selling one or from the used-car lot by the north road; a driver is hired from the regulars or a stranger who asks at the counter. Every car and driver is on the board and named in the Ledger, never as a number. His cars pass him in the street.

Ranks and accounts. The rank outside Mickey's is his. The ferry rank and a second rank are won by being there with a car on the board enough days, or by a word with whoever holds them. Accounts add fares to the book: first the cold-store shift contract, then the Harbour Board's late men, then a solicitor's office.

## 24. The three rivals

Three, each going after a different part of him, each knowing only what its own people have seen or heard:
- Maureen Jensen, the Widow: the docks, muscle and smuggling. She goes after his people: a tax on his drivers at the dock gate, his least loyal man poached.
- Geoffrey Agar: old money, lawyers and stamps. He goes after his clean money with paper: the private-hire licence, a solicitor's letter, an inspection of the cars.
- Danny Cammack: the new crew, loud and reckless. He goes after his cover: trouble on Quay Street that brings heat Tom did not earn.

All three want the Hook because it is the port's gate (the ferry, the Old Basin, customs, the Harbour Board) and Mickey's death left it open.

Rival eyes. Each has eyes among the regulars: Lee Drewery and Kevin Needham for the docks, Susan Coverdale for Agar, Rita Welburn for Cammack. So the first thing the Widow learns of Tom may come from his own day driver, over a radio nobody can help overhearing. Every rival move traces to something one of its people saw or heard.

Rival ladders. Each rival climbs the same ladder, never skipping a rung, and each rung is said to his face or shown on his street: noticed (a man at the rank looks him over; Kevin mentions a woman asking about the new owner); a visit (two of the Widow's men at the office counter, polite); pressure (the dock gate tax; the licence letter; a fight Cammack's boys start outside Mickey's); a threat; a sit-down.

Pressures and answers. Every pressure has three answers: pay, defy, or counter with what he knows. None ends the game. What rises with him is risk: more men who know, more eyes on the firm, rivals one rung higher.

The Fixer. Keith Garbutt sells true reads of a rival (true to what the town really holds), a meeting, and one truce.

## 25. Fronts and the cold-store contract

A front is a share of a real business on the street (the launderette, the caff, the empty unit once let), got by money, a favour or pressure. It washes night money through its till (raising what the count can wash by a set amount a week), hides men and things, and is a place his men are. A round is a set of payments collected on a day by a man of his crew; trouble on it comes back to Tom through that man. Each front and round has its named man with his nerve and loyalty, and is a target for the rivals. No stock, no prices, no sliders, no screen: money arrives as the man brings it, short if the man is.

The cold-store shift contract, cabs for the packing shift morning and evening, is the legitimate cover: the daily loop that earns and explains money, won at rung 3.

## 26. The rise, heard in what people call him

Four rungs, and no number anywhere:

| Rung | What the town calls him | What he holds | What opens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. The nephew | the new owner | two cars, two drivers, Ron, Sheila, £23.50, the debt book, the run | the street, the rank, the landing |
| 2. The firm is his | Nowak | his answer to Sheila, a third car, a first recruit | Mickey's locked office and the real book (on trust), Rita's back room |
| 3. The Hook's firm | Tom | five cars, a night desk, a first front, a first round, the cold-store shift contract | the dock gate, the Harbour Board's counter, a solicitor |
| 4. The port's man | Tom to most, Tommy to two or three | fronts and rounds across the Hook, men at the dock gate, the cold store and the landing, a brief on call | the sit-down; people step aside |

The name ladder. Each person uses the name they have reached: the new owner, then Nowak, then Tom, then Tommy. The gate is knowing, not liking: a man who fears him may still call him Tom. A step never goes back, and each step up is heard within a game day of being reached. Sheila is the exception: she withholds his name until she trusts him, as she promised Mickey; her trust comes with time and a clean week, a week in which he never asked her to put the books off.

Greetings by regard are how the rise is heard: a nod, a door held, a path cleared, "morning, Mr Nowak", or a street that goes quiet. Afraid, angry, liking, his appearance or neutral each have their lines. Walked at rung 1 and at rung 3, the same street gives at least three differences a newcomer can point to.

Access as soft keys: standing, dress, an introduction, an appointment the Fixer brokers. A filthy man is refused at doors that would open to him clean; a decent jacket gets him past the Harbour Board's counter.

Property that reacts: the office fills (a night desk, more phones, the board crowded), and the flat above stops being Mickey's.

Tom's wardrobe: three outfits across the game (as he arrived, a decent jacket, a good overcoat), each changing how strangers speak to him and which doors open, and a dark coat for nights. A second coat bought after a deed breaks the mark rung for anyone who saw the first, so they can catch only a shape. A barber changes the look for a week. A scarf up and a hat down at night break the face rung but are themselves a mark and draw a look by day.

The hard opening stays hard: in week one he can be refused, ignored and knocked down.

## 27. Guns and the escalation dial

The game starts with no gun in the Hook. Guns come only through people: bought, stolen, taken, or found once at Mickey's. They are rare and late, after melee, and AAA when used: a gun in the hand, an aim row on the camera, a shot that sounds like a shot. A gun can be used as a club (a strike that also shows the gun); a warning shot is a shot. No silenced .22, no ammunition count, no crosshair.

The dial is a fact in the town's memory, not a meter, in four states:
- 0, Fists. Nobody carries a gun.
- 1, Shown. A gun has been seen. It is news across town, and the rivals' top men start carrying.
- 2, Fired. A shot is heard to about 177 m by day; whoever hears it keeps it for good. Armed officers come from the station for gun calls, slowly. Ellis's interest rises a step. Only named men carry.
- 3, War. A few named men on each side carry, and everyone knows who. The police bring in armed cars and stop and search on the street. Watchers run sooner and report more; night fares fall. A shot can now kill, and each death is a named, permanent event.

It rises with what the town learns and with who pushed: arming his crew or buying from the docks moves it, and the town remembers who brought the guns in. It never moves on a deed nobody saw or heard. It falls one step only after about two quiet game weeks, and never back to 0. Even at War guns stay rare: a few named carriers per side and few deaths an act. Staying a firm of fists and bats is a real way to play the whole game.

## 28. Talk, where the consequences come back

Talk is the moat and never the main thing the player does. It has five uses and no others: it comes back (said to his face, once, by somebody who can tell it is him); the deal (a job offered, taken or refused); the witness (talked round, paid or threatened); the question (the way, a name, what somebody saw); and the sit-down.

Live talk. Anyone named can be spoken to: live talk for every named character where possible, the main characters at least, only when the player speaks to someone. Before a live answer, a short thinking sound and a glance, cut when the answer starts, never a loading sign. A reply is heard within 2 s or the written line plays; a line slower than 8 s is dropped with an in-character brush-off and a small sign, and offline the written street carries on the same way. No prepared openings, no skill checks, no mission nags.

A character says only what the simulation knows. The claim check refuses any sentence naming a person, place, event, number or year the speaker's facts lack; replies cite their facts. Ada knows what Ada saw and was told, and no more; a man who only heard of last night says "I heard something about you", never what happened. Live talk names no real make, brand, shop, club, programme, paper or public figure, and nothing after 1992.

Written street lines. Everything the town says unprompted is written ahead and checked once: about 7 words a street line, a remark said within 7 m, everyday street talk at most every 45 s, a person's own line at most once a day and a shared line once a week per street, at least three closing lines each.

Decision lines. Every line that decides is the game's own: a yes, a no and a line that keeps talking. Only a plain yes is taken; anything that cannot be undone asks twice; Leave is always offered. Talk never decides alone.

Suggested lines, about 4 words, on Tab on a keyboard and shown at once on a pad; written for hellos, goodbyes and decisions, the small model's otherwise.

The AI notice shows once, before the first talk. Any live line can be reported from the pause menu or by a key.

How the player speaks. On a keyboard he types or picks a suggested line; on a pad suggested lines show at once and an on-screen keyboard is there. Tom's lines are text; he is not voiced. A whole talk, decision included, can be done by pad alone.

The talk screen. Tom stands 1.5 m from the speaker and both face each other; the camera comes closer. Typing never moves Tom; the clock stops in talk; no talk scene runs 60 s without a choice or control. Talk can run while walking or driving where a scene is staged that way.

Talk stays under 30% of play.

## 29. Calling on people, friendships and the club

Calling on people. Tom can call on a friend or the love interest at a place and hour their routine allows: tea at Ada's, the caff with Alison, a walk with Philip Danby. It is a short scene of talk under 2 real minutes, with no score and no pass mark. What is said draws on what each knows, so a visit after a bad night carries it ("You look like you've been in the wars"). Calling again too soon is remembered: a cooler door, "you again".

Someone worth being honest for. One of Act I's threads: a friendship that can hold, with Alison Sedman or Philip Danby, built from these visits and the honesty in them. Whether it holds at the close is part of every ending.

Father Walsh is met by the chapel and in the street, with his own lines and memory; the chapel is a place in his life, never a system.

The boxing club. An amateur boxing club in the old hall behind the chandler's on the quay: sparring with gloves (the fight system with the fall rule off and no blood), men who train there who can be recruited, the trainer, and a place to be seen. No stakes of any kind, no betting, no drink. It is chosen in free time because the fighting feels good.

## 30. Act I, The Inheritance

Week one, about two hours, the most scripted, seven days from Monday to Sunday. Five threads run through it: the outfit's claim (the asks on nights one, three, five and seven, each an envelope or a carry Ron brings); the warehouse fire (its street version is a street fact, Iveson bought out of it, Alison asks about it, Ellis is on it; the truth is never the street's); Mickey's real book (behind the locked door, shown when Sheila trusts him); the street deciding who he is (every deed coming back to his face); and someone worth being honest for. Rent falls on Friday. On Sunday Sheila comes in on her day off: "So which is it going to be?" Wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say. The street learns the answer.

Mickey's past. Mickey died of his heart; nobody thinks otherwise. Winding the business down ends Mickey's arrangement. The fire's street version stays a street fact however it is asked about.

Keeping or ending the arrangement. The men behind the landing are the last of an old firm. If Tom kept the run (went to the landing and took the firm on), they hand him their end of the Hook in Act II and fade, and he becomes what Mickey was: night money, cover and asks that grow. If he ended it, the ground is open and the three rivals come sooner: freedom without cover. Both reach Act II.

A week holds: fares, debts, the asks, a fight, a break-in or a chase, Ada's tea, Alison's fare, Ellis on the street if it is loud, and Sunday's question, with every day bringing something back.

## 31. Act II, The Rise and the Squeeze

About seven game weeks, 11 to 14 hours. It opens when the business has grown: Sheila has her answer and the firm is his (rung 2). Beats fire on states, never on days, in any order the states allow, and each changes the game the player plays:
- The old firm's end. On the state of the arrangement kept, the landing men hand over their end of the Hook and fade: the asks become Tom's own runs and the landing is his ground. On the state of it ended, the rivals' ladders start a rung sooner.
- Each rival's first notice. On the first thing one of its people sees or hears of Tom, a rival climbs to noticed, and from then on its pressure is part of every week: the dock gate tax on his drivers, the licence letter, Cammack's trouble outside the office.
- The real book. On Sheila's trust, the locked office opens and Mickey's real book shows what Mickey ran, and with it the names of debts and arrangements Tom can take up.
- The cold-store contract. On rung 3, the packing shift becomes the firm's daily legitimate loop, and with it the dock gate opens.
- The first gun. On the first gun reaching the Hook through a person, the dial moves, and the town's fear changes with it.
- One case. On Ellis reaching investigating, she joins the street's crimes to the fire: one case. Her questions start naming the fire beside his nights, and the office search with its notice becomes possible.
- The collision, guaranteed once. On an evening when a day-life appointment (Ada's tea, a call on Alison or Danby) and a night job fall together, his night life comes to the door of his day life: a man at Ada's door for him, a rival's men outside the caff while he sits with Alison. It plays in the world, in front of the people he is honest with.
- The sit-down. On a rival's ladder reaching its top rung, the sit-down with that head: accept, defy, or counter with what he knows. Act II ends there.

The firm climbs rungs 2 to 4 while each rival climbs its ladder. Sending (the eight verbs) opens here. No dead weeks: every game week of Act II brings at least one rival rung, one firm change and one return of a deed.

## 32. Act III, The Ledger Comes Due

One week, 2 to 3 hours. A courteous letter on the counter: a VAT inspection by Customs and Excise. The inspector comes each morning and asks for one item (a week of the fare book, the cars' records, the wages, an account's invoices); Tom produces it or asks for it in writing. Each morning is one deciding step, graded by what the books hold: every week Sheila put the books off is read. The inspector's manner shows it first, warmer or colder each morning. Sheila, if she trusts him, says where the lie holds. Ellis offers a trade: give her a rival, and the street learns who talks. Someone in the crew may ask to take the firm over: the successor's offer, which is withdrawn if the books fall. On the last day Tom can reach only a few people. Then the books open.

## 33. The endings, their signs and Tom's reading

The endings are states, never picked from a list, and no single beat fails the run. Five built states are kept underneath; three endings are shown, each with a better and a plainer way to turn out:
- The Kingdom. The business survives and the books hold. At its best, one friend still counts him and the street does not hold his rackets as fact (the built "Both", rare). At its plainest, people are civil and no more.
- The Straight Life. No business at the close, and one friendship holds. At its best, he handed the firm to someone who can keep it and walked away clean (the built "Quiet", never while hunted for a killing).
- Burn Both. Neither. What doing nothing produces, and where a hunted man can end.

No redemption path. Every ending is reachable by a route, including from a hunted state.

Every cost that decides the ending shows its sign before it decides: the inspector's manner when the books stop standing; Sheila's words when the true books cross the line; a friend's unasked act; a telling overheard as fact ("everyone knows he robbed the grocer"); Ellis asking, then telling; the police's four stages; the successor's offer, and its withdrawal; the keeper's shutter coming down. No ending lands while Tom's own reading says things are quiet, and every sign he was shown agrees with the ending he gets.

Tom's reading. On Full stop, and at each act's end, the Ledger gives Tom's reading: how things stand as he sees them, built only from the signs he was given, never from the true state. "Sheila won't look at me when the inspector's in. Alison came round without being asked. Ellis has stopped asking and started telling. The grocer pulls his shutter when I pass." A player who reads it can say how the game will end, and why.

After the ending, a short close in the town (who is where, who calls him what), then the credits, then play returns in the town at the ending's state within 10 s, with no new main beats; the Ledger's last page says so. The player can quit to the title instead. No new game plus.

## 34. The hour budget and the feel targets

The game is measured as it is played. Over 15 to 20 hours the time falls roughly so:

| Activity | Share |
|---|---|
| On foot: walking, looking, carrying, following, running | about 25% |
| Driving: fares, tails, getaways | about a fifth, never over 25% |
| Talk: what comes back, deals, witnesses, sit-downs, visits | under 30% (aim about 19%) |
| Jobs with the hands: break-ins, collections, searches, the count | about 17% |
| Fights and chases | about 8% |
| The Ledger, the map, getting ready | about 6% |
| Watching and waiting | under 10% (aim about 3%) |

About ten distinct activities every thirty minutes. Never five minutes with nothing new anywhere, and something new every three minutes in the first hour.

Always something to do. By day, with no job live, a fare, a debt or a person with a word for him is within 30 real seconds of wherever Tom stands. Street happenings fill the gaps.

Waiting and time skips. Nothing forces Tom out and sleeping is a skip, never a need. Waiting is a key that offers "wait until" each known appointment (the landing at ten, Ada's pot at nine, the shift at half past five), and a wait or a sleep always stops for what the town has for him: a man at the door, a message, a constable. No player is ever forced to wait.

Feel targets, every one measured in play:
- Control within 1.5 minutes of New game (here 20 s); a verb with the hands within 3; a choice and a deed within 20.
- Never stuck for 5 s anywhere; a failure returns control within 2 real minutes; beaten, within 60 s; an arrest under 2 minutes; every chase ends within 90 s.
- Inputs answer within 100 ms; a press 100 ms early counts.
- Melee: a blow starts within 0.1 s; jab 0.25 to 0.40 s; heavy 0.63 to 0.85 s; tells 0.45 to 0.80 s; at most two swinging, 0.4 s apart; slide to about 1 m; slip about 0.3 s; grab about 2 s; mashing empties in 6 to 10 jabs, back in 4 to 8 s; a fist from behind knocks out for 10 to 30 s.
- Driving: lock in 0.2 to 0.35 s; centre in 0.4 to 0.9 s; a lane change at 60 mph under 15 degrees, settled within 1 s; keys and pad within 10%; in or out under 2 s; a touch under 10 mph forgiven; 35 mph in one hit stops the car.
- Eyes: shape 35 m, mark 18 m, face 8 m, recognition 25 m if known; light 1, 0.7, 0.25, 0.12; glance 0.35 s, knowing 1.2 s, warning at least 0.8 s; a witness's walk at least 30 s in sight.
- Sound: a 3 a.m. footstep about 3.6 m; the car about 44 m by day; the horn about 60 m; a shot about 177 m.
- Camera: street at the Between height, 86 degrees, 3.1 m back, feet in frame; car about 5.8 m back; never inside a wall.
- Screen: the Ledger in 120 ms; nothing under 24 units; a reply within 2 s or the written line.
- Tension: at least twelve tense moments per thirty minutes from at least four sources; one retellable moment in the first thirty minutes and two an hour after; the town visibly knows Tom by minute 30.
- Performance: 60 frames a second at the target, never under 30, in rain and at night; walking and every timing independent of the frame rate.

The vision is met when a panel of eight kinds of player, after a whole evening and a whole week, mostly recommend it, and at least five of eight name a retellable moment as the most exciting thing that happened.

## 35. The screen, settings and accessibility

### Photo mode

From the pause menu only, "Photograph" freezes the town and frees the camera within 10 m of Tom and never through a wall, body or car (the camera rule). All interface is hidden. The player can tilt, roll, change the lens between 24 and 85 mm, focus and exposure, and save a picture at the window's size. No posing, no filters that break the grey range, no stickers or text. The content rule holds: nothing the photo mode can show is anything the game could not show. Leaving it returns the frozen moment exactly.

To pass: (1) Reachable only from pause; (2) camera never more than 10 m from Tom and never inside geometry; (3) no interface element in a saved picture; (4) the town state after leaving equals the state before (save hash).

### Crash and bug reporting

When the game crashes, it writes a local report (build, time, last 200 log lines, hardware summary) and, on next launch, asks once: "LEDGER closed unexpectedly. Send the report? It holds no save, nothing you typed and no name." Yes, No, Always, Never. Default is No until the player chooses. A save is attached only when the player ticks it, per report. Typed talk text is never sent in a crash report. A "Report a problem" item in the pause menu writes the same report plus the player's own words.

To pass: (1) Nothing leaves the PC without a yes; (2) the report contains no typed talk and no save unless ticked; (3) the choice is remembered and changeable in Settings; (4) the game never crashes while writing its own report (a forced crash test).

### Text and interface size, contrast and font

All game text (prompts, subtitles, the belief line, the Ledger, menus) is at least the description's 24 units (the prototype's unit, 24 px at 1080p) and scales from 100% to 200% in Settings without overlap or leaving the screen at any window size. Body text has a contrast of at least 4.5 to 1 against what is behind it (subtitles have an optional background box). One plain, readable face; no all-capitals body text.

To pass: XAG 101 (text display) and 102 (contrast): text at 200% never clipped; contrast at least 4.5 to 1 measured on 50 frames by day, night and rain.

### Motion and comfort settings

Each motion effect has its own switch: camera shake (the hit shake and the car's), head bob, motion blur, the brief camera close-in on a landed blow, and depth of field. The field of view stays at the ruled 86 degrees (ruling 28 Oct); a field-of-view setting, which the guidelines recommend, would change that ruling and waits on the owner's ruling, not built meanwhile. A "reduce flashing" switch dims muzzle flashes and lightning, beside the photosensitivity line the description already shows.

To pass: XAG 117 and GAG: every listed effect can be turned off alone; flashing reduced below three flashes a second when switched.

### Audio options

Sliders for master, voices, effects, the street (ambience) and music, each 0 to 100; a mono switch; a "voices clear" switch that lifts voices over the street by 6 dB; and a "loud noise softening" switch that clips gunshots and explosions to the level of a door slam (GTA VI's tinnitus reduction in LEDGER's form). The sound-shown assist already in the description stays.

To pass: XAG 105: five sliders, mono, voices clear and loud noise softening present and each changes only its own thing.

### Input options beyond rebinding

Every action rebindable on keys and on the pad, with "reset to defaults" per device; mouse and stick sensitivity separately for X and Y; invert per axis per device; every hold has a toggle (the description has this); no action needs repeated pressing (mashing in fights is a choice the breath rule punishes, never a requirement); an aim-assist strength in three steps once guns exist (off, light, strong; the dot stays optional and off by default), and a "hold becomes tap" switch for every hold.

To pass: XAG 107 (input): all items present; the game completes the first hour with every hold set to tap and on pad alone.

### Menu usability

Menu targets are large and never move; Back always goes one level up and never quits; the focused item is always visible; Settings are reachable before play and from pause; every setting is remembered between sessions and survives an update; nothing destructive sits one press from Resume (already in the description).

To pass: XAG 112 to 115 checks pass on every menu screen.

### Save thumbnails, rotating autosaves, never losing the only save

At least eight manual slots, each with a picture, the game day and hour, the place and Tom's rung in words. Autosaves rotate through three; a save is written to a new file and swapped in only once complete, so a crash during a save never loses the last good one. Quitting saves. A crash loses at most the last deed.

To pass: (1) Eight manual slots with picture, day, hour, place; (2) three rotating autosaves; (3) power cut during save x 100: a good save always loads.

### Accessibility listed in game and on the store

An "Accessibility" page in Settings lists every assist and option with one plain line on what each does; the same list is on the store page.

To pass: GAG basic, XAG 121: every option present on both; none missing (a script compares the settings file's keys with the page).

### Screen narration of menus

A "read menus aloud" switch speaks the focused menu item, its value and the Ledger's headings and lines through the system's speech voice; prompts in play can also be read aloud. Off by default; offered on the first menu.

To pass: XAG 106: every menu item and every Ledger line is spoken when focused with the switch on.

### Pause and focus safety

When the window loses focus, the game pauses (Esc menu shown) unless the player turns this off; alt-tab and a change of window size never crash, never lose input and never change the town's state.

To pass: 100 alt-tabs and 50 window resizes in a soak: no crash, no stuck key, no town state change while paused (save hash).

### Never by colour alone

No information is carried by colour alone: the light cue is darkness at the frame's corners, the belief line has words, the seeing outline is a shape. Key objects (doors usable, the cab of the firm, a constable) are told apart by shape and light, never only hue. three colour-blind filters (red-green two ways, blue-yellow) are offered, though the design does not rely on them.

To pass: Every cue passes a check under simulated protanopia, deuteranopia and tritanopia: the meaning is still readable.

## 37. The game's shape over many hours

### Travel time across the playable map

The Hook is crossed end to end (ferry landing to the north road) on foot at a walk in a set number of real minutes and by car at ordinary speed in a set number; every go-to step is reachable unaided in 3 minutes and nothing to do is more than 30 real seconds away by day (section 40). the bar is density over size, so the crossing must be short and full rather than long.

To pass: Provisional bands until measured: on foot 3 to 5 real minutes, by car 60 to 100 s; to be replaced by the reference games' town crossings (KCD2's Kuttenberg, RDR2's Saint Denis, a GTA V district, GTA VI's Vice City streets as a fourth).

### Density

Something to do or notice every so many seconds of street walking: a person with a word, a fare, a debt, a happening, a lit window with a life, a thing to touch. Also: distinct people in one busy view, and no street line repeated within an hour of play.

To pass: Things per street minute within the band the teardown's town pieces show (to come); at least 25 distinct people in the busiest view of the Hook at its busiest hour (a judgement figure, replaced by the footage pass's count of GTA VI's and KCD2's busiest views); no shared street line heard twice in one real hour (raised from the description's "a shared line once a week per street", which allowed repeats across streets).

### Jobs done out of order and sequence breaks

Every job is a set of step states (the description's job template), never a script with an order. A step met early (the key found before the job is offered), late (after the hour), skipped by another route (in through the yard instead of the door) or done by someone else (a crewman sent) counts as met. A job never waits on a step already made impossible: it moves to failed with a state the town can talk about, and control is never stuck.

To pass: For every job, the AI tester runs all orders of its steps it can reach plus one skip by each other route; no run ends stuck or waiting for something that cannot happen.

### A second playthrough

A replay that answers differently meets named differences: Sunday's three answers; the arrangement kept or ended (rivals a rung sooner); which rival notices first; the five debtors each paid another way; the fronts and men chosen; the endings, three with a better and a plainer turn each; and whatever branches the measured story keeps.

To pass: A replay with a different Sunday answer and a different first rival meets at least eight named differences a player can point to in its first five hours. The figure is judgement until checked against KCD2's and RDR2's published branch counts.

### Humour and a mix of tones

Canon's tone: grounded noir with dry British wit and seaside-postcard smut, sparingly, so the noir holds; never American satire. Every evening holds at least one light moment carried by a person (Darren, Pauline on the radio, a fare), and every act holds at least one scene written to be funny (the current bible's inspector ride-along is the model).

To pass: Humour is praised in 5 of 8 games' story reviews (6% of story sub-themes); a clash of tone is punished. Pass: each act has at least one comic scene and each evening at least one light moment; no comic scene sits inside a scene of death or grief.

### Economy over weeks

Money buys position, never numbers (the description, section 24). The curve: Act I, £23.50 and rent at £18 a week, a week of fares pays it, the debts at £156; Act II, what money buys rises with the rung (a third car, a driver's wage, men's cuts, a front's share, a bribe to a bought man, the decent jacket, the good overcoat, the doctor); Act III, money cannot buy the books clean. Sinks never run dry before Act III: at every act's end there is a named thing the player wants and cannot yet afford.

To pass: The players study punishes money with nothing to buy (GTA V and RDR2 late game). Pass: at each act's end, in both a greedy and a lazy bot playthrough, at least one named thing on the rung ladder he does not yet hold is still out of reach of his money; the curve's shape is set from the reference games' money over a playthrough.

### The catalogue of jobs and stories per act

Every job has a named giver, a place by landmark, an hour, three keys to every lock (the description, section 14: no job needs talk, a fight or a car to finish), and a return (somebody tells it back). Kinds: carries, collections, break-ins, lifts, tails, minding, fares with a wrinkle, sit-downs, and from Act II the eight sent verbs. Act II has no dead weeks (a rival rung, a firm change and a return every week).

To pass: Jobs per real hour and kinds per act inside the band the teardown and the reference games' mission counts show; no set piece twice in thirty minutes (section 14).

### Rival threat rung and counters

The threat rung is a named harm said to his face or shown on his property, never yet done to a person: the Widow's men put the office window in, or tell a driver he will not drive again; Agar's solicitor names a date for the licence hearing; Cammack's boys slash the tyres of car two on the rank. "Counter with what he knows" is spending a hook (see Hooks and knowledge as leverage) on the rival's man who made the move, or on the rival. The Fixer's truce holds one rival at its rung for two game weeks, once a game.

To pass: Each rival's threat rung shows one harm in its own style; every harm repairable; a counter needs a hook Tom holds; a truce holds exactly two game weeks.

### Hooks and knowledge as leverage

A hook is a fact Tom holds (a Ledger "People" line he is sure of) about a person, which that person would not want told to a named other (his wife, his guv'nor, the police, a rival). Tom gets one by being told (Darren sells what he hears), by seeing it himself, or from Mickey's real book. Spending it is one line of talk: it buys a recruit (quick and brittle: that man's loyalty ceiling is lower and he breaks sooner), an answer to a pressure, or a counter at a sit-down. A hook is spent once: the person now knows Tom knows, and remembers it. A "guesses" line is no hook; using it as one is a bluff, which works only if the person believes it.

To pass: Hooks exist only from a perceived source; spent once; a hook recruit breaks sooner than a need recruit under the same pressure.

### Sending verbs in play

A send is given in talk with the man (or by the board's line for him): two inputs, the verb, then a target from the Ledger (a person or place Tom knows). The man does it on the next night his routine allows. Its outcome is decided by the same rules as Tom's own deeds: the man's nerve against the target's, the watchers there, light and noise. The player learns it the morning after from the man's own line and, later, from the town's version. Each send leaves a chain the town can trace, and the man knows.

To pass: Every send resolves within two game nights; its result runs the same perception and memory as a deed by Tom; nothing is shown at length.

### Tom's line against women, the over-sixties and the priest

If someone his line covers attacks him, Tom guards, slips and backs off; the strike input does nothing and he says so. In a fight that includes them, he can strike, shove or grab only the others; he does not grab a covered person even to take a weapon, and steps away instead. Tom will not give a send that hurts a person his line covers: the order is refused in his own words. A rival's men are not bound by his line.

To pass: Zero strikes, shoves or grabs by Tom on anyone covered, and zero sends that hurt one, in any fight, hold-up or send.

### Firearms as a verb

Late and rare (the dial). Held semicolon draws the gun from under the coat; the camera moves to the aim row; the mouse or stick aims with no crosshair (an optional dot, off by default; the aim-assist steps in the input settings). A shot's hit depends on aim, distance and whether Tom moved. Before War a hit wounds (arm, leg or body) and never kills; at War a body hit can kill, a named and permanent event. an aimed shot to the arm disarms, and a shot to the leg stops a man running. A revolver holds six and reloads in a shown action after the sixth; no count on screen. Men with guns shoot from where they stand, never use cover, and run when outmatched. A shot Tom goes down, comes round in hospital (days, never money) and never dies. Nobody shoots from a moving car.

To pass: The rules above pass or fail; draw, aim and hit timings within the band of the firearms footage measuring.

### Hidden damage and injury states

Each man has a hidden capacity by fighter type (big man most, knife man least); a jab takes little, a heavy blow about three jabs' worth, a weapon more (bat more than cosh), a blow from behind with a cosh or fist knocks out for 10 to 30 s. A man is down when his capacity is spent; down is out, never dead from fists. Tom has three states, shown only in his body: fresh, hurt (guard slower to rise, breath shorter, a stagger), beaten (down). A knife wound on Tom bleeds (blood on the coat, a give-away) until stitched by the retired doctor or the hospital; a bat leaves a limp for a day. Hurt does not stack past beaten; he can fight on hurt.

To pass: Blows to put a man down per type within the band of the damage footage measuring (KCD2, Sleeping Dogs, RDR2); "who was winning?" answered right by 4 of 5 testers from the body alone.

### Escalation dial: what takes Fired to War

Fired moves to War when the town learns of either: a second shooting by a different side from the first (two firms have now fired), or a named man of one firm wounded by a shot from another. Tom's men seen carrying (more than two of them, each seen by someone) also moves it, because the town then knows who carries. Nothing moves it that nobody saw or heard. It falls one step after about two quiet game weeks, never back to 0.

To pass: War only on these learned events; never on an unseen one; the fall rule as the description.

### Ellis's case model

The case is a list of charges and talk. Charges accumulate one per arrest that ends in a charge; an office-search find adds one (possession or handling, by what is found). Her interest falls one stage after two quiet game weeks with no new reported deed and no new charge, never below asking round once she has reached investigating. A manhunt ends with an arrest, or after three game days with no sighting it falls to investigating. Tom's "day world" is the named people of his day life: Ada, Alison Sedman, Philip Danby, Father Walsh, June Suddaby, Darren, and any regular he has called on or carried as a fare twice.

To pass: Stages move only on these rules, each with its sign; the charges list read at the ending equals the arrests and finds.

### Typed-talk interpretation

Each typed line is read into one act: a question, agree, refuse, an offer (with the amount read: "a tenner" is £10, "twenty quid" £20; an offer over what Tom carries fails in character), a threat, a promise (with what and when), a claim (a statement; a lie when it contradicts what Tom knows), an insult, small talk, or leave. Offers, threats, promises and lies are acts with the seven slots and are remembered by the hearer like any deed; a broken promise comes back.

To pass: On a hand-labelled set of typed lines, the reading agrees with the label on at least the agreed share; amounts read exactly; every offer, threat, promise and lie enters the hearer's memory.

### Money pots in practice

Three places money is: the pocket (cash on Tom), the tin (night money at the office, which cannot be explained) and the book (clean money, which can be spent anywhere, as the description says). Rent and drivers' wages come out of the book weekly; a car, a front's share, shops and the doctor can be paid from the book or from cash. Night money paid where it can be seen to be explained (a car bought, a front's share) is a lie someone may one day have to hold up, as any fare written for an hour he was elsewhere. Bribes and men's night cuts come from cash, as the description has the cut paid from the tin. The count turns tin money into book money within the wash limit; nothing turns the book back into tin. The tin can be stolen in an office break-in (a rival's threat, or a thief) and is then gone, said in words. A constable's search finds what is under the coat and in the hand, cash included, and what he finds he keeps.

To pass: Every payment draws from a place the rules allow; the flows ledger balances; a search keeps exactly what was found.

### Pause and clock

The clock stops in talk (section 34) and in the pause menu, and nowhere else. Opening the Ledger, the paper map or a letter stops Tom but not the town, as the description already says of the map (section 24). Getting ready takes a game minute, never a screen. To pass: Clock frozen only in talk and pause (and with the assist on, in the Ledger); time passes at the clock rate otherwise.

Street happenings are authored (ruling 13 Nov), each with a spec and a check, never rolled at random. ## 39. How some systems are judged

### Showing what people know

Every person who knows something of Tom shows it on that ladder before any line; nobody who knows nothing shows it; the step shown matches what they hold (a told version never above a seen one).

### Armed response and the dial's police

No armed officer before Fired; at Fired every reported gun call brings armed officers later than an ordinary call; at War at least one armed car is on the Hook's streets and searches happen on the street; none of it shown on any map.

### Clean money and the fare book

Every fare driven, by Tom or the firm's drivers, enters the book; rent and wages leave it on their days; the book's balance always equals its flows.

### Night money and the tin

Every night job's take goes to the tin or the pocket, never the book except through the count; the tin can be lost only by being stolen or searched, said in words.

### The wash

The count never washes over the limit without Sheila's warning first; each week over is recorded for Act III; a front raises the limit by its set amount. The 80% and the front's amount are balance figures checked against GD-07's money curve.

### Partial payment and the book

A payment never exceeds the debtor's purse; the remainder stays and is said in the Ledger's Money page; a squeeze or a let-off produces a told version within a game day.

### Keeping or ending the arrangement

Kept gives the hand-over beat; ended starts every rival one rung up; both reach rung 2 (RT-04).

### The Fixer

Every read he sells is true to the town's memory at that moment; a meeting he brokers happens; the truce holds exactly two game weeks and is offered once. His prices follow GD-07's curve.

### Recruiting

A need recruit's loyalty ceiling is higher than a hook recruit's; each named recruit brings the use the description gives (locks, fists, nights on the warehouse row, the ferry, paint); boxing club men can be recruited there.

### Crew fates

Each fate reachable in play; each produces a told version and a Ledger line; a poach always takes the least loyal man.

### Rung table

The rung table of the description, section 32, with the conditions of RT-04.

To pass: As RT-04: each rung on exactly its holdings; what opens at each rung opens then and not before.

### Access and soft keys

Each soft key opens exactly its doors: the Harbour Board's counter refuses him in the arrival clothes and lets him in with the decent jacket; any door that opens to him clean refuses him bloodied or filthy.

### Ranks and accounts

A rank is won by either road and never otherwise; accounts open in that order, each at its rung (the cold store at rung 3); "enough days" is five days in a fortnight (a balance figure checked with GD-07).

### Sit-downs

Each answer leads to a different following state; counter needs a hook Tom holds (see Hooks and knowledge as leverage); what was said is what the town then believes (a told version within a game day).

### Collision and physics

Nothing passes through walls, bodies or cars; nobody and nothing is ever stuck for more than 5 s; no car launches into the air or spins on a touch; a struck person falls as the hit's direction says; things to hand break by the description's blow counts.

To pass: In a 2-hour soak of walking, driving and fights: zero pass-throughs, zero stuck over 5 s, zero launches.

### The boot, seen

The cab's boot holds what is not on him: the envelope, a spare coat, a box. Opening it is an act with the seven slots; a constable who stops the cab with cause may look in it; a spare coat there is preparation.

To pass: Opening the boot is perceived like any act; a stop with cause searches it; what is in it survives save and load.

To pass: A changed slot no longer matches for witnesses who had not told; gossip already carried is never undone; the Ledger shows Tom's guess per slot in words.

To pass: In a car chase with a passenger, at least one landmark line per 20 s of chase; every line names a real landmark on the way.

### Places stay open

Every building used in a job stays enterable afterwards, as a place in someone's life, unless the story closes it and says so.

To pass: Every interior used once can be entered again after its job, or its closing is said in the world.

### Bugs at release

The release build has no blocking fault: never stuck, no lost save, no crash in a whole evening.

To pass: Three nights running, the AI tester's whole-evening and whole-week walks in the packaged build find zero blocking faults and zero crashes.

### How a deed is told: restraint and cruelty

The telling carries how a deed was done, not only that it was: a man put down with three blows who is left alone is told differently from one who was kicked on the ground. The worst telling, once carried, is never undone.

To pass: Two seeded fights differing only in restraint produce different told versions and different greetings within a game day.
