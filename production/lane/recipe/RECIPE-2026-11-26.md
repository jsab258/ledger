# RECIPE: building a whole AAA-level game with AI

The playbook for building a whole AAA-level game of any genre with AI only, solo, from a prompt plus a few decisions by its owner (the owner's aim, 12 Oct 2026). It is rewritten from NOTEBOOK.md at each phase's end; this rewrite (26 Nov 2026) closes the design phase and puts the method in the order a game is made. LEDGER is the first worked example, not the subject: setting, period, places and people stay in each game's own data.

Chapter 1 is the method: lessons tagged ANY, each checked on paper against two far games (a real-time strategy game with no hero, many units, an economy and short matches; a one-hero action game of movement feel, combat and set pieces). Chapter 2 holds what is true only for open-world social simulations. "NB date" names a notebook entry, which holds its evidence; "scan: name" a design-phase job summary folded into the notebook on 26 Nov; "RECIPE 23 Nov" the previous rewrite.

## 0. How to use it

**The bar: what AAA means here.** Quality, not budget or size: every part a player feels is judged against the best shipped AAA game for that part (the bar is set per aspect, not by one famous game), against reference frames and photographs, and against what players say in reviews; only work that stands beside those, at the current year's grade, passes. Scale may start smaller (one dense area first) and grows as the tools make authored breadth cheap; the claim that sells it (what makes it distinctive) must beat the best AAA reference outright, the rest at least equal it where the player meets it. The measurable goal is a shipped game that players rate highly (NB 21 Nov; 1.2).

**Status.** Not yet run end to end on another game: the planned proof is a small roguelike built from it after the first game's stage 1, on the owner's yes (NB 26 Nov). Most costs and numbers come from one game on one machine.

**Reading order.** This chapter, then the phase's chapter and its genre pack section, before any first try; chapters give Works, Failed, Cost and Links.

**Words used.**
- Session: a long-running model worker with its own work list. Helper: a bounded task a session briefs and checks, never a director (1.9 step 9).
- Advisor: the owner's own chat session; it plans with the owner, sends out jobs and reports their results (NB 24 to 26 Nov).
- Keeper: the scheduled session that folds each day's design work into the blueprint and logs conflicts and sweep hits, deciding none (scan: keeper log).
- Lane: one parallel line of work in its own worktree (1.6 step 6). Stage: one big build step in playing order (1.6 steps 2 and 3).
- Weekly points: percentage points of the weekly model allowance. The pace line is the share allowed by each hour of the week (for example 90% spread over 162 hours); a session's pace stop halts it above the line (1.9 step 5; scan: checkers).

**The owner's first decisions.** Everything else the sessions decide within canon and the owner's rulings, record in the decisions log and leave open to the owner's overturn.
1. **Genre.** Picks the genre pack, or orders one written (below).
2. **Scope and length.** Hours of play, world size, the count of people, units or levels; it fixes the census (1.4) and the scale ladder (1.8.7). Scale is priced per actor and per action, not per map (NB 25 Nov).
3. **Platform.** Machine, graphics card, input devices; it fixes the budgets. Check the player's hardware vendor against the tooling any technical route needs (NB 1 Nov).
4. **Art target.** The reference frames the look is judged against and the closest distance the player meets each thing; it sets how deep each production discipline goes (1.8).
5. **Content rules.** What is never shown or said; they become canon (1.3) and blocked words in checks and every helper's brief. A rule covers denials too ("has opinions on X" still speaks of X: NB 8 Nov).
6. **Budget and allowance.** Money (every paid tool or asset is the owner's call), the weekly allowance and its pace line, the machine's drives (1.9).
7. **Reference games.** At least two shipped games of the kind, best if they answer the central design question in opposite ways (for an open world: one stages its world, one simulates it), plus the strongest game for each aspect, which leads for it; every outside comparison names all of them. Licence and law settle first whether their files may be read (NB 28 Oct; 24 Nov).
8. **What makes it distinctive.** Its claims, checked against the newest market leader (1.3), become pillars and acceptance tests.

After these, only taste and identity, violence, scope, canon and money go to the owner, one multiple-choice question at a time with a recommendation, shown in the product where possible (1.9).

**The first day.** Set up before the first study:
1. Canon: the game's facts and content rule, above every other file (1.3).
2. Rulings: the owner's standing decisions, one line each, capped, read at every start (1.9 step 2).
3. Decisions log: one entry per material choice (date, decision, reason), capped, read by search, never whole (NB before 13 Oct; 8 Nov).
4. Notebook: one entry per lesson (1.9 step 1).
5. Order ledger: one line per item (stable id, status from a short fixed list, text, evidence or reason), seeded from every place open work hides, checked on push (NB 25 Oct).
6. Standing page: one data file of the owner's open decisions (open or done, when and how each closed), rebuilt at one link; taps stored and read before anything is asked again (NB 2 Nov).
7. Data: every tunable number in a table under a schema, one home each; a naming rule with a check from the first week (NB 28 Oct).
8. Folders: every folder mapped (size, readers, where it comes back from); work only in named folders; large binaries outside version control (1.8.6 step 8; 1.9 step 10).
9. Sessions: which run, each with its own list; one recurring gate; a pace stop in each (1.9 steps 5 and 7).
10. Technical route, chosen by research (1.1) and logged: engine, models and tools, the hardware vendor checked against each (NB 1 Nov) and a faster open-source alternative read first (1.1 step 11); asset families costed licence first, then generated, then made (1.9 step 6); budgets before content (1.8.7 step 1).

**The phases.** A builder drafts each phase's pieces with hour ranges, one fresh reviewer checks, open scope questions go to the owner (NB 8 Nov); each exit gets a fresh clause-by-clause review (1.7 step 14).
1. Research (1.1, 1.2): from the owner's decisions and written questions until each is answered, marked "none found" or closed by the cut-off (1.1 step 10), and the bars are set.
2. Design (1.3, 1.4), as first run: each pass's census (1.4 step 1); the question pass; new angles; the angle hunt; rules fitted under laws. The sweep, reality check, simulators and outside tests run on the design as it stands and rejudge what a revision touched (scan: questions); experience risks and rule costs join every pass. The counts given (14 lenses of about 50 questions, 100 angles) are the first game's: stop rules, not counts, end a pass, and the relevance and cut-off rules decide what a smaller game skips (NB 25 Nov).
3. Blueprint (1.5): ends at the start gate.
4. Stages (1.6 steps 2 and 3): none has yet been built this way, so stage sizes are unmeasured.
5. Loops (1.6 step 9); the opening is polished again last.

**A genre pack** holds what chapter 2 holds: references as studied, naming the game that leads each aspect; a few laws with typical rules; genre method; measures with kind and sample count; production notes; Failed, Cost, Links. A new genre writes one from its first studies and design passes. The notebook holds nothing yet on many-unit pathing, opponent AI, netcode, balance and match pacing, or on traversal and combat feel, hit reactions, set pieces and a fast camera: research those first where the genre needs them (far check, 26 Nov).

**Rules in every chapter.**
- The whole-game order: research, then a measured design, then building in stages; nothing is built at scale from an unmeasured design (NB 23 Nov).
- Reference first: how do the reference games do it (studies, then files)? Copy the method; take numbers only once they fit; else the plan says "none found".
- Research the professional method end to end before a first try; on failure, question the method, not the settings (NB before 13 Oct).
- Relevance: before proposing any work, say whether the next build needs it; if not, it waits (NB 25 Nov).
- Tries count per fault, not per method: two failed tries set it aside; then one genuinely different researched method, up to four tries while each closes a third of the gap with no new fault; then a removing or simplifying fix under an hour, bigger ones listed. Check the stopped methods' failure lists before calling a cause new; a fault in the check is not a try (NB 30 Oct; 17 Nov).
- Name the visible fault in a measure that moves with it (the frame the eye objects to must read worst), and prove a count measures the fault before tuning to it (NB 30 Oct; 19 Nov: 124 of 296 "through" points were another layer's own cuffs).
- After any setting, measure that its thing moved, from the result line the run just wrote (NB 26 Oct; 5 Nov).
- A pass counts only in the packaged game launched as the player launches it (NB 7 to 11 Nov).
- The goal is itself a bar: a fix passing every local bar can miss its purpose (NB 13 Nov).
- One home for every number; everything derived is made from it by script. Times come from the clock, never memory (NB 13, 29 Oct; scan: data build).

## 1. The method

### 1.1 Research and references

**Works.**
1. Write the questions before any search, and search the project's own research, asset lists and canon first: an answered question gets no viewing hours. Mark each finding confirms, contradicts or adds, both sources named (NB 21 Nov).
2. Run studies as bounded helpers in parallel, one per question set or game, each with its questions, the legal rule, a stop rule and one thing it must not copy; raw counts stay outside the repository; one synthesis keeps every hedge. One fresh reviewer per study re-measures a sample against the raw sources and checks the advice against the rulings; faults go back to the author (NB 13, 25, 28 Oct).
3. Judge every claim a study makes about the project against the project's own files, and give the reviewer those files: in three of four studies the heaviest fixes were there (NB 25 Oct; 1 Nov).
4. Reading a shipped game's files, where licence and law allow: index first; unpack only readable data; export metadata, never assets or audio; cap each extract and check its size per batch, outside the repository, one extractor at a time; delete it, and any key a tool stored, once the review has re-measured. Seed a coverage map from the engine's script container, which names every system even in encrypted packs: per system, where its logic lives and which study covers it, so code-only logic shows as a blind spot (NB 25, 28 Oct).
5. Tag every number measured, read, inferred or name only, with date and source. Each finding becomes adopt, adapt or ignore, each target gets a check, each recommendation one order-ledger line copying only numbers the study states, re-read first by a fresh reviewer (NB 28 Oct: 145 lines, 15 corrected).
6. Sources: primary texts in full; quotes verified word for word in the raw page by script (a summarising fetch is not verification); an unreached source listed, never used; blind spots (engine code, sound banks, the played mix) named, never filled; a refused site never got round with a browser identity (NB 12, 18 Nov; 28 Oct; before 13 Oct).
7. Check a reference's reputation in its data before copying it (the "marker-free" game showed a marker on 8 of 10 starts); quote only from the opened line, summarise from notes, never memory (NB 28 Oct; 21 to 24 Nov).
8. Player evidence: reviews pulled several ways (most helpful, newest, each release's first five weeks), de-duplicated; a word-list coder tuned on one hand-read set, frozen, scored once on a fresh one, with precision and recall per theme before any share is quoted (NB 20 Nov). First hours from public video: transcript cue words (bored, how do I, skip) read in context, frames every 2.5 min classed control, scene or menu (NB 21 Nov).
9. Other AI-built games (as of 23 Nov 2026 none had shown a large story-first 3D game rated well) lend their feature census and scripted playthroughs (NB 23 Nov).
10. Close a missing source before the build only if it changes how the game is built; two readers reaching no primary text is the stop signal, and after two failed searches a different source class is named (NB 25 Nov; scan: street photos).
11. Before speed work on a tool, one reader answers written questions about a faster open-source alternative, and the own tool is checked against them (NB 16 Nov).
12. What the cloud cannot reach is read from the machine; figures resting on judgement meanwhile are marked as shortfalls (scan: combat).

**Failed.**
- An uncapped extract (26 GB) filled the shared disk; five studies lost data, one its raw exports, deleted before review.
- Eight clips viewed blind to earlier research spent hours on what photographs already answered.
- Word-list shares quoted without hand checks (precision 0 to 80 by mechanic).

**Cost.** A study 0.6 to 1.0 helper hours, its review 0.2 to 0.3; sixteen studies in about 6 h of wall clock. Helpers in parallel spend the allowance about twice as fast.

**Links.** NB 25 and 28 Oct (step zero; studies), 1 to 25 Nov.

### 1.2 Setting the bars and the measures

The bars are AAA bars: for each aspect, the best shipped AAA game for it sets the level, measured from it, not remembered (chapter 0, the bar).

**Works.**
1. Sources by kind. Facts of the setting (looks, sizes, materials, prices, timetables) come from dated real sources; what the game compresses or exaggerates for play (scale, speed, time) is a ruled decision with its ratio written; reference games tune feel and rules. Every number carries its kind: real, game, ruling or judgement (NB 24 Nov). Real sources follow the setting's ladder: a photographed period takes dated photographs, then books, element by element (NB before 13 Oct); a period before photography takes surviving objects and sites, excavation records, period texts and scholars' reconstructions; an invented setting takes canon (far check, 26 Nov review). Where steps 5 and 6 say photographs, read the first rung.
2. A design counts as measured when each part a player feels has a standard: a reference game's measured number, a dated real figure or published standard, or a pass-or-fail rule of the owner's or canon's. A standard the design chose is a decision, listed, never counted as measured; reports give all three counts (RECIPE 23 Nov).
3. Solid or lead: a number is solid on at least five samples from uncut, dated sources, read at a step finer than the thing measured; anything less is a lead. The strict rule applies only where the number decides a design choice; the rest is priced at the depth the gate needs (NB 21, 23 Nov).
4. Write the inclusion rule before reading any sample and apply it to all; save each measured edge's crop with the upload date as it is read; a second upload of one event is not a new sample (NB 24 Nov; scan: foot chases).
5. Exact targets before building: each part in millimetres with material, colour, wear and source tag, from own past tries and reviewers' faults, then primary sources and dated photographs, then a synthesis with confidences, then a fresh reviewer. Every check row gets a wrong copy that must fail it by name (NB 13 Oct; 16 Nov).
6. Photographs are measured as the check measures renders: the same statistic, the box tight on the object's own face and recorded; the source's error kept apart from the builder's tolerance; the camera height read before sizes (an assumed 1.6 m ran 55% high) (NB 13 Oct; before 13 Oct).
7. Statistics: a timing in two groups is judged by the share under the bar and the 75th and 90th percentiles over at least 40 cases an arm, arms alternated in one sitting beside the same load and set against two plain runs' spread; every job runs at least twice; a count is only what its filter caught; colour in linear light; a ceiling reads every frame's maximum, loading included; denominators come from the files (NB 1 Nov; 25, 27 Oct; 3, 8 Nov).
8. Measure the shipped path, not a tool's own: compare the harness's steps with the product's and read which model or path each result used against the class the bar names (a speed bar was read on the wrong model for weeks) (NB 1, 2 Nov; scan: live talk).
9. Reading footage of any reference game:
   - Read the publish date first; drop pre-release, edited, modded and scripted clips (NB 21 Nov).
   - Incidents live in long uncut sessions of the play the system appears in. Scan only the screen box where the state shows (a colour count every 2 s), drop menu frames, group hits into runs, look at every run, re-read edges at 1 and 0.5 s; skim long videos first by storyboard thumbnails or a motion scan set per upload (NB 24 to 25 Nov; scan: foot chases).
   - Reactions run from the cause's first clear frame to the subject's first clear body change, cropped to the subject at 0.3 to 1 s steps; a maker's cut is unreadable, not data (NB 21 Nov).
   - Blinking indicators: find the period, then step at half of it or finer (steps on one phase invented a pursuit); check the upload plays at real speed against an in-game clock (NB 21, 25 Nov).
   - Cadence at 0.1 s cells over five stretches within 10%, re-read blind; speeds never come from footage cadence, and footage never gives a rule the game scripts away. Counts in view are floors (NB 24 to 25 Nov).
   - Icons and timers by fixed-slot code templates; models label scenes but do not count (47 of 49 against 63 to 78%) (NB 23 Nov).
10. Installed-game drills beat footage for feel numbers: keys scripted from outside; the window grabbed in a tight loop with input stamped on the same high-resolution clock, only changed pictures counted (timings over 0.1 s); the opening filmed first with no keys, then fixed timed taps; the game's readouts as rulers, its checkpoint restarts for repeats (NB 23 to 24 Nov).
11. Shipped budgets come from a game's own settings files; check what a key means before using it (a "frame time" key was a hitch threshold) (NB 23, 25 Nov).

**Failed.**
- Photo boxes drawn wide by eye measured the surrounding wall; sheets kept only in the page could not be re-read for review.
- Speed from following-camera footage (cadence times step, autocorrelation); vehicle speed with no speedometer.
- A model reading 1,710 icon cells (US$3.83, unusable); a grab-then-save loop with a pause (5 frames a second).

**Cost.** A target 1 to 2 h, review 0.4 h, fixes 0.8 to 1.7 h; a state timed to five samples about 4 h by eye, under 1 h by scan; the feel block about 16 h by drills against 79 h by footage (estimate).

**Links.** NB 13 Oct, 1 Nov, 21 to 25 Nov; RECIPE 23 Nov; scan: foot chases, live talk.

### 1.3 Canon and pillars

**Works.**
1. Canon (the game's facts and content rule) outranks every other file; the owner's rulings come next. Read all standing rulings, not only the latest, and a thing's record before changing it; a conflict goes to the owner as one line with evidence and a recommendation (NB 13 Oct).
2. Whatever names the setting lives in data, never in method or tools; a placeholder name is marked as one in data and never reaches the owner's page (NB 13 Oct; the notebook rule).
3. Rules as code: every standing rule names the test, gate, scheduled check or weekly audit that catches a breach, keyed by its opening words so an edited rule is looked at again. An exception lives in the rule's own instructions, never in a note; a time-limited rule carries the ruling it serves, not a free end date (NB 11, 17, 21, 25 Nov).
4. After any reversing or global ruling (a protection removed, a clock changed), sweep every data file and part for the old value and list the hits for the keeper (54 files still held a removed protection). A content-rule fix reaches the build, checks and pictures in one pass (scan: what-if, data sweep; NB 13 Oct).
5. Content checks: a blocklist holds real names and only names; every printed word and placed texture is read at full size; a person's contact sheet is the first check, a text reader the second (NB 13, 24 Oct).
6. A specific case never goes to the owner when a general rule covers it; a proposed law often belongs under an existing law, in the technical chapter or in canon (NB 25 Nov).
7. Pillars come from what makes the game distinctive. Check each uniqueness claim against the newest market leader's coverage (it did 16 of 122 and a weaker form of 17 more); its strengths nobody asked about become candidates; the signature moments become acceptance tests (NB 24 to 25 Nov).
8. Say how the game behaves only from the design (the advisor once did not, and was wrong), and what it does not hold as unknown. Contradictions a pass finds are logged for the keeper; the pass decides none (NB 24 Nov; scan: questions).

**Failed.**
- A photograph tally overrode a standing ruling, and an object the owner had removed was put back silently.
- A rule's end date outlived its week and the old behaviour came back for a night.

**Cost.** Enforcers for all 84 rules about 1.3 h (51 by code or schedule, 33 by the weekly audit).

**Links.** NB 13 Oct, 11 to 25 Nov (rule enforcers; uniqueness); scan: what-if, data sweep, questions.

### 1.4 Design

**Works.**
1. **The census before any scope.** Every pass starts with a census of its subject from the design and the references, checked blind, before any plan; never propose a scope from memory (a combat pass left out stealth until the owner asked) (NB 25 Nov).
2. **The cut-off rule.** Before the build, only gaps that change how the game is built must close; content gaps go to the loops. When 545 of 691 shortfalls changed the build, the cut-off, not the count, decided the work (NB 25 Nov; scan: questions).
3. **The question pass.** Many player lenses (14) each ask about 50 concrete questions, every earlier one added, each answered against the design (well, partly, missing, contradictory, out) with a proposal written as a general rule, never a case fix; the lead merges coined rule names (252 into 24). A fresh blind set measures the miss rate, lenient and strict (10 and 15% against 5%); fixes stay unchecked until a second fresh set; a set the design was revised against is spent, so hold back a never-seen set of over 20 per measure and record the design version each answer was judged against. Rank questions by how likely a player meets them early times the harm (dead end 4, immersion 3, minor 1) (NB 25 Nov; scan: questions, combat, what-if).
4. **New angles, not more questions.** Situation lenses miss whole-game questions (play styles, a second run, pacing over weeks): a whole-game pass of 350 questions found 8 new rules. Barely-asked kinds (in the first game: players breaking the AI, institutions acting alone, two things at once, losing, feel and learning) were tested beside a well-asked control: only the new kinds found new rules (NB 25 Nov).
5. **The angle hunt.** Map every angle a game can be questioned from, from professional sources (design lenses, QA test design, MDA, motivation models, systemic design, narrative review, red-teaming, accessibility); test the uncovered in batches of six plus a covered control, with a blind writer, fresh judges and a fresh reviewer of every new-rule claim; stop when fresh angles need no new law (100 angles, 30 tested, none needed) (NB 25 Nov).
6. **Laws, rules, data.** A flat rule list grows without end and mixes levels. Fit every rule under a few laws for the genre (chapter 2 has eight); a new question is answered by finding its law; growth at the rule level is the design itself (NB 25 Nov).
7. **The sweep.** Every verb against every kind of actor, place and moment, asked as "what happens if", answered against the design and scored worse, same or better than the best reference; the bar is set per family of verbs (NB 24 Nov).
8. **The reality check.** Reader helpers by chapter, research helpers and a blind re-judge give every number a fault kind (ok, unsourced, wrong source, carried from another setting, self-judging) and a harm score 1 to 5; harm 4 and 5 go first. Audit the source label, not only the number (one file tagged 427 keys "real"); a rule widened so its own test passes is flagged self-judging. Reader counts are rough (the re-judge agreed on 25 of 40): say about 15% either way (NB 24 Nov; scan: reality check).
9. **Run the systems before building them.** A seeded simulator per system plays it with styles as players (four styles, 300 runs over 13 game weeks; 44,000 fights), every flow audited, breakages counted, rules such as no strategy paying twice the middle one; it has no geometry and rare rates rest on few cases, so say so. A trait or stat earns its place only if a rule reads it and the player can see its effect (scan: economy, combat, temper).
10. **Outside tests.** Show the game as a stranger meets it ("50 things you didn't know you could do", "I tried to break the AI", 15-second reels, likely top comments and reviews written in advance); the best moments become acceptance tests. A self-written list flatters (39 of 50 backed against 15 of 50 from a blind outside list): pair it with an outside one. The owner's chance questions found gaps no check did, as every check graded the design against itself: count the owner's catches; the method fails while they do not fall to zero (NB 24 Nov; scan: fifty).
11. **Capability is not experience.** Question passes test whether the world can respond, not whether it is good to play. Take experience risks from playability heuristics (Desurvire's, PLAY, games user research): frustration, confusion, invisible depth, aimlessness, tedium, flat pacing, feeling cheated, save anxiety, one best way, disappointing responses; keep those that apply as measures in every stage check (NB 25 Nov).
12. **Rules carry costs.** Every rule gets a line on what it asks of production (faces, clothing layers, two-body animation, props, crowds, units) with a cost band, and the ranking counts it (NB 25 Nov).
13. **Openings**, where the game has an authored opening. Design it before it can be played: fix the newcomer, the clock and the cost cap; define the judging words (empty minute, choice, consequence) first; write each route as rows a program reads (minute, place, people, mode, tension, what happens, status). A beat fits what the player does in it (100 m at the design's pace is a minute); hints fire on the player's own hesitation; watched scenes are few, short and skippable (NB 13, 28 Oct).
14. **Story on paper**, for a game with an authored story. AI-written plans share faults (a passive lead, the big moments happening to others, repeating stretches, no rise), so a craft teardown and a checked scene-level judge come before writing (NB 23 Nov).
   - The judge: pass mark fixed in a pushed file first; label-hinting words scrubbed blind for both sets; scenes of equal length (length pulls 10 to 15 points); seeds varied; read as a screen (admired against forgettable, not great against good); checked by judges from another model family (scan: story judge; NB 25 Nov).
   - Writing: one brief fixes the owner's decisions and the readers' weak points; key scenes are written in order, the cards following them (NB 24 Nov).
   - Testing a fix: the writer gets the complaint, not a theory; only the scenes that test it are rewritten, among unchanged controls; one neutral question counts the complaint; the gain is quoted raw and against the controls, since panels drift (0.4 on the same text) (NB 24 Nov; scan: story test).
   - Stopping: under 0.1 gained stops a revision; after two failed rewrites, rebuild from a different seed; a changed top complaint ends polishing of the old one; claim no cure beyond the evidence (scan: story judging).
   - Facts left unchecked get a second run on primary texts, then every card and scene carrying them is re-keyed; every cast gets a "voices alike" and a "line repeated" read (NB 24 Nov; scan: story judging).
   - Helper-written content gets a fresh reviewer for what each speaker could know; bulk text goes by half-price batch, built by script, thinking set explicitly, output tokens checked on a sample first (NB before 13 Oct; 8 Nov).

**Failed.**
- Written lines for the lead moved nothing (readers want deeds); a line for another character answering the lead took the scene.
- The first bulk batch lost 32 of 35 answers to the token limit.

**Cost.** A reality check about 2 weekly points; story on paper about 2 h of wall clock and 25 helper runs; a blind scene test about 3 h; bulk text US$1.15; an opening drafted as rows 0.8 h.

**Links.** NB 13 Oct (opening), 8 Nov (bulk batch), 23 to 26 Nov; scan: reality check, what-if, questions, combat, economy, temper, fifty, hype, story judge.

### 1.5 The blueprint

**Works.**
1. Class every part by whether the build could make it exactly from what is written (as written, words only, data elsewhere unreferenced, no data needed): 168 of 437 would have left the build guessing. A spot check of 40 agreed 90% on need; fix the pattern behind its misses across the whole sheet (scan: data sweep).
2. Whatever a rule reads is typed data, never prose (relations, links, dependencies), and the build fails when a tunable number also sits in code (NB 28 Oct). Values the design gives only in words go into data with a "chosen" flag; every data file names its open points; drawings and dependent files are built from the one source by script and name that script (scan: data build; NB 24 Nov).
3. Every drafted data file gets one fresh review and one fix pass (13 to 31 points a file; none passed unmended). Checkers must be able to fail, proved by breaking copies on purpose (scan: data build).
4. The keeper folds a day's work with extraction helpers and one fresh reviewer, a coverage file, a "no part lost" check and part counts by status in every run line (scan: keeper log).
5. The start gate before stage 1: every part a player feels is measured, decided (listed for the owner's overturn) or ruled out; every review theme of the reference games maps to a part with a test; the blind miss rate meets its target; two red-team rounds in a row (fresh helpers given the description with ten parts silently removed) find no material gap and each catches 9 of the 10, "material" fixed beforehand (met in the first week of play, can end a session stuck, breaks a ruling, blocks a playthrough check); checkers proven on planted faults; a technical plan, a full inventory and a sound plan; the design's numbers run through the simulators; every runtime-AI feature settled from its bench; where the game has an authored story, the owner has approved the measured story. What stays open after capped rounds goes to the owner once as scope, with recommendations (RECIPE 23 Nov; NB 25 Nov).

**Failed.**
- A 200-part checklist produced rules with passing tests the game never called (2 of 12 parts real in play).

**Cost.** One data wave of four files with strong-model reviews about 6 weekly points (estimate: the meter was shared).

**Links.** RECIPE 23 Nov (the start gate); scan: data sweep, data build, keeper log.

### 1.6 Building in stages

**Works.**
1. One run cannot build a big game: an overnight run built 21 systems in 3.5 h, but a fresh check found 7 of 8 parts thin and it passed 6 of 41 play stories in 7.5 h; public long runs read shallow; the vendor's own write-ups agree (one feature at a time, a pass list, a separate checker) (NB 25 Nov).
2. Build in big stages in playing order, each playable from the start, every system at its depth so the joins between systems meet early: a slice, not layers. Stage 1 lays the foundation designed for the whole game; the opening is polished again at the end (NB 25 Nov).
3. Each stage has finish lines fixed in advance and an automatic go or no-go; fixes are carried as a short list; there is no re-planning between stages, and the design is complete before the first (NB 25 Nov).
4. Where rules must match exactly and a cheap test bed exists, a system can be written first as a reference implementation and then ported (the first game did so for its simulation). Each rule has its home in the reference; the port answers golden rows through its real code across every branch edge from real data (below zero, zero, first step, cap, past it), plus random runs through both; planted one-line breaks find port-only rules, each homed with its test first and replanted to prove the rows fail; seeded draws carried exactly; text contracts pinned byte for byte (NB 26 to 28 Oct; 3, 6 Nov; before 13 Oct: 57,913 rows and 650,000 random runs, no difference).
5. Work out what a behaviour does at the moments the game actually has before porting it, and wire it only when every reachable outcome has something to show (NB 27 Oct).
6. Parallel lanes: each from one main commit with a written brief and acceptance cases; one fresh reviewer per lane reading the diff; main re-merged right before review; the whole suite run on main after every merge (lanes that passed alone silently dropped each other's additions). Fixes merge as one candidate of main plus only lanes that passed their own checks, run whole under one heavy lock; main moves only when every job is inside its bands (NB 17 to 18 Nov).
7. New behaviour sits behind an off-by-default switch whose off path is gated identical to the baseline (NB 17 to 18 Nov).
8. The strongest model leads mission-critical lanes; routine steps go to the standard model (NB 23 Nov).
9. After the stages, loops are few, large and measured: each takes a whole class of the biggest measured gaps, with a budget, before-and-after scores on fixed checks, a feature census and a played-evening read; a gain under the set minimum ends that area's loops, as one question to the owner (NB 23 Nov).

**Failed.**
- A build from one giant prompt began before its design was measured; its report was never written and everything waiting on it paused.
- A merge candidate carrying a start-position change failed 19 measures and was dropped whole.
- A prototype gate found untold crashes, an unusable pad and a camera hiding the player indoors.

**Cost.** A rule port 0.25 to 1 h; four lanes about 1.5 h of helpers and 0.3 h of session; a merge candidate 0.5 h of machine.

**Links.** NB 26 to 28 Oct (port), 17 to 18 Nov (lanes), 23 to 25 Nov; RECIPE 23 Nov (the one-go run).

### 1.7 Testing and review

**Works.**
1. **Fresh reviewers.** One view each, beside its last good picture and the reference, briefed from the picture (what changed, known shortfalls by name), given the requirement word for word, never the maker's verdict; they recompute from raw files and measure from the asset; new faults block; a never-reviewed view gets a full fault hunt. Every lighting state and camera side the game has is reviewed before a piece is done; a fix re-reviews every view it reaches (NB before 13 Oct: 18 of 20 planted faults caught alone, 20 of 20 beside the last good; 22 to 24 Oct).
2. **Disagreements.** Take the review with the better evidence and say which; a misnamed shortfall goes back to its reviewer with the evidence; what reviewers call subtle or taste goes to the owner (NB 14, 23, 29 Oct).
3. **Status tables lean generous.** Sample 15 rows of every self-judged table for a fresh reviewer whose reading stands unless a source says otherwise (upheld 20 of 24); a second fresh read of finished files is its own step; every "before" column names the version it read (scan: fifty, hype, story test).
4. **Checks that can fail.** Prove each new check on a control built to fail and one built to pass; plant faults, each caught by exactly one test; a crash on a missing part fails; an "ok" records what was tested. Refuse, in both the judge and the tool, two inputs whose measures match under different names (one shared on purpose is a recorded exception), testing the refusal both ways: six weeks of "heavy body" results were on an average one. A fault the eye finds and no row caught becomes a row with the faulty build as its wrong copy; a failing row is fixed in the build, never by loosening the gate (NB 13 to 14 Oct; 13, 17 to 18 Nov).
5. **A tool's verdict is a claim.** Every count check needs a picture check beside it, and the picture decides. Prove the subject is in the picture by changing one setting per run against run-to-run noise; include one test on a real file from another tool (NB 13, 18 Nov).
6. **The player's game.** Read the logged launch line and class every switch as the player's, watch-only or test; any test switch fails the pass. The game logs which path it took, since a quiet fallback can pass every mark while the feature never runs (NB 27 Oct; 1, 7 to 11 Nov).
7. **Automated players.** The tester carries a model of the controlled actor's or units' movement and reads every picture beyond its stages. When a playthrough shows a fault, first decide whose it is (the tester's steering, the game's movement, or a picture mid-stop) from the position log; stop on the goal met, never on a tolerance smaller than a step; ask the game, never a looser check (NB 27 Oct; 17, 20, 21 Nov).
8. **The nightly walk.** Every changed piece is walked in that night's packaged build; report lines never fail a walk and are checked on past nights' real files first (one found a spot missed every morning for two weeks); a check counts the outcome, not the process, and is added only where a fault slipped past (NB 5, 8, 12 Nov).
9. **Bots and experience measures.** A clumsy-player bot, a newcomer bot and one bot per play style take the experience measures (1.4 step 11) at every stage check; a reviewer reads one full played evening. Planted faults measure the checkers too (a first round caught 7 of 17; mends made after the runs do not count) (NB 23, 25 Nov; scan: checkers).
10. **Comparing variants.** Test what is recommended, not a cousin of it; cost a pipeline from its code; check the harness before blaming a variant; replay every claimed win once (half changed); differences inside judging noise are ties; a speed win that raises fallbacks is not a win (scan: brain comparison; live talk).
11. **Held-back jobs.** A tool or method improved in a loop is judged on jobs of kinds no practice job has, hash-locked with the judge outside the tool's copy, never shown to the fixer, run unchanged at every milestone; no fix may serve one job alone; far worse held-back results mean the method is fixed next (NB 14, 16, 17 Nov).
12. **Regression gates** band on work done (frames, solver passes) with clock times as information; bands come from the method's own noise (NB 17 to 18 Nov).
13. **Diagnosis.** Write the verdict rule before the run; a plain run must equal the baseline to the digit; find the frame where the fault is born; switch one mechanism off per run and give the general fix a one-sentence mechanical reason; test a named cause before building on it; when a number looks impossible, read the engine's source for that step. Count a rare fault before another try (one in 25 needs about 75 repeats) (NB 6, 14, 18, 20 Nov).
14. **Phase exits.** One fresh, read-only reviewer per exit on the strongest model, a verdict per clause (met, not met, not shown) with the deciding file; rehearsed two days early with the same brief, waiting jobs and approval stops cleared first (NB 22 Oct; 7 Nov; before 13 Oct).

**Failed.**
- Reviews of changed views only missed faults for rounds; wrong "before" pictures failed good changes.
- Three runs judged a part in pictures where it was never drawn; 17 of 17 of a tool's checks passed on torn collars.
- Tests on made-up file shapes passed twice; the first real file failed.
- A nightly walk measured nothing for three days (it looked for the wrong picture type).

**Cost.** A review takes minutes; four exit reviews about 6 minutes each; a tester fix 0.1 to 0.6 h; held-back jobs about 2.2 h to set.

**Links.** NB 22 Oct (phase exit), 7 to 25 Nov (launch line; checks; tester; bots); scan: checkers, brain comparison, fifty.

### 1.8 Production disciplines

How deep each discipline goes follows the art target and the closest distance the player meets each thing; the checks stay. Costly effects go only on what the player meets close; the rest is staged (NB 28 Oct). A far-check judgement, not a lesson: a far camera over many small units needs little of the close-up work below, a near camera on one hero needs all of it.

#### 1.8.1 Look: surfaces, light, scenery and props

**Works.**
1. Calibrate first: confirm the exposure in force from the game's run line; read a known chart through the player's camera (middle grey 110 to 135) and validate the base-colour view against it; then set surfaces inside researched reflectance bands, one kind at a time, against the reference's own regions. When two rounds' faults sit on one kind of surface, research that kind's whole method again (NB 29 Oct).
2. Wet ground: rough saturated ground plus a water clear coat on its own material copy; darkness, not smoothness, is the lever (NB 29 Oct; before 13 Oct).
3. Wear by counts studied in the references (one set in the genre pack), measured on and off in buffers with anti-aliasing and streaming off. Stains placed from their sources and baked to the piece's own unwrap; use-driven masks (where hands, feet and weather reach) beat curvature or occlusion alone; chips in a material mask on outer arrises only; grime only a little duller than the paint, and lighter on near-black (NB 21 to 30 Oct; 11 Nov).
4. Decals darken relative to the ground, never as a fixed colour; masks fade at every edge, with a test; a tint multiplies the picture's grey; loose objects receive no decals, which print on them and climb uprights (NB 26, 29 Oct; 5 Nov).
5. Scans: read the maps before choosing the material path; cut them to the prop's size class; break a tile's repeat per element (each unit its own tone, mean within 0.5%, simulated in numpy first); a pattern no scan offers is cut from real scan units into a drawn true-size layout (NB 3, 5, 12, 16 Nov).
6. Light: find the cause in code, and confirm the feature is on, before tuning; find the one quality setting by halving groups and raise it only where needed. Light a display to its designer's plan, lights off first to find what burns, each swept against a written bar (no channel clipped on over 1%). Small lights cast no shadows, but contained lights keep theirs or they light the next room; a shadow no bounce reaches is set design (NB 22 to 28 Oct).
7. Exposure: each lit interior gets its own volume weighted by the camera's place in its box; a night keeps one soft desaturated fill and tinted haze, near-black and clipped shares measured together (NB 26 Oct; 4 Nov).
8. At range, test gaps with haze off; find by review the few details that make each kind of thing read as itself at its far distance and keep those, the list held in the setting's data (NB 4 to 5 Nov).
9. Variety from material values, colourways and decals on few meshes, not more meshes. Props by script from written exact targets, one mesh per material at real size; goods at 0.9 to 1.1 of real size or left out and named; fittings as parts at mount points; no two faces share a plane; every lighting state and camera side seen through the game camera before any reviewer (NB 13 Oct; 3 Nov).
10. Shading: smooth flat-shaded goods by angle, large flat faces kept flat (fail over 5 degrees); a crease is a hard edge; fail any added face opposed to its surface; a glow sits in the model switched off for the game to raise (NB 13 to 23 Oct; 3, 11, 19 Nov).
11. Every frame-free row (intersection, floating, real size, printed words) runs before any long render; every intermediate picture is looked at as it comes off the queue (NB 14 Oct).
12. Delivered assets: fix faults in the source script and rebuild, never patch the export; join many-part models keeping every slot; read each piece's own zones, since a copied placement rule carries its assumptions (NB 13 to 24 Oct).
13. Designed shapes (a vehicle, a weapon): class dimensions from real examples, ratios checked in code, flat outlines read by a blind reviewer before any 3D work (NB 17 Nov).

**Failed.**
- One water film read as milky tile; world-aligned run maps read as grain; fixed-colour decals became cut-outs; chips as geometry read as confetti.
- A shared worn material swapped in drew the asset darker and redder by view.
- A good modelled from a description read as another trade's.
- A procedural surface passed every offline measure and read newly built.

**Cost.** A surface or piece 1.5 to 4 h; a light fault 0.3 to 2.5 h; a scenery fix 10 to 40 min; a prop 1 to 2 h; a second unit by a passed method under half the first; path-traced renders were four fifths of a 16 h batch.

**Links.** NB 13 to 30 Oct, 3 to 16 Nov; the project's look and asset-import skills.

#### 1.8.2 Clothing and cloth

**Works.**
1. Read a comparable garment from a shipped game (method, never assets); pattern at the wearer's measures; detail baked from the high model; game mesh by own retopology to the reference's targets (triangles, texel density, detail levels) (NB 27 to 28 Oct).
2. One input standard for every body (one detail level, one rest pose, one skeleton, head and hands joined, real scale, a joints file), refused by name when broken; measure the body before drafting (NB 30 Oct; 16, 18 Nov).
3. Place pieces from landmarks in the body's geometry (neck ring, shoulder and arm lines from cross-sections), never shares of height fitted on one body; check every pattern is drapeable first (closed pieces, paired seams, each ring against the girth at the strain limit) (NB 16 Nov).
4. Drape with straight elbows and bend back with the body's own weights (the exact inverse of each point's blended move); build bands and cuffs from the shell's own edge ring, sized from the body and the fabric's textbook ratios; shoes before hems, the footwear given to the solver as a smoothed separate collider (NB 28 to 30 Oct; 17, 19 Nov).
5. Solver discipline: seams closed inside each step; passes raised with the square of the fineness; the stretch limit as the published end-of-frame repair, iterated to a cap, what it cannot reach reported, kept off until a garment run shows no body-through with it on (it runs after body contact), seams still given the last word after it; layer contact along the inner cloth's facing, layers told apart by order, never name; friction once; tangles measured by winding, not stretch; every scripted setting read back (NB 29 Oct; 13 to 19 Nov).
6. Skin from the wearer's full body (nearest place, blend, keep four, renormalise), proven first on a hand-skinned garment; hide skin by the character system's body-removal map painted from the actual garments; clear every body mesh that can show on a 1 mm grid from the seen side, unsigned distance first (NB 30 Oct; 7 to 9, 17 Nov).
7. Refit to other bodies: open garments count lower layers as cover and as colliders; band rules tell cuffs from button bands; weights come from the target body (capped at 8); untangle seam-side crossings before the start check, sew with bands frozen, settle, the pass line set before any run (NB 12, 19 Nov).
8. Checks: a per-pose check (rest, arms up, stride, sitting) judged against a garment already passed, not zero; seated bars from a passed garment; each version seen packaged at the closest distance the player meets it, indoors under even light too, beside the last good film; on failure, back to the last good at once (NB 29 Oct; 17, 19 Nov).
9. Fabric reads from the garment's own colour map (weave direction, warp against weft, fades at wear points, tonal stitching), wear placed from the pattern's seams and body landmarks (NB 11, 18 Nov).
10. Chains: record every command; a changed input re-runs the whole recorded chain by one cheaper helper, old-beside-new numbers as acceptance, from the current job writer; an outside tool's skinned garment is first imported onto the wearer's skeleton in a scratch folder, every bone compared (NB 13, 17, 19 Nov).
11. An under-layer trim saves triangles, not clipping; a part always covered is a listed shortfall, not a block (NB 30 Oct; 11 Nov).

**Failed.**
- Bent-elbow drapes; the solver's elastic for cuffs (three tries); a skinned stand collar (one weight set cannot follow trunk and neck); hems cut on bare feet.
- Offline marks passed while the game drew holes (no per-pose check, reduced detail never looked at).
- A second solver on the first one's job slid or hung high with four to five times the faults; a commercial auto-fit at defaults; layering in an outside simulator; a layered fix where seams lacked the last word after the strain limit (two tries, set aside).
- Bought garment packs judged against the floor: none passed. Tiling fabric layers alone read as felt or chalk.

**Cost.** The chain about 3 h against 17 planned; a fix 0.2 to 1.5 h; a re-run chain 0.1 h of helper; a refit about 7 h; solver loop rounds 43 to 57 min each.

**Links.** NB "Clothing: one game garment"; 26 Oct to 19 Nov; skill game-garment.

2. Hair chosen by a side-by-side in the shipped build: the same camera, a patch's grey normalised by skin, a decision table written first; every change judged at every distance, strands only after the engine has warmed them (about 60 frames) (NB 7 to 8, 17 Nov).

#### 1.8.4 Motion and animation

**Works.**
1. Start from the method the references actually use, read from their files (NB 28 Oct).
2. Measure the source first: a planted foot must not travel in its own frame; check pelvis height after batch retargets, a third-party clip's rest pose and bone names, and an export read inside the engine before fixing the reader (NB 25, 30 Oct; 13, 17 Nov).
3. Judge a retarget against its own source frame by frame: slide and foot height by what the retarget adds over the source's planted spans, knees relative to the source's, any limb turning over 90 degrees a frame flagged; run source against source first (NB 17 Nov).
4. Find a planted foot by its height over the ground under it; judge worst steps by eye on a fixed time step with a still side camera, and at the player's camera steered as a player steers; measure every axis of the head before fixing one; measure from exported bones, never a tool's summary (NB 24 to 28 Oct; 6, 8, 13 Nov).
5. Stops chosen by foot, stance quarter and remaining distance and played whole; carry a figure at its clip's whole-clip speed; turn with turning clips (NB 26 Oct).
6. Spots a body must land on exactly (a seat): entries measured once, starts derived to land on the slot, every entry played in the real room sampling limbs against the world, kept by a tool, chosen by heading (NB 28 Oct).
7. A clean-up tool fixes contacts, not posture, and is judged on real footage and on whether the move reaches its end pose; a real-footage control runs through any solver before generated input is blamed (NB 11 to 13 Nov).

**Failed.**
- Motion matching rebuilt on another skeleton; feet pinned to a sliding source; in-place turns; speed read past a clip's end; absolute 2 cm foot bars that raw capture fails itself.

**Cost.** 1 to 3.5 h a try; a retarget check 0.25 h.

**Links.** NB 22 Oct to 17 Nov (walk, sit, solves, retargets).

#### 1.8.5 Audio and voice

**Works.**
1. Study a reference's soundscape and dialogue from its metadata (cues, volumes, attenuation, lines counted by situation and speaker type, its own timers), never its audio; balance only the played mix can give is a blind spot (NB 28 Oct).
2. Voices: takes gated per joined take as well as per line, every scripted word checked as spoken; two tries per candidate, and fewer candidates rather than a weaker one; ship exactly the settings the owner heard; remake every prepared line when a voice changes engine; loudness matched by measurement; every shipped model registered with the disk cleanup by the tool that loads it (NB 26 Oct; 1, 6 Nov; before 13 Oct).
3. Accents and sound faults are judged by ear; a classifier only screens; a listening test changes only the voice and asks the owner what numbers cannot (NB 26 Oct; before 13 Oct).
4. Where the game calls a model at run time: measure delay inside the packaged game from the line sent to the first sound; send the lowest thinking setting under a small output allowance (read the shipped code, not the bench: it cut a first sentence from 3.20 to 1.24 s); caching saves cost, not wait; record real lines for the owner's ear; scan local models from written questions with speeds measured on the target machine (NB 2, 4, 16, 25 Nov; scan: live talk).

**Failed.**

#### 1.8.6 Tools and the asset toolchain

**Works.**
1. Search the project for earlier tools before building one; split method from content in every tool (NB 13, 26 Oct).
2. Desktop programs by script: a plug-in timer runs job files from a folder and writes a heartbeat; each job checks its files exist first; no unattended step may raise a box only a person can close (NB 26, 30 Oct; 9, 21 Nov).
3. Prove a step ran by the result line it just wrote; check units at every hand-over; check the exported file, not the scene; prove every scripted material by a render log with no compile failure (NB 26 to 29 Oct; 12 Nov).
4. Edit scripts, tables and expressions with an exact-text editor, never a shell heredoc or one-line stream edit (five losses) (NB 13 to 21 Nov).
5. Test a tool from copies of its job files, outputs outside its folder: run twice and compare hashes, measure in a second program, the identity case first, one real production input its examples avoid. After a maker's update, date the source files themselves and compare outputs byte for byte first (NB 11 to 13 Nov).
6. Improve an in-house tool by loop: a copy under version control as the maker's version; a short design note of shared interfaces, each part naming the game item it serves and the hours it saves; one fixer a round with a written brief; behaviour proved unchanged by a call-stream hash (NB 13, 18 Nov).
7. Speed work: trace which path each judged job runs and measure there, then one change at a time, outputs hashed against the baseline (NB 16 Nov).
8. One shared check resolves every output path, refuses relative paths and places outside the named work folders, and fails closed; long runs queue only through the session's background runner; before a tool's subscription lapses, every project is written out in open formats by script (NB 14, 18, 21, 24 Nov).

#### 1.8.7 Engine, performance and builds

**Works.**
1. Write the frame, memory and animation budgets, and those for characters or units, before the first content, and test them in a scene far fuller than play (NB 28 Oct).
2. Size the heaviest repeated content by a ladder climbed from small N (copies of a district, a unit count), the clock held during captures, the cost per copy read (NB 16 Nov).
3. Measure the shipped build on a scripted run with the real background load; report the worst frame, the count over the bar and peak memory; locate each slow frame, break it down with the engine's named timers, then a full trace; fix and re-measure the whole matrix (NB 21 Oct; 6 Nov).
4. A measure writes its own conditions into every line; new content changes the budget, so re-measure every condition after each piece; test without an outside load before blaming it; read card memory per process (NB 23 to 27 Oct).
5. Find a cost by switching one thing off in the packaged copy's data before writing code; bake reflections once, stamped with hashes of their inputs; record a shader cache on the packaged game for the one platform the cook builds (NB 21 to 24 Oct).
6. Card memory: name every uncompressed picture, block-compress each family by its use, and try the engine maker's next-lower presets before custom values (NB 16 Nov).
7. Builds: content loaded by name needs a copy step and an always-cook rule in the same change, and the build fails when code names a file staging leaves out; check a build's size against free space above the drive's floor; keep test frames out of version control (NB 27 Oct; 3, 16 Nov; before 13 Oct).

### 1.9 Process

**Works.**
1. **The notebook.** Whoever learns a lesson writes it the same day (what was made, the method in setting-free words, tools, target and check, cost, what failed and why, evidence), tagged ANY, GENRE or game-only (LEDGER for the first game); every helper's brief ends with its notebook lines. At each phase end writers reading by search rewrite the recipe, a fresh reviewer per piece, the writer resumed to fix, every ANY lesson checked against two far games (NB 8, 9, 23, 26 Nov).
2. **Records.** Cap what is read most (by entries or by words, as the rule counts); move, never drop; a start reads only rules, state and open asks; progress numbers are derived by script from the work's own files (NB 25 Oct; 4, 8, 20 Nov).
3. **The owner's time and taps.** The standing page's taps are counted per day as a cost. The owner's hands are asked for only where nothing else can do it, enforced by the page's own check; nothing waits on the owner off the page. Choices are shown as pictures from the shipped build with numbers beside them, the pick stored where the system reads it; a narrow miss is said plainly (NB 28 Oct; 2, 17, 18, 23 Nov).
4. **Time.** Log each unit's window from the clock as it starts (a reconstruction from commits is only a floor); re-estimate after review and first use (NB 28 Oct; 12 Nov).
5. **Allowance.** Every session, started by anyone, carries its own pace stop against the shared line; the weekly stop sits below the limit by the readings' lag, and a reading's date is checked against the reset; a job on the remainder before a reset saves after every step, keeps a state file and is restarted after the reset if cut off (NB 23, 25 Nov; scan: keeper log, checkers).
6. **Cost.** Quote from the bill or recomputed tokens, never a budget counter; pilot a paid run at a few dozen rows; check the model in force in the worker's own records at every start; sweep every setting that can raise cost or stop work; compare a cheaper model blind on about five recent items (it must match four at clearly lower cost); a paid tool that would fix an open problem goes to the owner within a day; cost an asset family licence first, then generated, then made (NB 27 Oct; 5, 8, 11, 14, 16 Nov).
7. **Sessions.** One cheap recurring gate starts work only when the last session has stopped, its go-or-wait check in the prompt hook so a wait costs no model start; a session stamps its stop after its last write, at least 10 minutes before a scheduled start, and counts as dead after 30 idle minutes; a stop hook knows the scheduled worker by how its log opens, never by name; an idle hold is bounded by the longest delay the owner accepts for an unread instruction (NB 4 to 16 Nov).
8. **Results never missed.** While any job runs, the results list is read before every message, however short; wake-ups are scheduled messages bound to the session, since timers in an idle cloud container do not fire (NB 25 Nov).
9. **Helpers** are briefed to publish nothing; no session commits onto another's branch (scan: combat; NB 21 Nov).
10. **Storage.** Clean only from the folder map; backups only copy, are read back by hash and sample-restored weekly; each scheduled job's last result is read from the scheduler's own records (NB 25 Oct; 17 Nov).

**Failed.**
- A chain of scheduled runs (a scheduled run may not start another); a gate counting its own runs; word caps hiding entry counts; rulings cited from memory.
- A stop hook matching the task's name held every chat that had read the task file; its synthetic tests passed.
- Nine parallel sessions with no pace stop used half a day's share in an hour; a whole day ran on the wrong model (cost +116%).

**Cost.** A gate or record tool 0.2 to 1 h; a playbook distillation 1.5 h and 2.4 million helper tokens; a blind model comparison about one weekly point.

**Links.** NB 25 Oct (order ledger), 2 Nov (standing page), 4 to 26 Nov (gates; spend; backups; pace stops; results); the owner's-page skill.

## 2. The genre pack: open-world social simulation

Games where the player lives among named people who notice, remember, talk and react. References studied: Kingdom Come: Deliverance II (simulates; leads for a town that notices and judges), Mafia (stages its streets), GTA V and Red Dead Redemption 2 (crowds, wanted states), Sleeping Dogs and L.A. Noire (foot chases), and six to eight story games for review studies.

**The eight laws and their typical rules.**
1. **Knowing.** Witnesses learn by sight and sound; sight passes see-through surfaces and an opening's own frame blocks nothing through it; within earshot a deed is heard without its doer; a threat alone raises no alarm until a witness reports. A speaker's facts are chosen by code before the reply; a usual week is derived from the routine data, never stored twice (NB 30 Oct; 5, 18 Nov).
2. **Things.** Readable physical rules: stealth works when done right; chance only at the violent end, from one seed held in the save so a reload never rerolls; effects by body zone (NB 25 Nov). Text an object carries (timetables, prices, hours) lives in data, checked against the place's files; repeated pieces vary by a written function checked by script (NB 13 Oct).
3. **People.** Ties are typed data (family, friend, grudge, debt) that form, strengthen and break in play, or gossip has nothing to travel on; traits kept only when read and visible, every person extreme on at least one; full memory and routines for the people who matter, a lighter level for the crowd, costs per person and per deed stated (NB 24 to 25 Nov; scan: temper).
4. **Exchange.** Economy run long with noise per play style (no one rich by the third week; no crime paying twice the middle one). Every talk act (an offer with its sum, a threat, a lie, a rumour, a bluff) is reachable from suggested lines: free wording adds colour, never power; a whole run per style by pad alone is a stage test (scan: economy; NB 25 Nov).
5. **Time.** Save on every change of state, the save waiting (never refused) in a fight or scene; any restart costs at most 2 min (NB 28 Oct).
6. **Recovery.** Failure carries on as a named outcome the world keeps and later jobs read, never a reload; job states none, active, done, failed; every failure opens something as well as costing something (NB 28 Oct; 25 Nov).
7. **Story.** Every job and beat has at least three routes (force, talk, stealth or scheme). Side stories as a ruled list: every ending changes what someone knows or says, givers from the existing cast, patience on people rather than deadlines, a place named by a landmark when there is no marker (NB 8, 25 Nov).
