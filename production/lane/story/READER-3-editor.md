## 1. The spine as it stands, and whether it holds

In October 1990 a stranger with £23.50 inherits his dead uncle's minicab office in a port town gutted by the end of the dock labour scheme, with a book of bad debts and a standing arrangement to carry an envelope to the ferry landing for people nobody names. Week one asks what he will do with that, and ends on Sheila's question: wind it down or take it over. For seven weeks the firm can grow into the port's criminal machine while three rivals attack his people, his clean money and his cover, and a detective folds his nights into an unsolved warehouse fire. In the last week a VAT inspector opens the fare book that has hidden his night money since day one, and what the books, the street's talk and one friendship hold decides whether he keeps a kingdom, keeps a life, or loses both.

Does it hold? As a frame for play, yes: a clean inciting incident on night one, a strong first-act break on Sunday, a hard end point (the books open), and one idea of real quality. The climax asks which of his two books was true, which is the outline's third question, "which life was real", turned into an object he can hold. As a story, no, not yet, for four reasons. The protagonist wants nothing, so no line runs from the first scene to the last. The founding mystery has no content, and the climax never touches it. The middle act, about 70% of play, has no scenes. And the forces the first two acts build (the outfit, the three rivals, the fire) are not what the third act tests: the climax brings in a new, unnamed antagonist in the last week. Today the spine is a situation with a deadline. The fixes below turn it into a story without adding a system.

## 2. Structural notes, most serious first

### 2.1 The founding mystery is empty, and the climax never pays it off

Where: section 3 (Alison, Ellis, Walsh, Iveson), Act I's five threads, gap 3. Five threads hang from the warehouse fire, and the fire has no when, no whose, no casualty, no street version and no truth. The ruling "the truth is never the street's" means the truth must exist somewhere other than the street; today it exists nowhere. Act III is about the fare book, and the fire is not in it.

There is a logic hole as well. The fire happened before Tom arrived, so a detective cannot tie his nights to it without a bridge, and none is written.

Why it matters to a player: in week one the journalist asks about it, the detective is on it, and the dead uncle's book names it. A player invited to care about a mystery that never resolves feels cheated, and spends fifteen hours watching for an answer that never comes.

Fixes:
- (a) Recommended: run the fire through the fare book. On the night of the fire, car two took two men to the warehouse row and back, and the fare is in the book in Mickey's hand, because "every fare goes in the book" was his rule. That one line is what ties the fire to the outfit; the real book only explains it. Ellis wants that page. The inspector's Thursday item is the September book. Alison knows a cab was seen. In Act III Tom has to decide what to do with one line in his own book: leave it, alter it (and the inspector may notice), give it to Ellis (her trade), or burn the book (a second fire, after which the books cannot hold). The mystery and the climax become one object, and Sheila's rule from minute 1:45 pays off.
- (b) Make the warehouse a bonded store holding tobacco under Customs bond (canon allows tobacco). Goods out the back, then the fire, so the missing stock never shows. Iveson's yard holds some of it, and the Widow's smuggling is the trade. The people robbed are Customs and Excise, who also run VAT, so the Act III letter is the same department closing in. The street's version ("an insurance job, Agar's doing") is the wrong one, as the ruling wants.
- (c) Decide whether anyone died. A night man dead in the fire turns a fraud into a manslaughter case. That explains why a detective sergeant owns it rather than the fire brigade, and why Alison will not drop it. If nobody died, say so, and give Ellis a smaller, personal reason (2.6).

Whichever is chosen: one page for the truth, one line for the street's version, and a list of who knows which.

### 2.2 The climax has no cause, and none of the story's antagonists take part in it

Where: Act III and the endings. A courteous letter arrives from nowhere. The inspector has no name. He brings one item each morning and the result is graded off screen. None of the seven factors that decide the ending (ending signs, 30 September) is a rival, and none is the outfit. Only Ellis crosses into Act III, through her trade. Fifteen hours of rivals end at a sit-down in Act II and have no bearing on the outcome, unless they have killed the business outright.

It also breaks the pillar. Act III is producing a document or asking for it in writing, a round of phone calls and one trade: talk and paperwork. The verbs that got the full budget (carries, break-ins, fights, driving) have no part in the climax.

The July balance log found that answering every morning saves only what was nearly saveable, and changes nothing for a player who never built a business. For one of the three ways to play, the climax is scenery.

The period logic is weak too. A VAT officer is paid to find tax that was under-declared. Fares inflated by the wash hand him more tax, not less, so "too much" is only a danger if he can see the fares are not credible (fuel and mileage against takings) and passes it on to Customs' own investigators or the Revenue. As far as I know, laundering the proceeds of ordinary crime only became an offence in its own right in 1993; check that with the period research. A British player who has run a small business will spot this.

Why it matters: a climax should put the protagonist's hardest choice under the most pressure from the forces the story built. Here those forces sit the week out and the player's hands are idle.

Fixes:
- (a) Give the letter a cause. Either Tom's own wash brings it (the declared fares climb past what a firm his size could earn), or a tip-off does, from whoever he has hurt most. Agar, whose weapon is paper, is the natural sender; a driver the Widow poached is another. Recommended: the wash raises the risk and the worst-hurt rival pulls the trigger, and in the last days Tom learns which one it was, as a sign. The climax then grows out of his own choices.
- (b) Make everybody move in inspection week. The rivals smell weakness: the Widow bids for his drivers, Agar's solicitor writes again, and Cammack makes noise to bring uniforms to the door on an inspection morning. The outfit wants its pages kept safe. Ellis times her trade to the week, and Alison's paper comes out on the Thursday. Each morning's item becomes a night's job: taking the September book back from wherever it has gone, clearing the office before a search, a drive with a rival in the back seat. Days are the inspector; nights are the hands.
- (c) Give the never-built player something to protect. His honest books are the only proof that he is a straight man, or the inspection reaches into his life (the friend's job, Sheila's thirty-one years). At the very least, write a "there is nothing here to find" scene so the week reads as peace rather than as a skipped act.

In every case, name the inspector and give him a manner. A tired, courteous man who has seen every trick is a character; "the inspector" is a function.

### 2.3 Tom wants nothing, so the story has no engine

Where: section 2 and gap 1. The treatment says it plainly: Tom has no want, no fear, no flaw and no arc. An unvoiced avatar with an unspoken past is legitimate, but then the situation has to supply the want. The pressures it supplies (Friday's rent, the envelope, Sheila's question) are week-one deadlines and all of them are gone by Act II.

Why it matters: without a want there is nothing to measure success or loss against. The endings describe where he stands because nobody decided what he was after, and the player cannot feel a price for something nobody wanted.

Fixes:
- (a) Recommended: name the want the design already has, which is to be known. The name ladder (the new owner, Nowak, Tom, Tommy) is a want made visible, and Sheila holding back his name is its lock. A man from "nothing and nobody" (the ruling's own words) wants a place where people know his name. His need is to learn what that costs. In the Kingdom everyone knows his name and nobody says it warmly; in the Straight Life one person does. The night Sheila first calls him Tom becomes a designed beat and the emotional peak of Act II, and the last word of each ending is a name (section 5).
- (b) Use the letter. Tom arrives with "one suitcase and a letter" and nobody ever reads it. Make it Mickey's, written weeks before he died, and about the Hook rather than Tom's past, so the ruling stands. For example: "Keep Sheila and Ron in work. Finish what I couldn't." That line is ambiguous (finish the arrangement, or finish with it), so it is Sheila's Sunday question in the dead man's own hand, and a want Tom can keep or betray.
- (c) Give him a fear: being nobody again, shown at the start in the £23.50. Show it once more at the end: on the last day he stands in a phone box counting coins, as he did on day one.

A flag on his one trait (he lays hands on no woman, nobody sixty or over, and not the priest), which still waits on a ruling. It makes two of the three rival heads physically untouchable: Jensen is a woman and Agar is sixty-one. If the trait stands, Cammack is the only head Tom can fight, and the story should be built knowing that (2.6).

### 2.4 The middle act is a framework, not a story

Where: Act II and gap 2. It runs seven game weeks, 11 to 14 hours, about 70% of play, and has no written scene. Three identical five-rung ladders run side by side. The one guaranteed collision has no evening chosen and nobody named at the door. The closing sit-down is with "one head", unnamed. There is no midpoint. Nothing says what Act II is for a player who winds the firm down. And the one story proposal (the old firm hands him the Hook and fades) removes a conflict rather than adding one.

Why it matters: the player meets the same five beats three times over (noticed, a visit, pressure, a threat, a sit-down) and starts to see the machine. With no midpoint there is no turn, and the act sags exactly where most players stop playing.

Fixes:
- (a) Put a named midpoint on the outline's second question, "how big can it get before your two lives touch". The guaranteed collision is that touch, so name the evening and whoever is at the door. For example: the first night the friend comes up to the flat, the night it stops being Mickey's, Ron is at the door with blood on his coat (or the landing man asks for Tom by name, or Ellis's notice of a search arrives), and the guest sees it. The calendar offers a stage: Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990, around game week 4 of 9, the act's middle. It brings a crowd on the quay after dark (the one night that is not empty), cover for noise, and a fire night that rhymes with the warehouse. Beats fire on states, not dates, so the rule would be: the collision fires on the first evening its state allows, and if that state holds by Bonfire Night, it is staged there.
- (b) Stagger the rivals into a rising order instead of three parallel ladders. Cammack peaks first (noise on the street, a man near Tom's level, a fight Tom can have). The Widow peaks in the middle (people: she bids for Ron and the drivers, which is the act's emotional cost). Agar peaks last (paper: the licence, the solicitor, then the tip to Customs that opens Act III). No ladder skips a rung; they simply peak in turn, and the act gains a shape: the street, then his people, then paper, then the books.
- (c) Write the wind-down player's Act II. The ground opens and the rivals come sooner (the proposal already says so). His verbs become protecting his people without cover: Ron courted by the Widow, the drivers taxed at the dock gate, Sheila leaned on for the book. It is the hardest way through, and it needs its own scenes.

On the old firm proposal: as written, the antagonist hands over the Hook and leaves, which is a deus ex machina. If it is kept, make it cost: they hand over the Hook along with their debts, their enemies and their share of the fire.

On the closing sit-down: write all three, with words, and let the state pick which one plays. The sit-down in the back of his own cab, driving while they talk, is the best idea in the rivals section; make it the default.

### 2.5 The outfit is faceless, and the treatment's reading of it contradicts canon

Where: section 3, Act I, contradiction 3 and gap 4. Canon says Mickey left Tom "a half-dead criminal outfit". The treatment follows a note that reads it as somebody else's outfit Mickey served. The landing man has no name and a dozen fixed lines, and nobody says what the arrangement is. Leaving the envelope's contents unsaid is fine. Leaving the arrangement unsaid is not, because the first ask is the inciting incident, so the story opens on a hole.

Why it matters: whether Tom is the boss or the courier decides the whole power fantasy. A player told he has inherited an outfit and then sent to run errands for strangers will ask who he works for, and the game has no answer.

Fixes:
- (a) Recommended, and canon outranks the note: the outfit is Mickey's and now Tom's, meaning Ron, the drivers who know, and the runs. The landing man is the other side of Mickey's trade, not his master: a buyer, a partner, or an official on the take. If he is a customs man (the customs lamp goes out at eleven, and DESIGN already counts a customs officer among those who never go to the police), then the arrangement is how Mickey's outfit paid for a blind eye at the landing. The letter from the same department in Act III then becomes a reckoning Tom can see coming.
- (b) A hybrid: the outfit is Mickey's, but it is the junior partner in someone else's trade (the Widow's, if the fire was her tobacco). Saying no on night one is then a declaration of independence with a price, and the Widow's pressure in Act II is that price. Canon stands, because the outfit he inherits is still his.
- (c) Whichever is chosen, give the landing man a name and one scene by night seven, and escalate the four asks of week one: an envelope, an envelope, a holdall, then a passenger in the back of the cab. That gives Act I's unwritten Friday to Sunday a shape, and it raises what a "no" costs.

Any change to how canon reads here needs the owner's ruling.

### 2.6 The antagonists have methods but no motives

Where: sections 3 and 4, gaps 10 and 11. The three lines of attack (his people, his clean money, his cover) are the strongest structural idea in the design: each rival is a different kind of threat. But each one wants only "the Hook, the port's gate". Their characters come from July cards written for an older version of the game, none has a scene, and no sit-down has words. Ellis has no want of her own, and whether she can be bought is still open.

Why it matters: an antagonist who wants only territory is weather. A player cannot outthink weather, and "counter with what he knows" needs something specific to know.

Fixes, one per antagonist:
- Cammack as the mirror (recommended as the default Act II head). Port-born and unwilling to admit it, he wants what Tom was handed for nothing: the stranger inherited the place the local lad could never earn. Their sit-down is about exactly that. If Tom's trait stands, he is also the only head Tom can fight.
- The Widow wants Tom's men because the dock gate after the scheme is her labour market and she hires ex-dockers. Ron's old gang already work for her. Poaching Ron, not "his least loyal man", is the dramatic choice: the outline had it, DESIGN softened it, and Ron's set-up ("strength and cash, in that order") points straight at it.
- Agar is old money with no money. In late 1990 interest rates stood near 15% and property was falling; he borrowed against the boom and is going under. His paper war is desperation. He wants the yard or the quay frontage, or the real book, because he is in it ("Mickey and I had an understanding once").
- Ellis needs a stake. Recommended: she wrote the fire up as an accident at first and was wrong, and she needs a name before her mistake comes out. That makes her trade logical, makes the threat personal, and gives Tom something to counter with. On bribery: recommended that she cannot be bought but a man on her team can (canon allows police corruptible as individuals, never as a thesis), and Ellis reads the bribe as a sign.
- The inspector needs a name (the July log had one) and a manner.
- Garbutt works as a function. Give him one secret: he went to Mickey's funeral, so why?

### 2.7 Too many relationships, none finished, and the friendship that decides the ending is generic

Where: section 3 and gaps 8 and 9. The day-life ring has six people. Ada's story stops after the tea and Darren's after week one. June has one line. Walsh has lines and no story. Alison has two unwritten scenes. Philip has nothing. The endings turn on "one friendship", built from visits with no score, and any friend counts. Sheila, the best-drawn relationship in the treatment, is never framed as that friend.

Why it matters: the ending's emotional weight rests on a friendship the player may have built by calling on someone regularly. A generic friend makes a generic ending, and six half-built relationships cost more to make than three finished ones.

Fixes:
- (a) Recommended: make Sheila the spine relationship. Her trust already opens the real book, the warning on the books and his name. The wash breaks Mickey's rule ("every fare goes in the book"), and she "thinks less of him for it". The protagonist's need and the central mechanic already pull against each other through her. Make her the friend whose judgement each ending shows first.
- (b) Finish three and trim the rest. Alison gets her Act III: her paper comes out in inspection week, and her week-one rule pays off in public, because true things he confided stay safe and lies she found out become the story. June gets a want: one thing from the locked room. She is also the natural heir if the will is ever contested, so Agar's lawyers could back her claim, which gives Agar a lever and June a choice; and she carries the handing-on in the Straight Life, as the July card had it. Walsh gets the first fare: it carries Mickey's last envelope to the chapel, so night one has two envelopes, one clean and one dirty. He buried Mickey and knows who came to the funeral, and Remembrance Sunday (11 November 1990) is his scene. Religion stays a part of life, never a mechanic.
- (c) Philip Danby. Canon names a teacher as the second love interest, but the school stands closed and no child may be shown or spoken of, so his working life can be neither seen nor talked about. Either give him a story about the building (Agar wants to buy the closed school; Philip wants it kept), with every word kept to the building and his post and never his pupils, or let the romance be Alison's alone and spend the saving on her. Recommended: the second, unless the owner wants two romances.

Ada and Darren: Ada's window should see an ending's last image (section 5). Darren is "loyal to whoever helped him last", which makes him Act II's natural informer: let him be the one who sells Tom to a rival or to Ellis, so his first week is set-up rather than a dead end.

### 2.8 Logic holes

1. Ellis ties Tom's nights to a fire that happened before he came. That needs the bridge in 2.1.
2. Sheila saw Ron arguing with a stranger two nights before Mickey died, and Ron found the body. The ruling says it was his heart and nobody thinks otherwise. As written, this is a murder-mystery clue with no murder, and every player will chase it. Either cut it, or keep the heart and give the argument a cause: Mickey had told the landing he was finished, Ron argued it out for him, and the heart gave out the night after. That is no murder, but it is a reason, and Tom's answer on night one then echoes Mickey's last decision.
3. Why the nephew and not the daughter? Players will ask this the moment June appears at minute 1:30. The letter or the real book should answer it.
4. Tom drives a fare in his first hour. In 1990, private hire outside London was licensed by district councils under the 1976 Act, and drivers and operators each needed a licence and had to be "fit and proper". A newcomer driving fares unlicensed is a hole, or a hook: Agar's first letter can say exactly that, and any later arrest makes "fit and proper" a real threat to the firm's licence.
5. Probate. Mickey has been dead three weeks, and his estate cannot be settled that fast. Either let it pass, or use it: probate explains the £23.50 (Tom cannot touch Mickey's money yet) and gives Agar and June a lever.
6. The VAT logic in 2.2: an officer gains nothing from over-declared fares unless they are not credible.
7. "On the last day he can reach only a few people", but nothing says why. Give a reason: Customs have taken the books and the office is closed to him, so he works a phone box with a handful of coins. Set the phone box up in week one (the witness's walk to it already does half the work).
8. Nothing says what happens if the constable takes the envelope. The outfit's answer to a lost envelope is the obvious first danger from the outfit, so write it.
9. Alison has lodged "above Ada's since a month before Mickey died". A local paper's port reporter would already live in town, so lodging marks her as new. Why did she come? Pay it off (she came for the fire, or for Mickey) or cut it.
10. If Ellis searches the office before Sheila trusts Tom, does she find the real book? Nothing says, and it decides whether the book can ever pay off.
11. After the Burn Both credits "the town stays open to walk" (addendum A7). For a man in a manhunt, that is either a hunt that never ends or a state the close has to change. Decide which.
12. Act II "opens when the business has grown". For the player who wound it down, it never opens (2.4 c).

### 2.9 Nobody else's loss is on the table

Where: section 5. Every ending describes Tom. In a town where the docks have laid men off, the firm is the living of Sheila (thirty-one years at the counter), Ron, Pauline, Kevin and Terry, and the treatment never says what becomes of them.

Why it matters: a man who arrives with nothing has little to lose, and the stakes of a rise story are what he gains and who comes to depend on it. The Straight Life's cost, "everything he could have built", stays abstract until named people lose their work over it.

Fixes:
- (a) Each ending's close says what happens to Sheila, Ron and the drivers: who still has a job, who is in the Widow's pay.
- (b) Make handing the firm on a choice about who keeps their jobs, not only about who can hold it.
- (c) Show the cost before it lands: on the Monday after the Sunday answer, Pauline asks whether her job is safe.

## 4. Clichés and familiar beats, and how to turn each

| Familiar beat | Where it comes from | The turn |
|---|---|---|
| A man arrives in a grim northern port after a relative's death and finds the local crime | Get Carter, almost beat for beat; Ron's argument with a stranger invites the comparison | The town expects a Carter and gets a manager. Somebody says it to him: "You're not here about how Mickey went, are you? Nobody did for him." The death really was his heart. |
| The heir to a criminal empire | Countless games | What he inherits is an obligation, not an empire: the real book shows Mickey was paying, not earning |
| The stranger who plays rival gangs off against each other | Red Harvest, Yojimbo | Keep the pattern and make it a verb: "counter with what he knows" is the player playing them off, and Garbutt sells the means |

| A crusading reporter whose editor wants the story dropped | State of Play and many more | Her editor wants it run, loud and soon; she wants it right. That is a conflict Tom can use |

## 5. The endings

All three share the same faults. None has a last scene, only "who is where, who calls him what". None resolves the fire, the outfit, the real book, June or Walsh. Nobody else's fate is said (2.9). And the better turns are built to be rare, "not on a first playthrough", in a 15 to 20 hour game most people will finish once. That puts the most satisfying ending in front of an audience that barely exists. Make the better turn reachable for a careful first run, and keep rarity for something harder.

The hinge for all three should be the inspector's closing line on the last morning, one line chosen by the state: "That all seems to be in order, Mr Nowak", or "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak". Then the close.

The count stands within the ruling: three shown is inside "two or three", and five underneath is a matter of names, not build.

### The Kingdom

Earned? Yes, by play. Since the July fix, the books holding is a judgement on three acts of washing, not on one week of paperwork, which is the right test. But the cost ("the port and nobody in it") is stated, never shown.

Last image now: none.

Proposed: after ten at the ferry landing, a man Tom sent steps up to a stranger off the boat, and the stranger asks, "Nowak's?" The arrangement carries his name now. He has become Mickey, and one day somebody will inherit him. On the plainer turn, everybody in the close calls him Mr Nowak and nobody calls him Tom. On the better turn, Sheila writes the day's last fare in the book and says "Night, Tom" on her way out.

Missing: what he gave up, named; whether Sheila stays; where each rival ended up; what happened to the fire-night page.

### The Straight Life

Earned? For the man who hands the firm on, yes. Handing over is a deliberate act, and three days of watching whether it holds is a good, quiet coda. For the man who never built anything, only half: the climax does not test him (2.2 c), so the ending is earned in Acts I and II and simply waited for in Act III.

Last image now: none.

### Burn Both

Earned? Not as defined. "None of the others fit" is a fall-through, and "what doing nothing produces" makes tragedy out of passivity, which reads as punishment, not drama. ## 7. Verdict

The premise and the first week are the best thing here: a specific, period-true machine in which the fare book is both alibi and noose, and a first night a player would retell. After week one's Thursday the treatment is a framework rather than a story: the hero wants nothing, the founding mystery is empty, the middle act has no scenes, and the climax is a paperwork test in which none of the antagonists, the fire or the outfit takes part. Decide what the fire was and run it through the fare book, give Tom a want he can lose (to be known, with Sheila's "Tom" as the prize), and make inspection week the moment every enemy moves, and the spine will hold.
