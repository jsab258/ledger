# LEDGER: the story, as the design tells it

## 1. The story in three sentences

In October 1990 Tom Nowak, a stranger with one suitcase, a letter and £23.50, steps off the ferry at Meridian to take over what his dead uncle Mickey left him: a minicab office on Quay Street in the Hook, a book of debts nobody can collect, and an envelope that Mickey's old arrangement still expects at the ferry landing after ten. Over the next two months he can turn the cab firm into the port's criminal machine while three rivals squeeze his people, his clean money and his cover, and a detective ties his nights to a warehouse fire nobody will explain. Then a VAT inspector opens the fare book, and what the books, the street's talk and one friendship hold at the end of that week decides whether he keeps a kingdom, walks away to a straight life, or loses both.

## 2. Tom Nowak

Who he is. Tom is 32, the son of a Polish post-war settler and Mickey's sister. He has never been to the Hook, and everyone there knows him only as Mickey's nephew by name. His life before is left unsaid by ruling: nobody asks, and he never tells. He is not voiced; his lines are text the player types or picks.

Where he comes from, in the story: nowhere the game shows. He arrives after Mickey's funeral, which he missed, with £23.50 against a rent of £18 due on Friday: a hard opening, "from having nothing and nobody".

What he wants. The sources give him no want of his own, only pressures: pay Friday's rent; carry Mickey's envelope or not; answer Sheila at the week's end. The approved outline frames the game as three questions put to him in turn: what did you inherit; how big can it get before your two lives touch; which life was real. The player's answers stand in for his wants.

What he fears. Not written anywhere. His one trait is a rule the prototype invented, that he will not lay hands on a woman, anyone of sixty or over, or the priest; it waits on the owner's ruling.

How he changes. By position, never by numbers or skill: money, crew, property, clothes (as he arrived, a decent jacket, a good overcoat, and a dark coat for nights), which doors open, and what the town calls him: the new owner, then Nowak, then Tom, then Tommy to two or three. The gate is knowing him, not liking him; Sheila alone withholds his name until she trusts him. By the ending:

- The Kingdom: the port's man, people stepping aside on the pavement; civil and no more, unless one friend still counts him.
- The Straight Life: no business, one friendship kept; at best he handed the firm on and walked away clean.
- Burn Both: neither the business nor the people, possibly while hunted.

The endings say where he stands. None of the sources says what he has become.

## 3. The people who matter

Mickey (Michael Suddaby). Tom's uncle, dead three weeks when the game opens: his heart, at the office, early one morning; Ron found him; nobody thinks otherwise (a ruling). He is seen only through others, in two approved lines. His real book, in his own hand, is the buried truth: one page ties the warehouse fire to the outfit.

Sheila Dunn. The bookkeeper, 53, at Mickey's since she was 22. She promised Mickey she would size up whoever inherited, so Tom is "new management" until she trusts him. She keeps the key to Mickey's locked office and knows where the real book is; she also hides that she saw Ron arguing with a stranger in the yard two nights before Mickey died. Her story runs through the nightly count, her Sunday question, and Act III, where, if she trusts him, she says where the lie in the books holds.

Ron Kirby. 58, a docker for thirty years until the dock labour scheme ended in 1989, kept on by Mickey for the door and the rank. He calls Tom "boss" and watches him; friendly on top, transactional underneath, he respects strength and cash, in that order. He brings the outfit's asks and stands at Tom's shoulder in a fight. In Act II a rival may try to poach him.

Darren Milner. 25, the hustler who walks Quay Street selling nothing anyone can name. He owes £22 and pays in what he hears; loyal to whoever helped him last, easily bought, easily scared. He is the first to say Tom's night to his face. After week one he has no story of his own.

Ada. A widow at her window on Quay Street (never a teacher, by ruling). She asks Tom to tea once, on the night of the outfit's second ask, and her window sees whether he goes down to the landing that night. Sat with, she would no longer go to the police about him; stood up, she might go to DS Ellis. Her story stops after the tea.

June Suddaby. Mickey's estranged daughter, back for the funeral and still in town, wanting nothing from the office. That is all the current sources hold. A July card made her a night nurse and the story's moral mirror, warming as the business shrinks, with the handing-on ending running through her; whether that stands is not said.

Alison Sedman. The journalist and love interest, drafted in July as Noor Farid: the local paper's port reporter, lodging above Ada's since a month before Mickey died, who will not drop the warehouse fire though her editor wants it dropped. She never burns a source, so true things confided are safe; lies she finds out become the story. She takes his cab in week one and asks about the fire. Her romance is two scenes, neither written.

Philip Danby. The other possible love interest, a teacher by trade, never seen at work because the school stands closed. "Still to write": no card, no scene.

Father Brendan Walsh. The Irish-born parish priest. Tom's first fare goes to his house. The current design gives him street lines and memory, and no story. The July card had him hold Mickey's confessions and the truth of the fire, "not mine to tell, yet".

DS Carol Ellis. The detective, already on the warehouse fire. She comes to Quay Street for a body, a reported serious crime, or, from day four, talk of his nights among his day world. In Act II she makes the street's crimes and the fire one case; in Act III she offers a trade. Her manner is the warning: she asks while the case is answerable and tells once it is not. Whether she can be bought is open; what she wants for herself is not written.

Maureen Jensen, the Widow. Head of the dockside syndicate. The July card made her fifty, running her late husband's business better than he did ("I buried a husband and kept his business. Draw your own conclusions"), keeping every deal. She goes after Tom's people: a tax on his drivers at the dock gate, a man poached. Her eyes include Lee Drewery and Tom's own day driver, Kevin Needham.

Geoffrey Agar. Old money, property and lawyers. The July card made him sixty-one and courtly, a man who apologises before ruining you: "Mickey and I had an understanding once. It died before he did." He goes after Tom's clean money with paper: the private-hire licence, a solicitor's letter, an inspection of the cars. Susan Coverdale is his eye.

Danny Cammack. Head of the new crew, loud and reckless. The July card made him twenty-six, port-born and unwilling to say so, charming and cruel in bursts he calls jokes, the recklessness a costume over calculation. He goes after Tom's cover: trouble on Quay Street that brings heat Tom did not earn. Rita Welburn, who fences from a back room, is his.

Keith Garbutt, the Fixer. Brokers between all three rivals: true reads, a meeting, one truce. The July card (as Hal) took percentages, not sides, and went to Mickey's funeral, "which is more than most".

Also in the story: Pauline Kitching on the radio; the drivers Kevin Needham (days) and Terry Hodgson (nights); the outfit's man at the landing, never named; the other debtors (Wayne Harrison, Lee Drewery, Julie Kettlewell, Dennis Iveson); and the VAT inspector, unnamed.

## 4. The story as it plays

### Act I: The Inheritance

One week, about two hours of play, the most scripted act. Five threads: the outfit's claim, the warehouse fire, Mickey's real book, the street deciding who Tom is, and someone worth being honest for (Alison or Philip). Underneath, never explained: the dock labour scheme ended in July 1989, which is why the debts will not pay and the outfit is half dead.

The first hour, as the current design stages it (real minutes; a game hour passes in 45 seconds):

Monday 15 October 1990, about twenty to four (0:00). The ferry comes in under a grey sky. Tom walks down the gangway with his suitcase and up the quay, and people look.

Quay Street (1:30). Darren on the pavement, friendly and owing. Ron on the rank: "boss". Ada at her window. June, wanting nothing from the office. A line each, in passing: "You'll be Mickey's nephew, then."

Sheila's walk-round (1:45). "New management. Sheila Dunn, I keep the books. Come on, I'll show you round before you start looking lost." The fare book: "Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule, and it's mine now." Takings have been thin since the docks laid men off. At the radio Pauline has a fare and no car.

The first fare (2:30). Car two, to the priest's house by the chapel, Pauline giving the way by landmarks. Dusk falls and the lamps come on.

The rest of the office (4:30). The door Sheila will not open: "That's Mickey's office. It's been locked since he died, and I've the key. Not today." The flat upstairs: "His things are still in it. I didn't have the heart." The debt book: five names, £156. Rent is due Friday.

The first deed (5:30). Darren owes £22 and is standing right there. Front him up, take what he offers, or strike the debt out. Somebody sees.

The first ask (7:00). After dark Ron comes in with a plain dark coat over his arm and an envelope. "Mickey took this down the ferry landing, regular. After ten, to the man who asks for Mickey's. Say no and that's Mickey's arrangement finished, mind; your call." Then: "If you go tonight, boss, wear that. In the dark nobody looks twice at a coat." Tom can go, stay away, or say no, which Ron makes him confirm because it cannot be undone. A no ends the arrangement, never the game. What is in the envelope is never said.

The carry (9:00), the scene a player would retell. The lit quay is quick and seen; the yard and the lane behind the warehouses are dark and slow; the cab is quickest, with the firm's name on its roof. The constable is on his beat; if he calls out, Tom stands and is searched, or runs. The vision's own example of a retold night is this one: walking the envelope down the quay when the constable turned round, cutting through Mickey's yard, and next morning the woman at the window asking what he had been doing in her alley.

The landing (11:30). A man steps up: "Mickey's?" He takes it: "Right. Wednesday, same again." Tom's cut comes back. Home by the yard, the money into the tin, sleep.

Tuesday (13:00). It comes back: Darren says it to his face, in words that fit what Tom did ("Heard you did Mickey's run", "Heard you told them no", "Heard they waited on you at the landing"). The Ledger, Tom's notebook, opens. Then the count with Sheila: how much of the night money the fare book can hide.

Tuesday's day and night (15:00 to 29:00). Fares, two with a wrinkle (a talker about the fish dock, a parcel for Rita's side door), then the cold-store packing shift. Wayne Harrison's £30 at the pawn's back room, in goods or money. Lee Drewery's £15 on the quay: he stands, it comes to blows, and people stop to watch (the design's second retellable moment). At night, Darren's tip for a first break-in, or late fares and a runner.

Wednesday (29:00). If a witness named him for the fight, a constable comes at ten. Ada: "There'll be a pot on at nine tonight, if you want it. I don't ask twice, mind." Alison takes his cab to the warehouse row and asks about the fire on the way. Julie Kettlewell cannot pay her £9: mercy or shame.

Wednesday night (39:00), the two lives. Ron brings the second envelope at eight; Ada's pot is on at nine. Sitting with her till half past ten leaves the landing only just reachable before it closes at one, and from her window she sees whether he goes. Her telling of it is what most surely brings Ellis.

Thursday (45:00). Both nights come back, and somebody calls him "Nowak". Dennis Iveson owes £80 and has it: Tom can front up his yard hand, come back for the hut at night, or lean on what Iveson bought out of the burned warehouse. If the street is loud, DS Ellis is on Quay Street, asking. After dark two men from the docks wait by car two, for Lee's sake: back off, run through the yard, or have Ron there. Tomorrow is rent day.

The rest of the week. The asks fall on nights one, three, five and seven. On Sunday Sheila comes in on her day off: "So which is it going to be?" Wind it down (which ends Mickey's arrangement), take it over, or refuse to say. The street learns the answer. Nothing else from Friday to Sunday is written.

### Act II: The Rise and the Squeeze

About seven game weeks, 11 to 14 hours. Beats fire on what has happened, never on dates; the act opens "when the business has grown". The firm climbs: the firm is his (a third car, a first recruit, the locked office and the real book once Sheila trusts him); the Hook's firm (five cars, a night desk, a first front, the cold-store shift contract); the port's man (men at the dock gate, the cold store and the landing, a brief on call, people stepping aside).

Each rival climbs the same ladder against it, never skipping a rung, each rung said to his face or shown on his street: noticed, a visit, pressure, a threat, a sit-down. Every pressure has three answers: pay, defy, or counter with what he knows.

- The Widow taxes his drivers at the dock gate and poaches a man; the first she hears of Tom may come from his own day driver, over a radio anyone can overhear.
- Agar sends paper: the licence, a solicitor's letter, an inspection of the cars.
- Cammack makes noise on Quay Street, and the heat falls on Tom.
- Garbutt sells true reads, a meeting, one truce.
- Ellis makes the street's crimes and the fire one case, and may search the office once, with notice the evening before.
- Guns come in only through people, and the town remembers who brought them. A firm of fists is a real way to play.

One collision is guaranteed: an evening of his day life interrupted by his night life at the door, so that both worlds see each other. The act ends at a sit-down with one rival head: accept, defy, or counter.

A proposal, to be judged when written: the men behind the landing are the last of an old firm. If Tom kept the run, they hand him their end of the Hook and fade, and he becomes what Mickey was; if he ended it, the rivals come sooner.

The sources give the shape of Act II's pressures, not a single scene of it.

### Act III: The Ledger Comes Due

One week, two to three hours. A courteous letter names a date: a VAT inspection by Customs and Excise. The fare book has been lying since day one: wash too little through it and the night money has no source; too much, and a cab office on this street earned more than it could. The inspector brings one item each morning, and Tom produces it or asks for it in writing. Each morning is graded by what the books hold. Sheila, if she trusts him, says where the lie holds. Ellis offers a trade: give her a rival, at the price that the street learns who talks; refusing can still reach the best ending, but only through what the town believes. Someone in his crew may ask to take the firm over. On the last day he can reach only a few people, and whoever picks up is the campaign he played. Then the books open.

## 5. The three shown endings

Endings are states the town is in when the inspection closes, never picked from a list; no single beat can fail the run. Five states are built underneath (Quiet, Both, the Straight Life, the Kingdom, Burn Both), and when several fit, the first in that order wins. The design shows three, each with a better and a plainer turn.

Every deciding cost shows its sign first. Tom's reading in the Ledger is built only from those signs ("The inspector wants the other book. Ron would still stand by me. The police have been at the door."), and says "quiet" only when everything is. The ending plays as a short close in the town (who is where, who calls him what), then the credits, then the town stays open to walk. No last scene is written for any of the three.

The Kingdom.
- Reached: the business survives and the books hold.
- Plainer turn: he keeps what he built and finds its people civil and no more.
- Better turn (the built Both): one friend still counts him, the street does not hold his rackets as fact, Ellis's case is answerable, and the books both hold and make sense. Rare; meant not to come on a first playthrough.
- Cost: the life. Without that friend he has the port and nobody in it.
- Signs: the inspector stays courteous; Ellis still asks rather than tells; Sheila has given no warning; no telling about him is overheard as fact.

The Straight Life.
- Reached: no business at the close, never built or sold up, and one friendship holds.
- Better turn (the built Quiet): he hands the firm to someone who can hold it and walks away clean, never while hunted for a killing; three days of watching show whether it holds.
- Cost: everything he could have built.
- Signs: a friend's act nobody asked for (a warning in private, a door held, an errand done), and the successor's offer.

Burn Both.
- Reached: none of the others fit; what doing nothing produces, and where a hunted man can end.
- Cost: the business and the people.
- Signs: the inspector's "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak"; Ellis's "We both know where you were"; the police's stages (her name in the street's talk, an officer at his door, a car on the street and his description said aloud); a remark that stops as he passes; the successor's "I can't take it on now".

An older balance run (July, marked not current) found that answering the inspector every morning saves only what was nearly saveable, and that for a player who never built a business the inspection changes nothing: the third act is scenery for that player.

## 6. What the story leaves to play

- Side stories: 25 to 40, found on foot, mostly light or grey, three to five steps each, each ending in something the town knows. The 32 drafts of 8 November are the list.
- Fares with something going on: each a named person on their routine (Brenda Laverack to the fish dock at dawn, the packing shift at half past five, a lodger for the last ferry). About half of Act I's carry a wrinkle: a talker who says something true about somebody; a parcel, "no questions"; a tail; a runner; "keep the engine running"; the last ferry in four minutes; somebody who matters getting in. A rival may hold a sit-down in the back of his cab.
- The debt book's five lessons: Darren pays in news, Wayne in goods, Lee stands with the docks behind him, Julie cannot pay, Iveson has three ways in.
- Friendships: calling on a friend or the love interest when their routine allows, a short scene with no score; too soon again gets a cooler door. The friendship that decides an ending is built from these visits and the honesty in them.
- Recruits, by meeting a need (slow, holds) or spending a hook (quick, brittle): Julie Kettlewell, who can drive; Ian Megginson, who knows every lock on the street; Lee Drewery's fists; Norman Dent's nights on the warehouse row; Karen Grummitt's view of the ferry; Shaun Hewson's paint. Each man sent is another who knows.
- Street happenings: a row on a doorstep, a shoplifter running from the grocer, a dog loose on the quay; a stranger helped can return the favour.
- Hurt and help: if his cab knocks somebody down, stop and help or drive off and be chased; beaten, Tom comes round on the pavement, in Grace Rinaldi's kitchen or in hospital; a retired doctor by the quay stitches without asking, and remembers.
- A boxing club in the old hall behind the chandler's, gloves on, no stakes.

## 7. Gaps and contradictions

Old names in the July files (Lena, Rocco, Sam, Noor, Aldous Vane, Sera Kest, Danny Ro, Hal, Father Emil) are read as their canon names.

### Where the sources are thin

1. Tom has no want, fear, flaw or arc. His past is unsaid by canon, he is unvoiced, and every ending describes his position, not who he has become.
2. Act II has no written scene: a ladder per rival, a guaranteed collision with no evening chosen and nobody named at the door, and a closing sit-down with "one head", unnamed. The old firm fading is a proposal. What Act II is for a player who winds the firm down is not written.
3. The warehouse fire, the founding mystery, has no content: when, whose, whether anyone was hurt, what the street says, what is true. A ruling of 30 September accepts it "as drafted", but no draft is among these sources, and the note of 29 September says the game has no fire yet. Alison's question, Ellis's case, the real book, Iveson's goods and Father Walsh's secret all hang from it.
4. The outfit has no face: the landing man is unnamed, with a dozen fixed lines; who they are and what Mickey's arrangement was are never said.
5. Mickey's real book: beyond one page about the fire, its contents and what showing it changes are unwritten.
6. No ending has a last scene, only "who is where, who calls him what".
7. Act III is a structure: the inspector is unnamed (the July log calls him Tobias Reese), his items are not listed, the crewman who asks to take over is unnamed, and the last day's calls are unwritten. The Core's Act III text still belongs to an older version of Mickey's and is owed a rewrite.
8. Philip Danby is "still to write", so the love-interest thread has one candidate, and Alison's two romance scenes are unwritten.
9. June's arc lives only in a July card, partly contradicted since; Father Walsh has lines but no story; Ada's story ends with the tea and Darren's with week one.
10. Ellis has no personal want or history, and whether she can be bought is open.
11. The rivals' characters come only from July cards written for an older version (a different business, a "Hook Street", "the Strip"); none has a scene, and no sit-down has words.
12. Sheila saw Ron argue with a stranger two nights before Mickey died, and Ron found the body; nothing follows it up, and the ruling says nobody doubts the heart.
13. Act I's Friday and Saturday have no scenes, and what the arrangement does if the constable takes the envelope is not written.
14. Mickey's two approved lines and the 32 side-story drafts are named but not among these sources.

### Where the sources disagree, and which was followed

1. How many endings: the 28 October ruling says two or three; the approved outline of 28 September and the built story have five. Open. Followed DESIGN: three shown, five beneath.
2. The opening: the approved first hour of 29 September has Sheila meet him at the office; DESIGN and VISION open on the ferry with a fare at 2:30 and the debt book at 4:30. Open. Followed DESIGN.
3. Whose outfit: canon says Mickey left Tom "a half-dead criminal outfit"; the first-ask note reads it as somebody else's outfit Mickey served, still awaiting the owner's confirmation; DESIGN proposes the last of an old firm. Followed the note and DESIGN, as unconfirmed.
4. June and the funeral: the July card says she stayed away; the outline says she came and stayed on. Followed the outline.
5. The real book: the outline ends the week with Sheila "over the real book"; DESIGN opens it only on her trust. Followed DESIGN, keeping the Sunday question.
6. Quiet: DESIGN files it as the Straight Life's better turn, which needs a friendship; the outline's Quiet needs none and outranks every state. Followed DESIGN's names.
7. Who is poached: the outline names Ron; DESIGN says his least loyal man. Followed DESIGN.
8. The Fixer: canon renamed him Keith Garbutt, yet DESIGN still lists a "Hal" with a shop, apparently someone else. Used Garbutt; Hal left out.
9. Waking after a beating: July has Tom wake to June at the hospital; DESIGN has the pavement, Grace Rinaldi's kitchen or hospital. Followed DESIGN.
10. Father Walsh: July makes confession a way to get ahead of a story; canon says religion is never a mechanic. Followed canon.
11. Police after a witnessed killing: the built game goes straight to a manhunt; the ending-signs note proposes a day of investigating first. Open; DESIGN follows the proposal for now. The owner's answer on the ending signs was never stored.
12. Sheila and his name: the 29 September first hour had everyone he met use it; canon's ruling that day makes Sheila withhold it. Followed canon.
13. Act III's length: one week in DESIGN, six mornings in the July log. Followed DESIGN.
