**Verdict: NO, at about 65% confidence.** Built competently inside its frame, I expect a very good small story that people love. I do not expect it to stand at or above the best of the three shipped stories as something players live through.

The bible's relationship craft and its planting and payoff are better than all three shipped pages. But its centre depends on three things a shipped story at that level cannot afford:
- A lead who is a hole by design, whose flaw is expressed by not pressing a button.
- A whodunit turn that a genre-literate player will solve by week two.
- Stakes that peak at a bookkeeper charged over a theft in which nothing was taken.

**What I would bet goes wrong. Faults of the story itself:**
1. **The flaw inverts in play.** Players take what is offered and press every prompt. The default player is therefore a taker and an asker, so "you never ask for owt" never becomes theirs, and the wall scene loses its weight.
2. **The turn is guessed.** The player is never made to commit to Ron in the game, so "being sure, and being wrong" happens only in the player's head, if at all.
3. **Low loss at the centre.** Nobody dies, nothing is taken, and the grave scenes are quiet conversations (kitchen, corridor). It is admirable, and it will read as small next to a story whose lead dies at sunrise.
4. **Contrivances players will name:**
   - Sheila writes a confession into a fare book and keeps it on the shelf behind her for a year.
   - The routine VAT inspector happens to belong to the department that was robbed.
   - The watchman's account is held back for a year.
5. **Density.** The cast is too big for the hours, with dialect and look-alike old dockers (Tapper, Shiner, the Bishop). The truth hangs on a minor man and a brass tally.
6. **The endings are decided by hidden ledgers** ("nothing marks which is which"), plus a skill race up the hill. Players will feel the outcome as arbitrary and reload.

**Things that can only be judged once built:**
1. **The free-typed fare line** ("the game takes what he puts"; she rules through untrue lines) needs reliable checking of free text against the player's own day. It will feel arbitrary if it misfires.
2. **Precision of the key lines.** Turns depend on exact wording ("I don't drive" against "I can't"). If talk is generated live, that precision dies.
3. **Comedy built on hands and timing:** the gull, the arm-steering, Shiner under orders not to speak.
4. **Ledger-reading as gameplay.** Mileage columns and a count by coin risk feeling like paperwork, not hands-first play.
5. **The tail and the timed race** must not feel like standard tailing and racing missions, and must not fail.
6. **The repeating week** (fares, dusk job, count, Friday round) may sag if the systems do not vary it.

# C. Six changes of shape, most important first

1. **Make the player commit to the wrong man, at a cost.** Before the 18th, give one playable act built on the Ron theory. For example: write "R. Kirby" in the fare book's open line, give his name to Ellis or Reese, or take car two's keys off him at the gate. Allow accusing Sheila too: right, but she stops the count and the pen. The turn then lands on something the player did, Ron or Sheila hears of it, and guessing early becomes a choice with a price instead of a spoiled surprise. *Answers T1, H1, S2, and risk 2.*

2. **Cut the tells that give Sheila away, and move the surprise onto Ron.** Keep two fair clues (the seat, "I don't drive"). Drop "nobody has seen her in a car" as a stated trait, and drop "never car two". Hold back Ron's reason (learning so it is never her again; the coat to silence Shiner) until the morning of the 18th. That reason is the reframe players cannot guess. *Answers T1 and S5.*

3. **Make Tom's flaw the default, and make the deed a played scene.** Ignored offers default to paying. The Ledger shows the running account plainly ("Paid for the tea again"), and taking has a visible early price (Danny's fifty read out at the hearing). Replace the "one press" across the road with a walk along the wall: man by man, each one's earlier turn recalled, some refusing. The change becomes lived, not pressed. *Answers C1, X1, and risks 1 and 6.*

4. **Raise the cost at the centre: let the Bishop die.** He dies in the week of the letter, after he has given June his account. His funeral, with the street and the envelopes, becomes the grave scene. The paper now names a death, Sheila's choice to write her name gets heavier, and Ron's lie has a real reason. This sits inside the frame: no killing by the player, no children. *Answers H1 and risk 3.*

5. **Cut and bring forward the cast.** Bring the people who matter down from 21 to about 14: fold Alison into Ellis's thread; fold Darren's function into Danny's; cut Julie's and Karen's plot weight. Put the Bishop on screen in week one on the Friday round, at the Ropery Lane door, so that the Remembrance scene and the tally pay off a face, not a name. *Answers X2 and risk 5.*

6. **Take skill and hidden tallies out of the ending decision.** Decide the seat on readable, story-level choices made in the weeks: whose name, the pen, who will get up. Make the race up the hill change the tone inside an ending, not flip it to another. Show the player, in the Ledger, the debts and turns that will decide who stands up. Fold Friday's three timed beats into one driven sequence whose failure branches are authored. *Answers E1, X4, and risk 6.*
