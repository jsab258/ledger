# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 1: a critic's review

Parts of this are as good as anything I have read in a game bible. It is not yet a story I would send people to play for its own sake, and the reasons are specific and fixable.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7 out of 10.

1. Tom: 7. The want (his own name, said by people who mean it), the flaw (he takes nothing and asks nothing) and the fear (the suitcase) all lock together, and his dry Ledger gives an unvoiced man a real voice. But in play his flaw comes down to a take-or-pay button, so he is a man who reacts more than one who drives the story.

2. The cast: 8. Sheila, Ada, Pauline, Terry and Reese are each somebody within a line, and Ron and Tom are the relationship people would talk about afterwards. Julie, Kevin and June are thinner than their billing, and twenty-one people who matter plus regulars is a lot to carry in sixteen hours.

3. The antagonists: 6. The Widow is excellent: lawful, honest, right about half of it, and dangerous because of that. Danny gets one great scene and Agar gets letters and one speech, which is the exact GTA V complaint BAR names.

4. The central mystery: 7. The truth really does change earlier scenes: the lessons, "Mind that wing", three of number nine, Ron getting into the back seat. But the clues come so thick that most players will have guessed Ron drove by the second week, so the reveal rests on the quieter turn that nobody threatened anybody.

5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The ferry, the walk-round, the priest's empty envelopes and the carry at minute nine make a strong first quarter hour, and the failed carry is well thought through. Act I is still two hours of the most scripted part of the game, and that is a risk.

6. Structure and pace: 6. Act II runs nine and a half hours and Act III two and a half. The last act has to hold Reese's week, Sheila's kitchen, the phone-box round, the counter and the last drive, and it reads as hurried, which BAR says players punish.

7. The middle: 7. The Empty Seat is the best-planned middle I have seen in a bible, with comedy placed where the ache is. But the routine around the scenes (fares, a job at dusk, the count, the Friday round) repeats about seven times and is barely described.

8. Surprise against cliché: 7. Turning down the hidden boss, the guilty uncle and the arsonist landlord is a real achievement, and "the secret is in the comic relief" is a good line. The blackmail that turns out not to be blackmail is clever, but it surprises the reader of the bible more than it will startle someone playing.

9. The endings: 8. Every close has a last scene, a last image and two written lines, and "which seat Ron is in" is a verdict a player can read without a word. The last input being one the player can choose not to press is the best single idea in the document.

10. The side stories: 6. Ivy Leadley is a real story with a turn I did not see coming, and the watch is good. Karen is light, and the umbrella is really main-line material, so that leaves two whole side stories for a sixteen-hour game.

11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline's radio, Terry's fifth word ("Tell Pauline it don't count"), the ham with its own seat and the crabs are actually funny. The noir side is played straight and quiet, which is right. But the humour is gentle throughout and never surprises the way the best set pieces in KCD2 do.

12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The handbrake, the passenger seat, the fare book count, the carry and the round of doors are all story told through what the player does with his hands. The midpoint loss is the exception, and I come to it below.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The climax's paperwork does not hold up, and the "T. Nowak" route leans on canon's silence as a trick. Where: section 0 item 10; Act III, Thursday and Five o'clock; the Straight Life's better turn. What is wrong: a Customs officer accepts a name he can see is invented, Tom comes down the station steps on bail for a reason nobody gives, and a man who drives cabs for a living signs that he drove a car unlicensed. The route only works because the game has never said where Tom was, which makes players think hard about the one thing the frame says they should stop noticing. Why a player would feel it: the biggest choice in the game hangs on a loophole, and the writer's hand shows just when the story must feel inevitable. Fix: replace the false name with a true sacrifice. Tom signs as proprietor that he will not name the driver and that the firm accepts the night as a working shift and pays the assessment. That costs him the firm, keeps the Straight Life's shape, keeps Ron's line about never being asked, and keeps Ellis's remark on the steps, without a lie and without using the past as a key.

2. The midpoint is on rails. Where: scene 7 to the Fifth, and "If the player found it out first". What is wrong: whatever the player does, Ron goes to the gate in her coat. Even the player who uncovers everything only gets to plan the defection with him, not stop it. Why a player would feel it: BAR asks for nothing on rails, and the most watchful player, the one this bible rewards everywhere else, finds the story's central loss was fixed before he started. Fix: give that player a third way. If he has asked Ron what he wants before the Fifth, Ron can refuse the Widow. She takes Tapper as foreman a month early, the gate's custom and the packing shift go, and Shiner's story starts travelling the street through Darren. The window, the effigy and the empty office door all still happen; what the player loses is the business, not the friend. The ache stays, but it has been chosen.

3. The theme is hammered. Where: Kevin in scene 3, Alison in scene 17, the Widow in scenes 13 and 14, Ada, Ron's last line, Ellis on the steps, section 2. What is wrong: "Nobody ever asked me what I wanted" and its variations come from nearly every major character, so by the third movement the cast are speaking one idea in turn. Why a player would feel it: by the third speaker he stops believing them as people and sees a writer making a point. Fix: keep the line said aloud for Kevin at the start and Ron at the end, and let everyone else show it in action. Give one person the opposite fault for contrast: Danny, who asks everyone everything and is trusted by nobody, or Reese, who is paid to ask and is the only one who gets straight answers.

4. Danny and Agar fade out, and Act III has no real threat from the antagonists. Where: section 5; scenes 10, 16 and 18; Act III's week; the endings. What is wrong: Agar's loan due on 31 December and the right of way to the old basin are set up and never paid off. Danny ends on "Fair enough". In the last week each opponent "moves once", which is how a list moves, not a story. Why a player would feel it: two of the three who pressed on Tom just stand by. Fix: make Agar's deadline a live clock in Act III. If Tom took the solicitor, Agar's man holds a lien on the yard at the close and appears in two endings. Give Danny a last shot in every close: he buys the roof sign in the Suitcase, and in the Kingdom he is the junior partner Tom never asked for. Let the Widow make one move of her own in the last week, still lawful and still without a lie.

5. The weeks between the scenes risk becoming the same loop. Where: section 7, "What a week is", and Ron's unfinished stories on every drive. What is wrong: there are eighteen scenes across nine and a half hours, and between them is a routine of fares, a job at dusk, the count and the Friday round that is barely specified. The unfinished story is a lovely device used too often. Why a player would feel it: it is KCD2's middle complaint and RDR2's slow stretch at once; the fifth Friday round is a chore. Fix: make every Friday visibly different. The round shrinks door by door (twenty-one, twenty, nineteen), each Friday brings a new face or a new refusal, and the count carries a running stake the Ledger tracks. Cap Ron's unfinished stories at five, each cut off later than the last, so the one he finishes at the bollard is the end of a pattern the player has been counting.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review?

No. Not yet. I would single out Ron and Tom, the handbrake and the two lines in the fare book; those are reasons to play. But the climax rests on a legal trick, the central loss is forced, two of the three opponents are scaffolding, and the theme is spoken aloud too often. This bible has the ending and not yet the rest. Fix the five points above and I would say yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron in the passenger seat, the lessons and the handbrake. "I go, you stop" turned inside out across three lessons, a reveal at the bollard, and a last input the player can choose not to press: this is the Henry and Hans of the game, told through the controls rather than through cutscenes.

2. The Widow who never lies, and the reveal that nobody threatened anybody. An opponent who keeps every deal and tells the truth when asked, and a disaster made by a year of good mornings, is the most original thing here. Do not let anyone add a hidden crime to her.

3. The fare book. "Every fare goes in the book" as alibi and noose, the two lines from November 1989, and Sheila's "15.10. Ferry landing. One. No charge." coming back as the Suitcase's last line. With Sheila as written and her two steps from "Mr Nowak" to "Tom", it is the spine that holds the rest together.
