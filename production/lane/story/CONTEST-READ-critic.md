# Contest reading: the critic

20 November 2026. A fresh Opus reader (a critic whose favourite game stories are KCD2 and RDR2), given BAR.md and the three treatments under shuffled labels and nothing else. For this reader: X was CONTEST-B (No Charge), Y was CONTEST-C (The Passenger), Z was CONTEST-A (Twice). The reader never saw the letters A, B, C. The text below is the reader's own.

## 1. Ranking

First, Y (The Passenger). It is the only one of the three built on a friendship that could be remembered the way Henry and Hans are, and its turn makes you replay every funny scene you have already enjoyed.

Second, X (No Charge). It has the best-planted mystery and the most hands-on midpoint, but it ends with the town filling the counter to save the hero, which is It's a Wonderful Life.

Third, Z (Twice). It is the boldest structure, with the crime handed to the player as an ordinary fare, but it gives away its mystery in week one and leans on a complicity trick that games criticism has seen before.

## 3. Each treatment

X, No Charge

Best things:
- The count (section 7). The player sits alone with the fare book and his own Ledger and reads "Rennison. Account. No charge." against "The ham had a seat. £2." That is a reveal done with the hands, set up hours earlier by a joke fare. It is the finest single beat in the three treatments.
- The turn reaches back a long way. The nineteen pounds, the £80 she knew without being told, "New management", and the wage packet going into her bag every Friday all change meaning. Ada's line in scene 12, "A club is a thing you're in. A charity is a thing done to you", turns Tom's flaw into the whole Hook's flaw. That is real thematic work.

Worst faults:
- The ending (section 9, Friday at five to five). Forty-one envelopes on the counter, the priest with the plate, the townsfolk paying the hero's debt while the villain watches from the door. That is the most familiar last scene in popular fiction, and BAR punishes the familiar.
- Sheila's kitchen speech (section 7) explains the whole scheme in one paragraph of telling. Act II's second half then piles a second solved mystery on top: the paraffin heater and the scorched wall in scene 13. "Nobody did it, it was an accident" is a deflating twist of its own, and it crowds a five-hour stretch that already carries Ada's refusal, Danny, Agar and the staged job.

Y, The Passenger

Best things:
- Ron, and the lessons. "I can drive, boss. Near enough" (scene 13) turns every Sunday comedy into confession without spoiling it in hindsight. The wing, the bollard, "Not down there, boss. Surface is bad" and the stories he never finishes are fair, played and funny before they are sad. This is the relationship people would talk about afterwards.
- The last input in the game is the handbrake, from the passenger seat. It is a last image and a last act of play in one, set up in the first lesson. The Widow is the best antagonist of the three: lawful, keeps every deal, often right, and she wins by asking the question Tom never asks. BAR asks for exactly that kind of motive.

Worst faults:
- The gate hut (scene 12). The Widow keeps a written book that says "Consideration: his silence and mine, 18.11.89, car two". A careful woman would never write that down. It is a villain's ledger put there for the player to find by torch, and it is the weakest link in an otherwise fair chain.
- The midpoint is announced in scene 6. Once Ron says "She's offered me the gate, boss", the player can see the Fifth coming. Most of what follows is then four and a half hours played alone with the best thing in the game gone, which risks the slow middle BAR warns about. Separately, the "T. Nowak" box in Act III has Reese all but asking where Tom was a year ago ("You came to this town in October"). That leans on the rule that his past is unsaid. It needs care to stay inside the frame.

Z, Twice

Best things:
- The same fare booked three times: Mickey's in 1989, the player's on the Fifth, and again on 15 December. It is the cleanest structure here, and the last night is played rather than watched. Sheila's virtue becomes the thing that records the crime, and she withholds every name, then says "Mr Nowak" for the first time at the shelf. That develops the character instead of just keeping her.
- The hearing's public bench (scene 5) is a gift the player was never offered a choice about. Agar's line in scene 17, "Your uncle's friends light them for nothing, and I buy what is left", is the sharpest rival line in any of the three.

Worst faults:
- There is no mystery (section 5, item 6, and the week-one summary, "What the player knows: all of it"). Cyril tells him about the fire on the first Sunday. The 1989 line in scene 13 only confirms what the player already suspects. BAR asks for a truth that changes what earlier scenes meant, and this one changes very little.
- The midpoint relies on a trap. Section 7 calls the lock-up burning with Darren inside "the common first playthrough". Tricking the player into an atrocity through an ordinary job is a known move, and if most players fall into it the result is the "sad for no reason, could not see it coming" ending BAR punishes. Cyril is a passive opponent who "never does anything himself", and Coultas is barely a name, so the antagonists are thin.

## 4. Would I single one out?

Not yet. Y comes nearest: on a good day I would mention it, but I would not call it a reason to buy the game. To get there it still needs:
- a find in the second half that does not depend on the villain writing down her own secret;
- a midpoint the player does not see coming from scene 6;
- something genuinely funny in the stretch without Ron, so the empty seat aches rather than drags.

## 5. What Y could take from the other two

From X:
- The staged "men over the water" job: borrowed car coats, the ticket clerk who must not be on, Shiner who must not speak. Y already has Tapper and Shiner, and this is the comedy its empty-seat stretch lacks.
- The count, played with the fare book and the Ledger side by side. It could replace the gate-hut book as the way the player finds what the Widow bought.
- Ada's club-not-charity refusal, which fits Y's flaw about never asking or taking.
- Sheila's line entering Tom on his first day ("15.10. Ferry landing. One. No charge."), shown to him in her kitchen on the Thursday.

From Z:
- Sheila calling him nothing at all after the Fifth, until he reads the line himself.
- Sheila in the customer's chair on the wrong side of her own counter.
- The full public bench at the hearing, unasked.
- Agar's "I buy what is left" walk in the rain.
- Ron's "That's all I'll say in a car with a radio in it", which Y's Ron could say on the second lesson.
