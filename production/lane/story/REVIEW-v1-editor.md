# LEDGER story bible, version 1: script editor's note

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7

1. Tom: 6. The want (a place where his name is meant) and the fear (the suitcase) are well chosen and carried by objects, which suits an unvoiced lead. But his line is a wall the game builds, not a choice he makes, and in most big scenes he is a man things are done to: told, offered, read aloud at a hearing.
2. The cast: 8. Sheila, Ron, Ada, Cyril and Reese are specific people with secrets that bite, and the list of forty-one turns minor faces into stakes. June and Philip are thinner, more function than person.
3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is the best thing in the rival set: she prices, never threatens, and her history with Ada is real. Cammack's grudge resting on a false belief is a good engine. Agar is the stock 1990 marina gentleman and fights mostly by post.
4. The central mystery: 5. All three of its questions (whose table, what happened at the warehouse, why him) are answered by the midpoint, and the climax turns on a line the player has known about since week one.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. Eleven minutes from ferry to landing, every beat planted, and the failed carry ending with "Nowak, T. £82" in the debt book is a fine piece of game writing. The tea as a retold scene only works for a player who did not guess the twist.
6. Structure and pace: 7. Calendar days pulling a slow player forward is the right answer for a game with no rails, and the rival peaks taken in turn give the act a shape. Act III's mornings and nights rhythm is strong.
7. The middle: 6. The second half has real set pieces, but the keeper's road rise, which is most of the hours, is named rather than told ("a first front, a first round").
8. Surprise against cliché: 5. The kindly widow at the window who turns out to run it all is a familiar turn, and the bible plants it so heavily that many players will call it in the first hour.
9. The endings: 7. Each has a last scene, a named judge and an image in the fare book; "Ferry landing. One. No charge." is excellent. But they settle exposure, not truth, and the Reckoning is thin.
10. The side stories: 7. The watch, the wardrobe and the mooring are proper stories with turns. Norman's turn is a non-turn, and the camcorder exists to serve the plot.
11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline on the radio is a real running voice and Reese's legal ride-along is a genuinely funny idea. The wedding car and hearse mix-up is an old farce gag, and the haddock line is weak.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 7. Almost every beat is a fare, a carry or a count, and talk is where consequence lands. Some of the most important beats ask a lot of systems not yet built: testimony drawn from memory, two passengers talking, five phone calls.

## 2. The five weakest points

### 1. The mystery is spent at the midpoint, so the climax reveals nothing

What is wrong. On the Fifth Tom reads the fire page and Ada tells him the whole history. After that nothing true is left to find. Act III is about hiding what is known, and Thursday's high point ("Somebody drove a long way for nothing") is a line Alison flagged on the first Wednesday.

Where. Sections 3, 8 (the midpoint), 9 (Thursday).

Why it matters. The climax pays off a fact, not a revelation: the last hours have suspense but no discovery, and that is when players decide if a story was special.

Fix. The bible already holds the answer and hides it: "That sentence has a second meaning." Ada does not know about the £4,000. Stage it as the late truth. Mickey had promised the importer's plot to Agar before Ada ever said "lesson"; her word was the cover he wanted, and the fire paid his debt, not the forty-one. Keep it out of Ada's second tea and out of the readable pages until Act III. Reese's Wednesday item (the 1988 to 1990 accounts) shows Agar's loan going in and the interest stopping the quarter of the fire. The two widows in the minicab turn on it: Ada learns she was used. It recolours the second tea and the letter, and hands Tom something only he can choose to tell.

### 2. Ada as the head of the firm is the guessable twist, and it is over-signposted

What is wrong. The watcher at the window, the single invitation, the tea against the landing, "What were you doing in my alley?", Ron in her ginnel at dusk, the reporter in her upstairs room: every one points the same way. Genre players know the old lady who watches everything.

Where. Section 0 change 1; section 7 (Tuesday to Thursday plants); section 8 (the midpoint).

Why it matters. If the player called it at minute forty, the two figures at the glass are a confirmation and the retold tea is not retold.

Fix. Give the player a strong false reading. Put Ada's own name in the Friday round: Ron knocks at her front door and hands Walter Jensen's widow an envelope like any other, and she thanks him. She is, on the surface, a recipient, the forty-second name. Let Cyril seem the head, and let Ron's dusk walk point at Cyril's lodging in the same ginnel. The midpoint then flips something the player believed.

### 3. Agar's ladder breaks on its own logic, and he has no scene that is his

What is wrong. Agar's last rung is a call to Customs and his named target is the 1989 accounts. Those accounts carry the importer's fake account work, which leads to the plot Agar bought for a third of its worth, which makes him an accessory to arson. He would be ringing the state about the one year that can ruin him. His other moves are letters and a solicitor. His only face-to-face scenes are in the back of Tom's cab, the same staging as the Widow's.

Where. Section 6 (Agar), section 9 (why the inspector comes, cause 3).

Why it matters. A cornered man doing something that hurts himself, with no scene showing him cornered, reads as plot convenience.

Fix. Agar's call names Tom's 1990 takings and the dead man's badge, nothing earlier. Then give him one scene not in a cab and not by letter: his bank's man in his own office, with Tom kept waiting outside a glass door and hearing the 31 December deadline said aloud, so the player sees the ruin coming before Agar prices it.

### 4. Tom's line is never tested because it is never offered, and the friendship the game needs is underwritten

What is wrong. "The game never offers the blow." That keeps the frame, but a line no one can cross costs nothing, so the two tests in sections 4 and 8 are tests of the game, not of Tom. Meanwhile the obvious lasting bond, Tom and Ron, is written as a loyalty check (will he go to the Widow?) with few scenes of the two simply together.

Where. Section 4 (his line); section 5 (Ron); section 8 scene 10.

Why it matters. Sheila is a judge, not a companion. Without a companion and a line that costs, Tom stays a cursor with a name.

Fix. Keep the blow off the table and offer it to somebody else. At the dock gate, Ron says quietly, "Say the word, boss." With Ada in Act III, Cammack offers to do it for nothing. Tom's line becomes whether he lets another man do what he will not, which is a real choice the town can remember. Then give Ron the passenger seat: a recurring short drive on the Friday round where the two of them talk, building through the watch and the supper to scene 10, so that losing him hurts.

### 5. The keeper's road rise is abstract, and it is most of the game

What is wrong. The keeper's road middle is described as "rung 2", "the fourth rung", "a first front, a first round, the cold-store contract", "rounds and fronts that have nothing of a club left in them". The rackets themselves have no people, no turns and no cost to a named member of the forty-one.

Where. Section 8, first half, and the paragraph after scene 8; section 10 (the Kingdom).

Why it matters. Eleven to fourteen hours sit here, where the same-loop complaint lives, and the Kingdom's "one thing of the kind Mickey swore was his last" needs to have been a scene.

Fix. Name three rackets, each with a victim who is on the list or kin to someone on it, each with a turn: the cold-store contract that puts Julie's laundry hampers out of work; a "rent" on the parade that Lily Tang pays and Danny then claims credit for protecting; a front in the closed school's yard that costs Philip the building. Each should make the club less of a club in a way a player can see on one face.

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. The arithmetic of the landing. If £15 is "a tenth of what comes back", the far bank returns £150 a week, nearly all of the £164, and the club has no money problem. Fix: the office's tenth is £1.50, and the £15 is a flat carrier's fee paid out of Ada's half.
2. Thursday 22 November 1990 is the day Mrs Thatcher announced her resignation. Anyone who lived through it will notice a licensing hearing that morning with nobody mentioning it. Fix: Pauline says it on air and the public bench is half-empty; it costs nothing and it is the year.
3. June "came to the funeral and stayed" yet is a night sister at the infirmary three weeks later, and Mickey's letters went to "an address she had left". Fix: she has nursed there for two years, living across the water.
4. Returned letters would come back to the sender's address, not wait at the ferry landing. Fix: Mickey gave them to Gordon Leckenby to post from the far bank, so June would not see a Meridian postmark; the "not known" returns came to Gordon.
5. Cammack tells Customs about "a cab office that pays forty-one pensioners", but Danny does not know his father is paid. Fix: Cammack's call names only what his lads saw on the quay: a cab firm's man carrying cash to the landing at night.
6. Ellis "sends notice" that she will search the office tomorrow. A detective does not warn a suspect. Fix: the constable who can be bought tells Tom, which also gives that constable a scene.
7. Driving on a dead man's badge from the first afternoon and keeping the operator's licence of a dead man would be an offence, not a review, under the 1976 Act. Fix: the hearing is Tom's own application for operator and driver licences, which Agar's solicitor objects to.
8. A Customs officer would not take original records to his room at a boarding house overnight. Fix: the books are locked in the boot of his car outside Mrs Rennison's.
9. "The estate is liable for tax on account work that was never driven" is muddled: rent written up as fares was declared and taxed. The danger is exposure, not a bill. Fix: Reese's problem is real money from an unreal source.
10. The watchman's absence is pure luck in a planned arson. Fix: Mickey timed the run to Norman's break.
11. Les Coultas is set up in the yard, at the landing and on Thursday night, then has no outcome. Fix: one line in every ending saying where Les is.
12. Cyril gave Ada his tally of Alan's thefts the week before Alan drowned. It hints that Ada had a hand in her son's death and is never paid off. Fix: either cut the timing or make it the question Maureen asks Ada in the minicab.

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review

No. It is well made, period-specific and better than most games manage, with things I would quote. But a reviewer singles out a story for a turn they did not see coming or an ending that reframes the hours before it. Here the big turn is guessable, the truth is in the player's hands by the midpoint, and the longest stretch of the game is described rather than written. Fix the first, second and fifth points above and it becomes a yes.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. The fare book and Mr Reese. "Every fare goes in the book" planted at minute two and read back by a mild man with a propelling pencil, ending in "Ferry landing. One. No charge." That is a climax nobody else has.
2. Sheila Dunn and the ladder of names: "Mr Nowak", "Nowak!", "Night, Tom", with Ada saying "Tom" first on the worst night, the wrong person getting there before the right one.
3. The failed carry and the forty-one: a lost envelope becoming "Nowak, T. £82" in the debt book, and a list of names that turns a detective's mother and a rival's father into doorsteps the player has stood on.
