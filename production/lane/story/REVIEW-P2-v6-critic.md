# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 6: a critic's review

I measure game stories against Henry and Hans in Kingdom Come: Deliverance II and Arthur Morgan in Red Dead Redemption 2. This one gets closer than most, and its faults are fixable.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8 out of 10. A serious, carefully built story with one great idea (a crime solved with a steering wheel, a mileometer and a fare book) and a closing hour I would remember. It is held back by a mystery that gives itself away early, a thin road for the player who refuses the crime, and a last act carrying too much.

1. Tom: 7. The flaw is played rather than stated, with take-or-pay offers and a page headed "Asked", and the suitcase under the bed is a lovely small fear. He is still mostly a pair of hands, and the thirty cut-out pages of his Ledger tease a past the frame forbids paying off.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Ada, Pauline and Terry are people I would talk about afterwards, and the two widows in the back of one cab is a scene. Twenty-one named people plus regulars, plus late arrivals like Shaun, Edith, Brenda and Frank, is too many for sixteen hours.
3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is excellent: lawful, honest, priced, and she comes at Tom four different ways, one of which uses his own line against him. Danny is lifted by his father at the gate, but Agar is letters, a verbal tic and a closing confession.
4. The central mystery: 8. A real truth, found by play, that changes what minute two meant ("Every fare goes in the book") and the name Sheila will not use. But the clues point at Sheila hours before the bible thinks they do, and the decoy is undercut by the game's own lessons.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The ferry, the carry with its honest routes, Ada's alley question and Ron's biscuit-tin L-plates at the bollard are a strong first two hours. Monday is crowded with nine beats of talk in its first twelve minutes, much of it referred to as "as approved" rather than shown here.
6. Structure and pace: 7. The three movements of Act II and the weekly table are well made, and nothing repeats on paper. Act III crams two weeks, eight beats and four endings into two and a half hours, which is exactly how last acts get called rushed.
7. The middle: 7. Returning fares, dusk jobs and Sheila's count give each week its own colour, and the Fifth is a proper midpoint. The friendship that is the plot is off the screen for three hours of the Empty Seat, and the middle leans on optional discoveries a hurried player may never make.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. The least likely suspect who never told a lie is a known shape; the way it is proved, on a dial and at a bollard, is not. Mickey's heart failing on the very night is a convenience.
9. The endings: 8. Each has a last scene, a last image and a line in each book, and Ron's seat as the verdict is clean. "You never ask for owt. You've just done it again" is the best line in the bible; Sheila charged in the best ending keeps it honest.
10. The side stories: 6. Ivy Leadley and her daughter protecting each other from a thing both know is a whole story, and the watch has a sharp second turn. One way is an errand with a wardrobe, and the umbrella mostly restates the main plot through Alison.
11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline's radio, Terry's fifth word, Shiner bursting in the back seat and the crabs are genuinely funny. Too much of it is listed rather than built as set pieces, and the bible says so more than it shows.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 9. The mileometer, the handbrake that ends the game best untouched, the seat runner, the hearing that counts who you let help you, the Widow pricing her last offer on your answer: this is story carried by systems. I would single this part out.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The mystery leaks and the decoy does not hold. Where: scene 10 (Car two), scene 15 (the third lesson) and section 10's "Why it surprises". The bible says every player will believe Ron drove. But lesson one has Ron hitting a bollard and lesson two has him at eleven miles an hour, so the game itself proves he could not have driven eleven miles at night with a fire behind him. Then scene 10 shows the garage bill struck off twice "with the initials S.D.", and June says Sheila has not sat behind a wheel in thirty years. Any player who has watched a crime drama has the answer around hour eight, and scene 15's "Still where she left it" lands as confirmation, not a turn. Fix: take Sheila's initials off the bill, or make the strikes in Mickey's hand on her instruction, and move June's line after scene 15. Make the real decoy Mickey: small, on a cushion, seat forward, the planner. Let the player suspect that the infirmary line is a false alibi Sheila wrote for him. The turn then becomes that the alibi is true and the noose is hers, which uses the frame's "alibi and noose" exactly.

2. The refusal road is thin. Where: section 11, the player who winds the firm down. The bar names this road and says it must not be thin. It gets one paragraph, a six-row table and a borrowed ending. Albert's net, the hall held with sixty chairs, Edith Stockill's finger on eleven miles and Ada walking out are good seeds, but none is written as a scene with lines, choices and a cost. A player who goes straight would feel he is playing the outline of a game. Fix: write three scenes at the weight of Act II's: the pay-out carried on foot past Danny's lads; Edith's audit as this road's scene 10, so the honest player finds the eleven miles by being honest; and Ada's walk-out with Tom's answer to it. Give the road its own version of the Widow's last offer.

3. Act III is crowded and too much of it is talk. Where: section 8, Agar's week, Reese's Monday to Wednesday, Sheila's kitchen, Thursday's phone box and two doors, Friday's drive, Ellis at the counter. That is eight beats, four endings and two weeks in two and a half hours. Agar explains his whole part in one speech in the rain; Reese reads the lines aloud and then explains the stakes; Ellis explains the charges. The frame says few short scenes. A player would feel the hands go still just as the story peaks. Fix: cut the phone box and fold its doors into Thursday's two. Let Agar's part be found, not told: the watchman in the back of a cab, or Agar's letter to the Crown on Reese's clipboard. Keep the yard gate lifted off its hinges as the hands-on set piece. Take half an hour from the Empty Seat and give it to Act III.

4. Ron's reason for the coat strains belief. Where: section 10, point 8, the midpoint, and "If Ron never left". Ron leaves the only friend he has made in ten years and takes a foreman's job to keep Shiner quiet. But in the branch where he stays, he simply has a word with Shiner and comes back with a split knuckle, which shows the coat was never needed. A player who thinks about it will feel the midpoint was arranged. Fix: make the coat the Widow's price. Shiner is going on her day crew among the talkers and the watchman's tea, and only a foreman says who works where; she will move Shiner to the night gate for good if Ron takes the coat. She prices everything, so it fits her rule, and it makes the coat something Ron bought rather than chose.

5. Agar and Danny are thinner than the Widow, and the cast sprawls. Where: section 5, Agar's letters and "I do apologise"; Danny's lock-up and car theft; the twenty-one named people plus late arrivals. Agar never comes at Tom in person until the week of the letter, and then he confesses. Danny's best moment is borrowed from the Widow's scene. Players punish antagonists thinner than the leads. Fix: give Agar one scene of his own before the hearing: the yard surveyed, or Tom asked to drive him round the basin he is about to lose, so his clock is felt and not only dated. Cut One way's clerk into another existing face, and fold Shaun, Edith and Brenda into regulars already met.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review?

No, not yet. I would single out its last Friday: the bookkeeper driving her own night with an officer in the back, the bollard, and the handbrake the player must leave alone. I have not seen that done before. But to be a reason to play, the whole fifteen hours has to hold. Today a sharp player solves it two hours early, the honest road is an outline, and the last act talks where it should drive. Fix the first and third of those and the answer becomes yes.

One frame note for the owner. The bar asks for Sheila "as written". This version keeps her lines and gives her one new fact that changes what every one of them means. I think it is the bible's best decision, but it is his to rule on, and nobody should treat it as settled.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. The eleven miles. The fare book's two lines, the mileometer driven by the player, the bollard with the firm's paint, and Sheila's last drive with Reese writing it down. Story and play are the same act here.
2. The handbrake. Set up on the first Sunday as "I go, you stop", and paid off as the last input in the game, where the right thing to do is nothing. That is the theme of a man who never lets anyone do anything, turned into a button.
3. Ron in the passenger seat. Naming buildings by what they were, the stories that each stop a sentence later, three of number nine with one inside his coat, the bench of dockers at the hearing, and the seat he sits in as every ending's verdict. This is the relationship that could stand next to Henry and Hans, and it must not be cut for time.
