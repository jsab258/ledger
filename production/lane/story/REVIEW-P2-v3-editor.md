# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 3: a script editor's note

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7 out of 10. Built with one relationship I would remember and one mechanic (the mileometer) I would steal; but the mystery is easier to guess than the bible thinks, the middle is thin, and the best ending rests on a legal soft landing.

1. Tom: 8. The flaw is played rather than stated: take or pay, the question that costs an answer, the packed suitcase. His change shows in Ron's last seat.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Ada, Pauline and Terry are people, and the Widow's rule makes her a character rather than a function. But nearly everybody speaks in the same dry epigram.
3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is the best thing in the bible after Ron: lawful, honest, and dangerous because of it. Agar's confession in the cab is on the nose.
4. The central mystery: 7. It is fair and it is found with the hands, which is rare. But "the man who cannot drive drove" is the first guess of any player who has seen a crime drama, and the real turns (he fled and came back; he asked for the coat) are emotional refinements rather than reversals.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. Ferry, walk-round, the carry, the landing and lesson one are strong, and the bollard already wearing the firm's paint is a lovely plant. The first hour is talk-heavy before the hands start.
6. Structure and pace: 7. The midpoint at about seven hours is well placed and Act III is tight. The first movement leaves the mystery dormant for four and a half hours, and fixed calendar scenes sit uneasily with "scenes fire on states".
7. The middle: 6. Six weeks of fares are carried by a six-row table: one returning fare, one dusk job, one count a week. That is a design note, not nine and a half hours of story.
8. Surprise against cliché: 6. Dying docks, an inherited firm, a friend with a secret: all familiar. The inspector owning the night is a real surprise; the rest is good execution of a known shape.
9. The endings: 8. Every ending is a seat and a last image, the handbrake left alone is a superb final input, and "Nowak!" from the wrong side of the ladder is earned. The better Kingdom is too tidy, and its legal basis does not hold.
10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and her daughter protecting each other from what both know is a proper short story; the watch has a sharp second turn. Karen's wardrobe is a sketch.
11. Tone and humour: 7. Warm, local, grounded; the crabs, Terry's fifth word and the ham will get smiles. I see few things that will make a player laugh out loud.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The dial, the handbrake, take or pay, the phone box that only reaches people who once gave you something: this is talk carrying consequence and hands carrying plot. The scale of an eleven-mile night in a playable town is unaddressed.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The best ending lets a crime evaporate. Where: section 8, Friday, the "R. Kirby" branch, and section 9, the better Kingdom. Ron writes, in his own hand, that he drove three men to a burglary of a bonded store that ended in a fire and a man burned. A VAT officer then "recommends that nothing is done" and the story treats that as the end of it. Reese owns the duty and the firm's assessment; he does not own reckless arson or burglary, and DS Ellis, two streets away with the file open, is simply absent. Why it matters: the most honest road's climax becomes its least believable. Fix: give Ellis the last decision in her own short scene. Reese settles the duty and the firm; the confession goes to Ellis; what she does with it turns on what the player has built (the Bishop's statement, no complainant since the importer went south and was paid, and how Tom handled Ivy). Let it cost something even at best: a caution, a court date in the spring, a licence test postponed.

2. The mystery is guessed too early and has no false floor. Where: section 4 (Ron "cannot drive"), Act II scene 10, and section 10's "why it surprises". The alibi that clears Mickey arrives in the same count that opens the question, and Kevin is dismissed in one line. So there is one suspect, obvious since minute seven. Why it matters: BAR's commonest punishment is a plot guessed in the first hour. Fix: split scene 10. Let the first count show car two out and eleven miles, with Shaun's "Mickey said he'd clipped the bollard" and Agar's watchman pointing at Mickey, so the player spends a stretch believing his uncle drove to a fire. Then June, the night sister, says where her father was at ten to eleven, and the floor drops. Ron then becomes a discovery, not a confirmation.

3. The hinge of the coat does not hold. Where: section 10, item 7, and scene 13. Shiner said who drove "in the Tangs' queue", a public place, and Ron takes the foreman's coat to put him on the night gate. Moving Shiner afterwards cannot unsay what a queue heard, and yet the street never has the truth. Why it matters: it is the emotional reveal, and it falls apart on ten seconds' thought. Fix: Shiner said it to one man, Darren, who sells what he hears. Ron's coat stops a second telling, and Darren's silence has its own price: the door, and a coat with a firm's name on it, which is the want Darren voices unasked in scene 8.

4. The middle is a table, not a story. Where: section 7, the weekly table and "What a week is". Nine and a half hours, eighteen scenes, and between them one returning fare, one dusk job and one count per week. Why it matters: reviewers punish this stretch as the same loop, and for three hours the best relationship is across the road. Fix: give every week two threads of the main story carried by the loop itself, not by scenes: a fare that carries a piece of the night (a docker's widow who smelt smoke on a cab, the watchman as a regular), and a count that changes what the player can do next week. Play Alison's hour or Ellis's ride inside the Seat, so the mystery moves before the Fifth.

5. Truth is too often volunteered. Where: Ada's sleeves at the first tea, Shaun's "second time", the Widow on Agar's watchman in scene 16, Agar's speech in scene 18, the Bishop's "came back". The best find (the dial) is the player's; too many of the others are handed over by people who happen to be in the mood. Why it matters: it softens "uncovered by play". Fix: make two of these earned rather than offered. Ada's sleeves only after Tom has taken her tea twice; the Widow's knowledge of the watchman bought with the straight question the player has to think to ask; Agar's call learnt from Reese's file rather than confessed in the rain. And cut a third of the epigrams.

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Section 0, item 4: "Mickey paid his keep every Friday until he died" reads as though the Bishop died. Fix: "until Mickey died".
2. Scene 10: Shaun has had car two apart before, "same place", yet a glove and a brass tally survived a year under the seat, and Sheila cleaned a car that smelt like a grate. Fix: the first repair was the wing panel only, and the glove is inside the seat frame, not under it.
3. Scene 4: a private hire driver's licence in 1990 meant a council check with the police on past convictions, which the council is bound to ask about. Fix: one line that the check came back clean and nobody on the bench reads beyond it.
4. Section 10, item 6, and Act I Sunday: why does a man sworn never to drive again take out a provisional licence the week after the funeral, before Tom arrives? Fix: Sheila tells him to, because the firm is a driver short.
5. Section 9, the Reckoning: the Investigation Division car takes Ron even when Tom wrote "M. Suddaby" or no name. Fix: Ron is in the back only if his name is on the paper; otherwise it is Tom's turn at the window.
6. Scene 16: the Widow knows of Agar's watchman with no source. Fix: he stands at her gate at six for a second wage.
7. Scene 18 and section 8: Agar telephones Customs rather than Ellis. Fix: one line: "A bond is the Crown's. The Crown does not lose files."
8. Scenes 12 and 17, section 16: an eleven-mile night at real scale is half an hour of driving in a district called the Hook. Fix: decide now whether the dial reads in game-tenths or the map is built long; the numbers must survive both.
9. Act II: the third lesson is fixed to 18 November, but a player may arrive there without scenes 10 to 12. Fix: a stated fallback (the confession becomes "Not down there, boss" again, and the lesson waits for the count).
10. Friday, "R. Kirby": a learner in a licensed private hire car would not be insured in 1990. Fix: the lesson car has its plates off and is on Tom's own policy, which Sheila arranged.
11. Section 0, item 1: "for the dockers' club" will read in 1990 Britain as a working men's club with a bar. Fix: "for the sick club" everywhere.
12. Table of pay-offs: "I can drive, boss. Near enough." and "at this bollard" appear in no scene. Fix: put both into scene 15.
13. Act I, the landing: "£19 carrier's money" against "the round's twenty" is unclear. Fix: say what the carrier is paid.

Period points that hold: the Dock Labour Scheme's end in July 1989; VAT under Customs and Excise with a six-year reach; the Registry of Friendly Societies; every weekday given.

## 4. Would I single it out in a review?

No, not in this version. I would praise Ron and Tom, the dial, the handbrake and the Widow by name. But I would also say I guessed who drove, the middle sagged between set pieces, and the happy ending asked me to forget a burned man. Fix points one to four and it could become yes.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. Ron in the passenger seat, the lessons and the handbrake. A friendship told through who sits where, ending in an input the player declines, is a reason to play.
2. The Widow's rule. An opponent who keeps every deal, tells no lie when asked straight and holds nothing over anybody turns the player's own suspicion into the twist.
3. The fare book, the mileometer and Mr Reese. Two lines of one night, an alibi and a noose, proved on a dial by a mild man with a flask: this is the frame's promise kept and improved.
