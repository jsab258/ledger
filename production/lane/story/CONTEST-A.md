# Twice

A treatment for LEDGER's story. Spoken lines are samples.

## 1. The shape in a paragraph

Tom Nowak inherits a minicab office and, with it, one seat in an old dockers' club that pays forty-one families four pounds a week. Nothing about the club is hidden from him: by the end of his first week he knows what the envelope is, who counts it, and that last year the club burned a warehouse as a lesson to a tenant who stopped paying. For three weeks the club is the kindest thing in the Hook. It fills the public bench at his licence hearing. Its free rides for old people are the best fares he drives. Then, on Bonfire Night, an ordinary fare comes over the radio, on the club's account, no charge: two men off the last ferry, to the warehouse row, wait, and return. The player drives it, or lets a driver take it, or works out in time what it is. In the morning the bookkeeper writes it into the fare book in her small upright hand, because every fare goes in the book. The line she writes is, word for word, the line she wrote a year ago for Mickey. The mystery of what the firm did last year is solved by the player doing it himself. The second half of the game is what a man does about a line in a book with his name on the roof above it, and it ends on a Saturday night in December when the same fare is booked a third time.

There is no hidden hand in this story. The surprise is the player's own.

## 2. The hours

Act I, the Inheritance (15 to 21 October): 2 hours. Act II, first half, the Account (22 October to 4 November): 4.5 hours. The midpoint, the Fifth: half an hour. Act II, second half, the Line (6 November to 2 December): 5 hours. Act III, the Ledger Comes Due (3 to 15 December): 2.5 hours. Side stories: 2 hours. About 16.5 hours in all, with fourteen played scenes after week one.

## 3. Tom

His want. The postscript of Mickey's letter: "Don't let them call you Mickey's nephew for ever." A place that is his and his own name said by people who mean it.

His flaw. He lets nobody give him anything. He pays for tea that was on the house and counts money twice in front of people. It looks like pride and it is fear: a man who owes nothing can pick up his suitcase. The offers run all game as one press, take it or pay.

The load the flaw cannot carry. The club gives without being asked. Its people write his references and sit behind him at the hearing. He has taken the biggest gift in the Hook before he knows it is one, and on the Fifth it is called in. After that the question turns over: the man who would owe nobody now owes a named person something he cannot pay for with money, and every later scene asks whether he will be owed to.

His change. By the three choices only he can make: whether the fare is driven; whether the line stands in the book; and what he does when it is booked again. Each ending is the sum of those three.

## 4. The people

Nineteen who matter. Nobody on this list is secretly anybody else.

- Sheila Dunn, 53, the bookkeeper. As written. She will not lie in the fare book for anyone, and that one rule is the whole of what she is hiding.
- Ron Kirby, 58, the passenger seat. He held the club's seat on the row for twenty years and collected its money on Mondays. He knows what an account fare for two men off the last ferry is.
- Cyril Blenkin, 63, the tallyman at the landing, the club's last officer. Not a mastermind: an exact old man who counted cargo for forty years and now counts this. "Forty-one go out. What comes in keeps the forty-one going out. That's all it ever was." He is the opponent, and he never raises his voice or does anything himself.
- Ada, 74, the widow at her window. Exactly what she seems: Walter Jensen's widow, on the list, lonely, sharp, the best witness on Quay Street. Her tea is tea, and it is where Tom is told what the envelope is. Readers who wait for her to turn out to be the boss will wait all game.
- Pauline Kitching, 44, the radio. She gives out the Fifth's fare in the same voice as all the others, and has to live with that.
- Terry Hodgson, 52, nights, four words a shift. He has driven nights for twenty years and seen nothing, on purpose.
- Darren Milner, 25, the first person who speaks to Tom off the ferry and the first debt he collects (£22). He sells what he hears, wants to be somebody's man, and sleeps wherever is dry.
- June Suddaby, 34, Mickey's daughter, night sister at the infirmary. "I don't want anything out of that office. I'd check what you're getting, if I were you."
- Father Brendan Walsh, who buried Mickey and counted who came.
- Danny Cammack, 26, the main rival. He runs Hook Cars, two yellow cars at £1.50 a ride, and things other people sell for him. He has rented a lock-up on the warehouse row and will not pay the club's weekly "watch". He tells the other tenants not to pay either. He is loud, he is funny, and he is right.
- Maureen Jensen, the Widow, the port's labour contractor. Small here: she wants Ron for her gate and the club's book closed, and she puts each offer once.
- Geoffrey Agar, 61, the landlord. Small here: his agents own the freehold of the parade and the row.
- DS Carol Ellis, 44. She asks while a thing is answerable and tells once it is not.
- Norman Dent, the night watchman on the row.
- The Bishop, 60, one of Ron's old gang, whom the player hears of in stories Ron never finishes.
- Alison Sedman, 31, the paper's port reporter, the someone worth being honest for. Small and whole: she rides in his cab, leaves an umbrella, and asks one question all game, "where did an hour go?", first about last year and then about him.
- Mr T. A. Reese of Customs and Excise.
- Two men off the ferry. The fare book calls them "two fares". One gives a name at the counter later: Coultas.

## 5. Act I: the Inheritance (2 hours)

The approved first week stands. In order, with what this version adds.

1. Monday. The ferry and the letter. Quay Street. Sheila's walk-round and the fare-book rule. The first fare, Father Walsh and the forty-one empty envelopes. The debt book. Darren's £22. Ron and the first ask. The carry to the landing, three routes, the constable. Cyril's thumb on the envelope: "Right. Wednesday, same again."
2. Tuesday to Thursday. The debts, the fares, Grace's tea (the first offer), Terry's flask on the rank (the second). Ada's question about her alley and her one invitation. Added: the first account fare. Pauline, on air: "Car one, Mrs Duggleby for the chapel, Blenkin account, no charge, and she'll tell you which way." Sheila, when he asks what an account fare is: "The club's people ride free. Mickey's dues. It goes in the book like any other. A fare at no charge is still a fare." By Thursday he has driven three, and they are the warmest minutes of the week.
3. Wednesday night, the tea. Ada tells him plainly what he has been carrying: the sick club her husband started, forty-one names, four pounds, half over the water. "The tallyman keeps it. He kept it for Walter. I get mine like the rest." Nothing is asked of him.
4. Friday. Rent to Agar's agents, wages by hand, and the round with Ron: twenty doors, a holdall, faces. One door Ron takes himself: "Not that one. That's Mickey's own." Then the quay wall. Ron buys three of number nine and puts one in his coat. "Feller I know."
5. Saturday. Danny Cammack on Quay Street, buying chips, friendly. "You've got the keys. I've got the accent. One of us is in the wrong house."
6. Sunday. The first lesson and the bollard. Sheila's question. That night the fourth ask is a passenger: the tallyman, to the far end of the warehouse row. He stands at a burnt plot. "A man on this row stopped paying the watch last year. Then four more stopped. Forty-one would have gone without by Christmas. So the row had a lesson, and it paid again for eleven months. Your uncle saw to the car. He stood here the morning after and said, that's the last one, Cyril. I told him it wasn't his to say." Then: "Blenkin. You may as well have the name."

What the player knows at the end of week one: all of it. The club is real, it feeds old people, it runs on a weekly payment from the row's tenants called the watch, and when the watch stops, something burns. Nobody has lied to him. The question he carries into Act II is not who. It is whether he can have the first half of that sentence without the second.

## 6. Act II, first half: the Account (4.5 hours)

Two weeks. The spine is the club being good to him, one gift at a time, while the row stops paying. Every scene is a named person and ends with something taken or paid for.

Scene 1. Monday's wages, and the answer. Whatever he told Sheila on Sunday is on the street by noon. If he took the firm over, Ron takes him on the Monday collection: eighteen units on the row and the quay, a few pounds each, "for the watch". If he wound it down, the carry and the round stop, and Sheila says: "The old people still want the chapel on Sundays. Charge them or don't. It goes in the book either way." Account fares go on in both. The two roads share every scene from here; what differs is whether his hands are in the money.

Scene 2. Lily Tang pays. On the collection, the takeaway pays the watch without a word, and stops saying "Same as last week? I remember." A woman who liked him becoming polite. If he is not collecting, he is in the queue when Ron does it, and she looks at him all the same.

Scene 3. The lock-up. Danny has a brick lock-up at the dead end of the row and has not paid the watch since he took it. Ron, at the door: "Watch." Danny, through it: "Watch what? Watch me." He says it in the caff by lunchtime and four more units stop that week. Cyril, at the landing, counting: "Five on the row have stopped. It doesn't add." The round on Friday is nineteen doors, and the player chooses whose is not full. On Monday that person is a fare.

Scene 4. The yellow cars. Danny's number goes up on the freephone in the ferry terminal; getting it back is a night's work with a screwdriver, a word with the clerk, or a price war. He rides in a Mickey's cab and pays a two-pound fare with a fifty: "Keep it. Call it rent." Take it, or pay.

Scene 5. The hearing, Tuesday 30 October. The council's licensing sub-committee; a councillor who owes Agar has referred the application over the dead man's badge. The clerk reads the references the applicant has handed in. Only people Tom has let do him a turn have felt able to write one. And then the public bench: it is full. Winifred, Albert Suggitt with his net, old men in their good coats, Ada out of her house for the first time. Nobody asked them. Cyril stands at the back with his cap in his hand and says nothing. Licensed, or licensed with conditions. In the corridor Cyril says the only warm thing he says all game: "They'd not have come for Mickey. He never needed them to." It is the biggest gift in the Hook, and the player was not offered the press.

Scene 6. A fare with a sleeping bag. Darren, at dusk, wants dropping at the row with a carrier bag and a sleeping bag. "Night watchman, me. Fiver a night off Danny to kip on his radios. Don't say I said, it's meant to look empty." Pay or no charge: the player's choice, and the first time "no charge" is his own to give.

Scene 7. The second lesson. Sunday 4 November, the dock road, L-plates, eleven miles an hour, and Ron asks to go round again. On the way back Ron says, looking out of the window: "It's a year since, near enough. Mickey was bad all that month. If anything comes on the account this week that's not an old woman for the chapel, boss, give it Kevin. No. Don't give it Kevin. Just don't take it." Asked why: "That's all I'll say in a car with a radio in it."

What the player believes going into the Fifth: the club is owed, the row is not paying, and Danny is asking for trouble. A player who has listened knows what is coming and where Darren sleeps. A player who has not is about to do it.

## 7. The midpoint: the Fifth

Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990. The busiest night of the cab year: the quay full of the town's grown people, the fireworks off the old coal jetty, Pauline running three cars like a general. Twenty minutes of the best fares in the game, fast, funny, one after another. Then, in the same voice:

"Car one, two off the last ferry for the warehouse row, wait and return. Blenkin account, no charge. They'll be at the landing with a holdall."

It is one job with three keys, and nothing stops the player taking any of them.

- Drive it. Two men in car coats, polite, who put the holdall in the boot themselves and say nothing on the way. Ron, if he is in the passenger seat, looks at one of them once and then at the road. At the far end of the row: "Wait here, driver. Light off." The wait is the scene. Three minutes, the engine running, the fireworks going up over the roofs behind him, Pauline's Bonfire Night commentary on the radio. Then a second glow, low, in the mirror, that is not a firework. He can sit. He can drive off and leave them. He can get out and go down the row. If he goes, the door of the lock-up is already alight and there is a sleeping bag inside it. Tom cannot die; he can come out of that door with Darren over his shoulder and his own hands marked.
- Do not take it. "Car two, then. Terry." Terry drives it, as he has driven everything for twenty years. The player hears it happen on the radio from the other side of the Hook: "Car two, waiting." Then nothing. Then Norman Dent's voice, from the phone box on the row, asking for the brigade.
- Work it out. A player who remembers the tallyman's passenger ride, Ron's Sunday and the sleeping bag has several honest ways through: fetch Darren off the row before ten; tell Danny, who empties his lock-up and is waiting with six lads; meet the ferry with Ron and send two men back over the water; or drive them, knowing, and see that the building is empty first. Each has its price, stated below.

What is lost, by what was done.

- If the lock-up burned with Darren in it: Darren's hands and one side of his face. He will not sell anything on a pavement again. This is the common first playthrough.
- If it burned empty: nobody is hurt, the lesson is taught, and Tom did it with his eyes open.
- If it was stopped: nobody is hurt, and the row has watched Mickey's nephew send the club's men home. By Friday every unit has stopped paying and forty-one envelopes are empty. Cyril, at the landing, without anger: "Then you'll go round on Friday and tell them why."

There is no clean hand on the Fifth. That is the midpoint: not a revelation but a deed, and the player picked it.

The quay, an hour later. Half the Hook has turned from the fireworks to the glow over the row. Ada is on her step, in her coat. She saw the cab go out and she saw it come back, and she saw who was in it. "You'd better come in, Tom." It is the first time anyone on the street has used his name, and she has used it because she knows what he is now and thinks he should not be on his own. It is tea. It is only ever tea. He can take it.

## 8. Act II, second half: the Line (5 hours)

Four weeks. The spine is one line in a book and the people who come to read it. Seven scenes.

Scene 8. The morning after. Sheila has Pauline's sheet in one hand and the fare book open. She writes, and the player watches her write: "5.11. 22.40. Car one. Landing to warehouse row and return. Two fares. Wait. No charge." If Terry drove, "Car two". Tom can ask her not to. "Every fare goes in the book." He can ask her why that one. She does not answer. She turns the debt book round on the counter instead, uncaps her pen and holds it out. Under Mickey's five names he can write a sixth in his own hand, and what he owes it. Or not. From this morning she calls him nothing at all: not "new management", not his name.

Scene 9. The ward. Visiting hour. June is the night sister. Darren, bandaged, cheerful, which is the worst of it: "I heard a car waiting outside. I thought, that's a Mickey's, I know that engine. I thought it was you, come to run me home." Tom has brought something or he has not. Darren will take anything. If Terry drove, Terry is sitting in the corridor with his cap in his hands and has been there since six, and uses five words for the first time in the game: "Tell Pauline it don't count." If the building burned empty or did not burn, the ward scene is Darren on the rank instead, telling everyone how close it was, and June's line comes at her door: "You've had your first one, then. He had his last year."

Scene 10. Danny gets in. Not a brick: a fare. Danny, in the back of car one, alone. "The row. Go on. You know the way." They stand at what is left. He cannot go to the police, because of what was in it. He wants three things: the two men, the tallyman, and to know whether the man driving him knew. The player answers, in his own words, once. Told the truth, Danny is quiet for the first time in the game. "Then you're the same as my lock-up. Something of theirs that got used." From here he is either the best ally the player has against the club or the best reason to keep it: he offers to do to Cyril what Tom's line will not let Tom do. "He's sixty-three. You can't. I'm not you." Lied to, he finds out by the weekend, and the yellow cars stop being a joke.

Scene 11. The row pays. Friday 9 November. If the lesson was taught, every unit on the row has paid the watch since Tuesday, unasked, and the holdall is full for the first time since Tom came. Twenty doors. Winifred has her coal money. Albert Suggitt says, "Tell the tallyman thank you." The player hands over, door by door, what the fire bought, and Ron says nothing on any step. On the quay wall Ron buys three again. If the lesson was stopped, it is the same round with an empty bag, and Tom says why at each door or lets Ron say it.

Scene 12. Remembrance Sunday, 11 November. The dockside memorial. Father Walsh reads the names: the wars, and the men the docks have taken since. This year, Michael Suddaby. Everybody is in one place for the only time: Ada, Cyril with his tally medals, the Widow across the square, Agar with the Harbour Board's wreath, Danny at the back, Ellis, Sheila, June. And beside Ron, in a wheeled chair with a rug, a man of sixty whose hands are in grey cotton gloves. Two minutes of silence in which the player chooses only where Tom looks. Afterwards Ron takes a foil parcel out of his coat and puts it in the man's lap. The third portion.

Scene 13. Last year's book. The fare books for 1988 and 1989 are on the shelf behind Sheila's chair, and have been in plain sight since the walk-round. Nothing stops the player taking one down at night with the office empty; nothing prompts him to. Saturday 18 November 1989, squeezed in under the day's last fare, in her small upright hand:

"22.40. Car two. Landing to warehouse row and return. Two fares. Wait. No charge."

The same words. And under it, the one thing this year's line does not have, in the same hand:

"23.55. Car two. Row to Infirmary. One. No charge."

The player has the whole of it without a word being said. Mickey drove it himself. Somebody was in the warehouse. If he finds it alone and is still at the shelf when she comes in at eight, she says, "Mr Nowak." It is the first time. Then, because he has read it and did not ask her to change his own: "He wouldn't write it down. Neither did you. I looked at Tuesday's sheet and there was a gap in it the same shape. Nobody reads a fare book. I've waited a year for one person." If he asks her instead of looking, she takes the book down and opens it for him, and says the rest, and keeps his name.

Scene 14. The third lesson. Ron asks for the car. He drives, properly, without a word, through the Hook to a terrace on Tannery Row, parks at the kerb at the second try and puts the handbrake on. Then he finishes a story for the first time in the game. The Bishop had nowhere after the scheme ended and slept in the importer's warehouse, on the cotton bales, because it was dry. Mickey carried him out. Mickey paid his keep every Friday until he died, and it is the door Ron takes himself. "Tallyman knew by the Monday. He said, the row's paid, Ron. I count what I'm given." Then: "I told you not to take it. I should have told you why. Twenty year you don't say why." If the player goes in, the Bishop looks at his hands and then at Tom's and says, "You'll be the nephew. He never missed a Friday. You needn't start."

After it, the Widow's offer to Ron comes due, and it is put kindly: "A firm that burns lads is no place for you, Ron. Mine only works them." He tells Tom himself and asks what he thinks. Let him go, ask him to stay, or tell him he owes it.

Scene 15. Two readers. The fire has put two people on the street. Ellis has a burned lock-up, a hurt man if there was one, and Norman Dent's "a cab with its light off; I couldn't say which". A witness is only as sure as he was. She asks to see the night's sheets and has no paper to make him show them. Alison has a second fire on one row in a year and gets into his cab to ask where an hour went. She asks him the question as a person first and a reporter second, and says which is which. What he tells her truly she keeps, at a cost to her; what he hides and she finds, she prints. Either of them can be told. Either can be pointed at Terry, whose car it might have been, and Terry will not say a word to save himself.

Scene 16. A man at the counter. Sunday 18 November, a year to the day. One of the two fares comes in out of the rain for the rest of his money. "Coultas. The tallyman says the office pays." Pay him from the tin; front him on the pavement; or follow him to the last ferry and see where over the water he goes. Whatever is done, he says one thing at the door that sets the end: "Same again when he sends for us. He says there's a chandler stopped."

Scene 17. Agar in the rain. That night Geoffrey Agar walks down Quay Street for the first time in his life, without a hat, and knocks. His agents have served notice on every unit on the row: fire risk. He would like to buy the yard. Pushed, he says the truest thing any rival says: "I have never paid anyone for a fire, Mr Nowak. I have never needed to. Your uncle's friends light them for nothing, and I buy what is left. You might ask your tallyman who his lessons have taught." The club that keeps forty-one old dockers has, one unit at a time, been clearing the quay for the man who will build on it. Sell him the yard, refuse him, or take what he said to the landing.

The act ends on a letter: courteous, dated, signed T. A. Reese.

## 9. Act III: the Ledger Comes Due (2.5 hours)

Why he comes: a registered trader has died and the name on the form has changed. Every player gets the letter. If Danny was lied to, Danny has also telephoned.

Monday 10 December. Reese at the counter with this year's book. He is not interested in who likes Tom. "The Blenkin account. Four hundred and twelve journeys this year and not one invoice. A fare at no charge is either a kindness or a hole, Mr Nowak. I'm paid to ask which."

Tuesday. The ride-along. Reese in car one all morning with his flask. Winifred for the chapel, no charge, and he watches Tom carry her bag to the door. Brenda with the box of crabs. "Are those declared, Mrs Laverack?" "They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in." By noon he has written "kindness" in the margin four hundred and ten times, in his head.

Wednesday. The old books. He goes quiet twice and makes two small marks.

Thursday. He reads one line aloud, and then turns back a year and reads the other. "Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak. Twice. I'll want those two in writing by five tomorrow: who the account is, and what was carried." He puts two slips of paper in at the pages. The books stay in the office overnight. They always have.

Thursday night. The office, dark, the two books on the counter with their slips, Sheila's pen in its groove and the stove lit. The player is alone with them. He can leave them. He can alter a line, for a man who notices ink. He can put a book in the stove, after which the books cannot hold. While he is deciding, the door opens, and it is Sheila, who does not come in at night. She has come to do it herself. She cannot. "Thirty-one years. I've never scratched a figure." She sits down in the customer's chair, on the wrong side of her own counter, and waits to see what he is.

Friday. The phone box on the corner, five coins, the afternoon. He can ring only people he once let give him something, and each answered call is an errand across the Hook: Ada, who saw the cab go out and come back and will say so or not as he asks; Edith, who can show what the account paid to whom; Father Walsh; Danny, if he was dealt with straight; June. His answer in writing is his alone. The truth names the tallyman and the two men, ends the account, and puts his own hands on the wheel of one line. "I do not know who the account is" leaves a hole the width of two fires. At five Reese reads what is in front of him and closes his notebook. "That all seems to be in order, Mr Nowak." Or: "I'll want to pass this along the corridor, Mr Nowak. I am sorry."

Saturday 15 December. The last night, and it is played. At nine Pauline's voice changes on the air. "Car one. Two off the last ferry for the row. Wait and return. Blenkin account." A pause. "No charge." The chandler has stopped paying. The tallyman is early at the landing with his cap on. Everything the player has done for two months decides what he can do now, with his hands, on a lit quay with a ferry coming in:

- Drive it. Then he is the keeper.
- Meet the boat. Whoever will stand with him is there: Ron, if he stayed, in his coat; Danny and his lads, if dealt with straight; Terry, who gets out of car two by himself; Ellis in an unmarked car, if she was told. Two men come down the gangway and see who is waiting. Cyril stands apart under the customs lamp with his tally book shut. Nobody lays a hand on him: he is sixty-three, and the game has never once offered the blow. What finishes him is that the boat goes back with two men on it and there is nothing left for him to count. "Then I've forty-one to tell."
- Be elsewhere. Then somebody else drives it, and the fare book says who.

## 10. The central turn, and why it is fair

The turn is the line. On the morning of 6 November the player watches the person he most wants the good opinion of write his fare into the book; a fortnight later, if he goes looking, he finds she wrote the same words a year before for the man whose chair he is in, and one line more. What it changes: the letter ("There is more to it than cabs. Come and look before you say yes"); "Every fare goes in the book", which was never a slogan; "New management", which she said to the last man too; the round's one door that Ron takes himself; the third portion; Ron's Sunday warning; and every account fare the player ever enjoyed.

Why it surprises. Every reader of a crime story is watching for the hidden boss, and there is none. The club told him what it was in week one. The thing nobody expects is that the game will hand the crime to the player as an ordinary fare in an ordinary voice on its busiest and happiest night, and then make the bookkeeper's virtue the instrument that records it.

Why it is fair. Every piece is in plain sight before it is needed: the tallyman's own account of last year; "your uncle saw to the car"; what an account fare is; five units stopped; Danny's lock-up; the sleeping bag; Ron's "just don't take it"; the old books on the shelf. A careful first-time player can see it whole on the Sunday and act. The second playthrough is a different game from the first minute.

## 11. The endings

States, never a menu. Each has a last scene, a line Sheila writes in the fare book and a line Tom writes in his Ledger.

The Kingdom. The fare was driven on the fifteenth, the business stands and the books hold. Last scene: the quay after ten. A cab comes back along Quay Street with its light off and stops at the rank. Two men walk to the landing. Above the office, a figure at the flat window. The tallyman, below, counts with his thumb. If Ron stayed, he gets out of the passenger side and does not look up. If Sheila stayed, it is because the line was left true: she is at the counter with the book open, and she holds the pen out across it, and for the first time the fare is entered in a third hand. Fare book: "15.12. 22.40. Car one. Landing to warehouse row and return. Two fares. Wait. No charge." His. Ledger: "41 out. T.N." On the better turn (nobody was in a building on either night of his, one friend counts him, the street does not hold it as fact) she says "Goodnight, Mr Nowak" and stays the quarter. She never says "Tom" in this ending.

The Straight Life. The boat was met and the account is closed. Last scene, the better turn: the chapel hall, a Friday. A plate on a table, Father Walsh, Edith with the pen, and forty-one envelopes paid in the open out of ten pence on every fare, printed on the receipt: "For the old dockers." Julie Kettlewell has the firm; Darren, if he was hurt, is on the radio beside Pauline with a headset he cannot put on himself. The Bishop's Friday is paid by whoever the player made it. On the last evening Tom gets into the back of car one. Julie drives; Ron is in the front passenger seat, by habit. "Where to?" "Just round." At the office Sheila writes the fare herself. "Mind how you go, Tom." Fare book: "Nowak, T. Round the Hook. £2. Paid." The first line in the book with his name in it that anybody paid for. Ledger: "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four." The plain turn: the board painted out, the forty-one on the parish, and somebody passing who says "Morning, Tom." Fare book: "Closed." Ledger: "Stayed."

Burn Both. The Reckoning, for the man who drove and whose books did not hold: "I'll want to pass this along the corridor." Sheila hands her keys to a constable half her age. Saturday night is the first night again, the quay, the lamp, and the constable does not call "You there." He calls, "Nowak!" The player has the two inputs of night one, stand or run. Fare book: closed unwritten. Ledger: the last page cut out. The Suitcase, for the man who was elsewhere and let it drift: a grey afternoon, the gangway, the gate's van passing with Ron in the passenger seat, not looking across. Fare book: "Ferry landing. One. No charge." Ledger: a date, and nothing under it.
