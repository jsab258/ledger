# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 2: a script editor's note

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7.

1. Tom: 7. The flaw is real, costs him, and is played through two plain systems, which is rare. But he mostly answers and starts little; his change lives in the endings more than the middle.
2. The cast: 8. Sheila, Ada, Pauline, Terry and Reese are somebody within two lines; Ron is the best thing here. Kevin, Julie and Darren still wait for a turn.
3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is a 9: lawful, never lies, prices everything, and wins most by saying nothing. Danny is a 6 with one good scene; Agar a 5, a calendar and some letters.
4. The central mystery: 7. The truth changes what the lessons, the bollard and "Not down there" meant. But the main fact (Ron drove) is guessable before the midpoint, by design, so the weight sits on two quiet turns, one resting on a weak motive (point 1).
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The ferry, the walk-round, the carry with honest routes and "Nowak, T. £80" under the debts make a strong first half hour; lesson one will be retold.
6. Structure and pace: 7. The movements are well shaped and the Fifth is well placed. Act II's calendar contradicts itself; Act III piles four long speeches into four days.
7. The middle: 6. The weekly table answers the "same loop" complaint. But the Empty Seat learns mostly by being told, and the road of the player who refuses the crime is a list, not a story.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. Refusing the hidden boss, the guilty landlord and the blackmail is clever and fair. But "the old friend took the job because of a secret" is familiar.
9. The endings: 8. Each is the sum of the player's play, with a last image and two closing lines. The handbrake as a last input the player may leave alone is the best idea in the document. Some routes in are vague.
10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and Ellis, each shielding the other from what both know, is a proper short story; the watch has a strong second turn. Karen is a sketch; Alison's turn is told, not played.
11. Tone and humour: 7. "The ham had a seat", the crabs and "Tell Pauline it don't count" are funny, and the dark is kept quiet. But the comedy leans on the radio and the cab, and Act III laughs once.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The fixed passenger, the handbrake, take or pay and the hearing are story built for hands. The typed question is a large promise for the systems.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The turn the story rests on is underpowered. Where: section 5 (the Widow's rule), scene 14, section 10 item 7. What is wrong: Ron leaves the seat and sells himself so that a man he has known for three weeks will not learn that he drove off. The Widow never threatened to tell Tom. And taking her job does nothing to stop anyone telling anything. Why it matters: the whole of the middle and both late turns stand on this choice. If the player does not believe it, scene 14 plays as a trick and the Fifth plays as a writer's convenience. Fix: give the choice a working reason that comes from inside the story. Shiner talks, and Shiner is on her gate. A foreman decides who works, so the foreman is the one man Shiner has to keep sweet. Ron takes the coat to shut Shiner's mouth, and the Widow knows that this is why. That puts something at stake that the player can see (Shiner talking, as he does in scene 10), and it keeps "Who is there he'd mind knowing?" as the turn. Then play Tom's pleasure on screen in lesson two, as one press (praise him, or say nothing), so that what Ron fears losing is something the player gave him.

2. The final act's noose hangs on a weak point of procedure. Where: Act III, Thursday and five o'clock. What is wrong: a VAT officer sends a copy of a driver's signature to the police because the police "asked the public". Then a detective sergeant tells a man who drove three others to a burglary that left a man maimed that it "will not come back". Why it matters: every ending passes through this counter. A British viewer will feel it is false, and the climax becomes something arranged. Fix: make the cigarettes duty-suspended stock in bond. Then the theft is Customs' own business, the copy is Reese's duty and not a courtesy, and the outcome is decided by the Customs prosecutors and the police together. Ellis can promise only to send "what I think on top". Say nothing at all about it coming back.

3. The road where Tom winds the club down has no turn of its own. Where: section 11. What is wrong: it is three jobs and a reasonable Widow, and then the same last act. Nothing it uncovers changes what it meant. Why it matters: the bar says plainly that the road of the player who refuses the crime must not be thin, and this road is the honest player's game. Fix: the friendly society cannot be registered without the club's past accounts and its first officers. The lawful road therefore demands the same thirty years of books that the crime road hides, and somebody (Ada, as first name on the list) has to sign over the 1989 page. Being lawful becomes the thing that brings the fire to the surface. That is a turn, and it rhymes with the other road.

4. Agar and Danny are pressure, not people with plots. Where: section 5, scenes 6, 17 and 18, the Act III week, the endings. What is wrong: Agar's call to Customs is a lucky guess ("does not know how right he is"). His clock pays off in summary lines. Danny's "Your father knows why" is set up and never paid off. Why it matters: the bar names thin antagonists and a hurried last act as the thing GTA V was punished for. Fix: the watchman who "swore he was on his rounds" is employed by Agar's agents, who hold half the row. He told Agar a year ago that a cab was on the row that night, and Agar has kept the fact to spend. That makes the phone call a choice rather than luck. Pay off Danny's father in one line on the Friday blockade: a Cammack once gave a name to the police, which is why the gate will not hire one. That gives Danny a reason to keep car two away from Ellis, which is his turn.

5. The middle is told more than it is found, and its calendar does not hold. Where: Act II scenes 9 to 12. What is wrong: scene 10 says the job has a week to plan, yet it comes before Remembrance Sunday on 11 November, six days after the Fifth, and the table puts it in the week of 12 November. Most of what is learned arrives as somebody's line. Why it matters: these are the hours with the friend missing, where boredom complaints land. Fix: put the staged job after Remembrance. Make Kevin's alibi something the player has to drive to and check (his mother on the far bank, a crossing). And make Shiner's admission come out because of something the player arranged (a seat, a silence on the radio), not a speech he happens to give.

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Two brass tallies: Ellis has one from the ashes, and the Bishop's is under car two's seat. Fix: say whose the ashes tally is (Tapper's, dropped going in) and let it name him.
2. How did the Hook explain for a year the Bishop's burned hands, which are shown publicly at Remembrance? Fix: one line: the street was told a chip-pan fire at his sister's.
3. Sheila is in the office at 22:50 on a Saturday with the men waiting outside, yet "never saw". Fix: she was called in from home when Mickey rang, and the men were round the corner.
4. "15.10. Ferry landing. One. No charge" is a fare that never happened, in a book Reese has been holding since October. Fix: Reese marks it on Wednesday; Sheila's kitchen explains it.
5. Section 4 says Ron is in a vehicle in every ending; the Reckoning has no Ron. Fix: the gate's van passes as the constable calls the name.
6. Section 10 says there is no guilty dead uncle, but Mickey planned the theft. Fix: own it: "guilty of the plan, not of the drive."
7. The importer was "long since paid by his insurers" while the street says he burned it himself. Fix: the insurers paid because the fire was undetermined, after a delay.
8. Ron's lessons on a public road need a provisional licence, and lesson one has no L-plates. Fix: Sheila sends off his provisional in week one; the first lesson is on the dock estate, which is private ground.
9. "The labour exchange" in 1990. Fix: the Jobcentre, or the dole office, with Julie calling it the labour exchange as a joke.
10. A Catholic priest's "chapel" and "chapel's fund". Fix: Walsh has a church; "chapel" stays Ivy's word.
11. The Bonfire Night quay "full of the town's grown people" reads as the document dodging its own rule. Fix: the council display is cancelled for cost, so the dockers' fire is the only one.
12. Under the private hire law outside London, a licence refused at the hearing would stop Tom driving at all. Fix: say so, and make "refused" mean Julie or Terry carry the fares while he appeals.

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review?

No. Not yet. The Ron and Tom friendship, carried by a handbrake and a passenger seat, is the one thing here I would quote, and the Widow is an opponent people would talk about. But the turn everything stands on is weakly motivated, the climax depends on a procedure an audience will not believe, and the honest player's road and the middle hours are thinner than the game around them. Those are where the bar says a short game sinks. Fix the first two and it is an 8 with a claim on a 9.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. The handbrake. "I go, you stop" from lesson one to the last input, which the player may choose not to press, with Ron putting it on himself. It is a theme turned into a control, and it is the reason to play.
2. The Widow's rule: she keeps every deal, never lies when asked straight, and has never spoken to a police officer. "Yes. You didn't ask before" and "Nothing. That's what I can't get past" are the voice of the game.
3. The fare book as alibi and noose: two lines of one night, Sheila's kitchen, and her entry for the day he came. With the dockers filling the bench at the hearing, these make the town feel it owes him.
