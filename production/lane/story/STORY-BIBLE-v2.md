# LEDGER: the story bible, version 2

20 November 2026. Canon outranks this file; nothing here is built; canon.md and RULINGS.md are untouched. Spoken lines and Ledger lines are samples of the voice, not a script.

How to read it. Section 0 is a list for the owner and gives away every turn of the story; a reader who wants to meet the story as a player would should skip it and come back to it last. Sections 1 to 5 set out the story and its people as the player first meets them. Sections 6 to 9 are the story as scenes, in the order it is played, saying at each stage what the player believes. Section 10 is what was true all along. Sections 11 to 15 are the side stories, the retold scene, the tone, the pay-offs and the rules of play.

## 0. Changes to canon or rulings, for his ruling

Each with its reason and the plain alternative.

1. Ada is the head of what is left of the outfit. Canon has Ada as a widow at her window. Here she is Ada Jensen, widow of the man who founded the Hook's dockers' club; the envelope is made up on her table. For the first half of the game she appears to be one of the people the club pays. It changes one ruling (1 October): she never goes to the police; stood up for her tea she talks at the grocer's, and her talk brings Ellis. Why: the outfit needed a face the player knows by day, and "the town is watching" becomes a person. Alternative: Ada stays a neighbour and the tallyman is the last of the old firm.
2. Whose outfit. Canon: Mickey left Tom "a half-dead criminal outfit". Here it is a club with three seats (the gate, the row, the office); Mickey held the office and Tom inherits that seat, its book and its debts. Why: canon's line and the first-ask note can both be true, and the inheritance becomes an obligation. Alternative: the outfit is somebody else's and Mickey was its courier.
3. Maureen Jensen is Ada's daughter-in-law; her husband Alan, Ada's son, held the gate and drowned in 1986. The men he had robbed, and his wife, stood on the quay and did not save him, after Ada showed them what he had taken. Why: it gives the Widow and Ada a history and the last act a scene. Alternative: no relation, and Alan's death an accident nobody speaks of.
4. The man at the landing is named in week one: Cyril Blenkin, the tallyman. Alternative: he stays "the tallyman".
5. The warehouse fire. The ruling of 30 September stands as what the outfit believes: the importer stopped paying rent and was burned out as a lesson. Added underneath it, and found only in the last act: the importer had not stopped paying. Mickey owed Geoffrey Agar £4,000 against the yard, promised him the importer's plot, told Ada the man had refused, and wrote that lie in his own real book. Also added: the date (Saturday 18 November 1989); Mickey drove the two men himself and wrote the run in the fare book. Why: the mystery needed a truth that play uncovers late and that changes what earlier scenes meant, and it makes the plain fare book the true book. Alternative: the fire exactly as ruled, known in full at the midpoint.
6. Mickey's heart stands as ruled. Added: he wrote Tom's letter the night before; Sheila posted it. The stranger Ron argued with in the yard was one of the fire's two men, back for money.
7. More people speak of Mickey than the ruling of 30 September allows. Alternative: he is met only through his books and his letter.
8. Sheila's name for him has two steps: "Mr Nowak" when she trusts him with the real book, and "Tom" once, later, for something she finds out for herself. Alternative: one step, as built.
9. Tom's line (QUESTIONS 2): no hands on a woman, anyone of sixty or over, or the priest. Adopted as recommended. Added: other people offer to do it for him, and whether he lets them is his choice.
10. Tom's letter has words. Tom's Ledger is an old notebook with its first pages cut out, and its entries are in his own dry voice. Keith Garbutt found Tom for Mickey and never says where. Canon's rule holds throughout: nobody asks about his past and he never tells.
11. DS Ellis cannot be bought; her mother's name is in the club's book; one constable under her can be bought.
12. June Suddaby has nursed nights at the infirmary for eleven years and has stayed on Quay Street since the funeral. Philip Danby is the last master of the closed school, minding an empty building; nothing he says touches pupils.
13. The inspection has causes: the registration changed hands on Mickey's death; Tom's own wash; and a rival's telephone call. Mr Reese reads 1988 to 1990 as well.
14. Burn Both is shown as two closes. Three endings shown, five states beneath (QUESTIONS 1).
15. Small things minted: Agar's agents hold the parade's freehold; Friday is wages as well as rent; Tom's licence application is heard by a panel at the Harbour Board on Tuesday 20 November; Ron cannot drive; the names Walter and Alan Jensen, Les and Ray Coultas, Frank Cammack, Ivy Leadley, T. A. Reese.

## 1. The spine in a paragraph

In October 1990 Tom Nowak, a stranger with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter from an uncle he never met, takes over a minicab office in a port whose docks have died. The letter says: people are owed. The office turns out to be one seat in an old dockers' club that still pays forty-one families a little money every week, by means that were always crooked and ended last year in a warehouse fire. For two months Tom, with an old docker in his passenger seat and a bookkeeper who will not use his name, decides what a debt like that is worth: keep the forty-one paid and become the port's man, one racket at a time, while a widow, a ruined gentleman and a local lad each try to take the street from him; or shut the book and try to keep an honest firm and its people alive without it. Then a mild man from Customs and Excise opens the fare book, in which Mickey wrote down every fare he ever drove, and it turns out that the plainest book in the office is the only one that never lied. What the books hold, what the street believes and whether anyone still says his name with warmth decide whether Tom ends as the keeper of the Hook, a man with a name and a life, or a stranger with a suitcase again.

## 2. The theme

Every fare goes in the book. LEDGER is about what a place owes its people once the official books have written them off, and who gets to keep the account.

- The dock labour scheme ended in July 1989. Nobody in the game explains why. Everybody lives after it.
- The club's book is the old answer: forty-one names, paid weekly out of theft, rent taken by fear, and one fire.
- Each rival is another answer for the same port. The Widow: work for those who can, nothing for those who cannot. Agar: sell the quay to people with boats. Cammack: take it while it is going.
- The town keeps its own account of Tom and reads it back to him as a name: the new owner, Nowak, Tom, Tommy. To be known is to be owed and to owe.
- There are four books in the story (the fare book, Mickey's real book, the debt book, Tom's own Ledger) and a man from the state who asks which of them is true.

The story never says which answer is right. It makes every answer cost a named person something the player can see.

## 3. Tom Nowak

Tom is 32, the son of a Polish post-war settler and Mickey's sister. He has never been to the Hook. His life before it is unsaid: nobody asks and he never tells. He is not voiced.

The letter. In his hand in the first shot and pasted inside the cover of his Ledger:

"Tom. You don't know me. I was your mother's brother. I have a cab office on Quay Street in Meridian, two cars, and a good woman who keeps the books. It is yours when I am gone, and the doctor says that is sooner than I would like. There is more to it than cabs. There is a book. People are owed, and I have not always paid them the right way. Come and look before you say yes. Ask Sheila for the book when she is ready to give it. If you cannot stomach it, shut the door, and nobody will blame you, least of all me. Every fare goes in the book. M. Suddaby. P.S. Don't let them call you Mickey's nephew for ever."

His voice. Tom speaks aloud only in the words the player types or picks. Who he is lives in the Ledger, his notebook: a used one, hard-backed, with the first thirty pages cut out close to the spine with a blade. Nobody remarks on it. Page one is Monday 15 October. Every entry is his, short and dry, and he is funnier on paper than he lets anyone see:

- "Paid Dawn Pybus £38. Counted it twice in front of her. Don't know why."
- "Ada asked what I was doing in her alley. Said I was looking for a cat. There is no cat. She knows there is no cat."
- "Ron says rain by four. Ron has said rain by four every day. It has rained by four every day."
- "Sheila: 'new management' x6 today. A record."

What the entries count changes as he does. In week one they count money. By the middle they count people. The player can see who he is becoming by reading how he writes.

What he wants. A place that is his, and his own name said by people who mean it. The letter's postscript is the want. Each act gives it a job: make Friday and learn what "people are owed" means; do right by the forty-one, by paying them or by finding an honest way to stop; keep the book from drowning everyone whose name is in it. One small standing choice makes it visible: Shaun Hewson is repainting the parade's boards and wants to know what Mickey's is to say now.

His flaw. He asks nobody for anything. He pays for his tea when Grace says it is on the house; he counts money twice in front of people; he would rather carry a thing alone than owe a man for helping. It looks like pride and it is fear, and it is why nobody can get near him. Ron sees it by the second week: "You never ask for owt, do you. Mickey was the same. Dead at a desk with the kettle on."

What he needs. To learn that a name is a debt: to be known is to owe and be owed. The ending the design already had turns on "a friend's act nobody asked for". Tom's arc is to become a man people do that for, and at the very end one who can ask: the last day gives him a phone box, five coins, and five people to ask.

His line. He lays hands on no woman, nobody of sixty or over, and not the priest. The game never offers him the blow. It offers him other people's hands. Ron, of Agar: "He's sixty-one, boss. I'm fifty-eight. I'm allowed." Danny Cammack, of Ada, in the last week: "You can't touch her. I can." Whether Tom lets another man do what he will not is his choice, and the town remembers it as his.

What he fears. The suitcase: being nobody again, moving on with what he can carry. It stays packed under the bed in Mickey's flat until the player unpacks it, one press, whenever he likes. Sheila notices the day he does ("You've unpacked"), and the flat stops being Mickey's. And being known for more than he has shown: Keith Garbutt says once, "It took me two days to find you for him, which tells me something, and I'll not say what." He never does.

How each ending changes him. Each close ends on two lines: one Sheila writes in the fare book, and one Tom writes in the Ledger.

- The Kingdom: he becomes the keeper, Mickey and Ada in one coat. His last entries read like Mickey's: initials and sums.
- The Straight Life: he becomes a man with a name and no kingdom. His last entry is the first full sentence he has written about another person.
- Burn Both: he becomes what he was on the gangway. In one close the page is torn out; in the other it is left blank.

## 4. The people, as the player meets them

What each seems to be and plainly wants. What each is hiding is in section 10.

- Sheila Dunn, 53, the bookkeeper, at Mickey's since she was 22. Exactly as written: "New management", the walk-round, the fare-book rule, the locked door, the Sunday question. She wants the office to outlive Mickey and the fare book kept true. She is sizing him up, as she promised.
- Ron Kirby, 58, a docker for thirty years, on the door and the rank since the scheme ended. He wants to be of use the way he was when he had a hook and a gang. He calls Tom "boss", brings the asks, and cannot drive.
- Ada, 74, the widow at her window on Quay Street. Exactly as written: the window, one invitation, "I don't ask twice, mind". She seems to want company. On the Friday round Ron hands her an envelope like the others: "Walter Jensen's widow. First name in the book."
- Cyril Blenkin, 63, the tallyman at the landing. "Mickey's?" "Right. Wednesday, same again." He counts an envelope with his thumb, eyes on the water. He seems to be the one who runs it.
- June Suddaby, 34, Mickey's daughter, eleven years a night sister at the infirmary, staying at the boarding house since the funeral and "wanting nothing from the office". Nobody knows why she has not gone home.
- Alison Sedman, 31, the paper's port reporter, lodging above Ada's since August. She wants the warehouse fire, right, under her name; her editor wants it loud, soon and safe. She never burns a source: true things told to her are kept, and lies she finds out become the story.
- Philip Danby, 36, the last master of the closed school at the hill end of the Hook, kept on to mind the building and list its library before it is sold. A chessboard, a paraffin heater, a dry mouth. He wants the building kept, for anything.
- Father Brendan Walsh, the parish priest. He buried Mickey and counted who came.
- Darren Milner, 25, who sells nothing anyone can name and wants to be somebody's man.
- Pauline Kitching, 44, on the radio: the firm's running voice, heard in every cab at once. Kevin Needham, days, who wants his own plate. Terry Hodgson, nights, four words a shift.
- DS Carol Ellis, 44, born in the Hook, a year on the warehouse fire. She asks while a thing is answerable and tells once it is not.
- Keith Garbutt, the Fixer: a moustache, a car coat and a percentage.
- Mr T. A. Reese, 57, Officer of Customs and Excise, who comes at the end: a flask of tea, a bad knee, a propelling pencil. His father drove a cab. He is sorry, truly, when a book does not add up. He is the first person in the story who only wants to know what happened.

### Ron and Tom

The friendship of the game, written for the hands. Sheila judges Tom; Ron keeps him company.

- The passenger seat. Ron cannot drive: "Never needed to. The work was at the end of the street." So on any night Tom takes a car, Ron can ride. He names every building by what it was ("That was Stannard's ropewalk. That was the Seamen's Mission. That's still a wall"), and the player learns the map as a list of things that shut. He forecasts rain. He has a name for every man in his old gang (Tapper, Shiner, the Bishop) and a story for none of them that he will finish.
- The lessons. On Sunday mornings on the empty dock road Tom teaches a fifty-eight-year-old docker to drive car two. Three lessons across the game, each a few minutes with the hands: Ron at the wheel, the player beside him with nothing but his voice and the handbrake. Lesson one ends on a bollard. Lesson two, Ron drives the length of the dock at eleven miles an hour, talking the whole way. Lesson three comes after the midpoint and is not funny.
- Fridays. After the round, two of number nine from the Tangs', eaten on the quay wall. Ron pays. If Tom lets him, it is the first thing in the game he has let anyone give him.
- What it builds to. The Widow offers Ron the gate: a foreman again, his old gang under him. If the player has kept the passenger seat warm, Ron tells Tom himself, at the wheel in lesson three, and asks what he thinks. The best thing for Ron may be to let him go. If the player never rode with him, he is simply late one Friday and gone. Either way the seat is empty for a while, and Pauline says so on air: "Car one, you on your own?"
- How it pays off. In every ending Ron is driving something. Whose it is, and whether he looks across as he passes, is the verdict on the friendship.

## 5. The rivals

Three kinds of threat, kept: the Widow goes for his people, Agar for his clean money, Cammack for his cover. Each wants something of his own, each has a page in Mickey's real book, and each comes at him a different way. No rung is skipped unseen by the Widow or Agar. Cammack skips rungs, because that is who he is, and even his blows show a sign a game hour before they land. Their peaks come in turn, so the middle has a shape: the street (Cammack, up to the midpoint), his people (the Widow, after it), paper and hired hands (Agar, last), then the books.

### Danny Cammack: the street, by noise

26, born three streets from the office. Loud on purpose; quicker than he lets on. His crew sell what moves faster than a shift change; the game never shows what.

- Want: the Hook, and the end of the old lot. "You've got the keys. I've got the accent. One of us is in the wrong house."
- Why it is personal: his father Frank, a registered docker, had his hand crushed in 1981 and was struck off the club in 1984 on Alan Jensen's say-so, as a man who had talked. Danny grew up watching his father wait at the window on Fridays for an envelope that had stopped.
- His climb: the first Saturday he is on Quay Street buying chips and talking to Tom like an old friend in front of everyone. Then he takes Tom's cab, has him drive round the estate he grew up on, and pays an 80p fare with a fifty-pound note the float cannot change: "Keep it. Call it rent." Then, with no rung between, his crew burn car two on Bonfire Night.
- His page, and what it does: "F. Cammack struck off, A.J.'s word. Wrong man. Paid him since from my own." Mickey paid Frank quietly for six years, and Frank took it and hid it, and let his son hate the office that was feeding them. The page does not heal Danny. It takes away the one true thing he had. What he does with that is the end of his story (section 7, scene 17).

### Maureen Jensen, the Widow: his people, by offers

50, broad-shouldered, dressed like a harbour master. She took the gate when her husband drowned and ran it better. Since the scheme ended, sixty men stand at the dock gate at six each morning and she picks twenty.

- Want: every job at the gate through her hands, and the club's book closed. "A pension for men who'll never work is a tax on men who do." Eleven men on the list could still work, and will not sign with her while the four pounds comes. She wants Mickey's as the gate's cab firm.
- Why it is personal: Ada is her mother-in-law, and they have not spoken since the funeral in 1986. "I buried a husband and kept his business. His mother buried a business and kept her window."
- Her climb: she keeps every deal, so her ladder is a run of offers, each fair by her arithmetic, each worse than the last, each put once. She never threatens. She prices. A voice that is not Pauline's on the open radio: "Tell Mickey's nephew the gate sends its regards." A dawn fare to the gate with the engine running while she picks her twenty: "That's the scheme now. Me." Fifty pence on every fare through the gate. Then the list: work for the eleven, the parish for the rest. Then Ron. Last: Mickey's becomes the gate's firm, and he keeps the name on the roof and a wage.
- What he can do: accept at any rung; defy, and have his cars stopped at the gate; or counter with what he knows.

### Geoffrey Agar: his clean money, by the calendar and by other men's hands

61, silver, unhurried, the third generation of a family that holds paper on half the port. He never raises his voice and apologises before every blow.

- Want: the yard behind Mickey's, which is the only road to the old basin, where he means to put four hundred berths and a hotel. "They will call it Meridian Quays. I voted against the name."
- Why it is personal: he is Tom's landlord; the £38 for the office and flat goes to his agents every Friday. And Mickey owed him: "Mickey and I had an understanding once. It died before he did."
- His climb: letters, each with a date in it, so the player always knows what is coming and when. A rent review, "with regret". An objection to Tom's licence, to be heard on Tuesday 20 November. A solicitor's letter to June, offering to fund a claim on her father's estate. When paper is too slow he pays for hands: men in the jackets of a security firm, at night, with bolt cutters, clearing the yard gate and Mickey's mooring. They are under sixty and they are not women. Tom can fight them, outwit them, or have them filmed.
- His own scene: Tom takes the rent to the Harbour Board, where Agar keeps a room, and is left waiting outside a glass door. Inside, a younger man from a bank is saying the thirty-first of December in an ordinary voice, three times. Agar comes out, sees who has been sitting there, and apologises for the wait. He is ruined at the year's end if the basin is not his to sell, and from this scene on the player knows it.
- What he can do: sell him the yard; beat him on paper with the people who know forms; or counter with what he knows, which by the end is a great deal.

### The three together

They know each other. The Widow despises Cammack and buys from him. Agar needs the Widow's men off the basin and will not be seen with her. Cammack wants what both have. Keith Garbutt carries messages between them and sells Tom true reads of each. From the midpoint on they move against one another as well as against him, and what Mickey's book holds about each is worth more to the other two. Playing them off is something the player does with what he has found.

## 6. Act I: The Inheritance

Monday 15 to Sunday 21 October 1990. About two hours. The most scripted act. The beats of the approved first week stand; what is new is marked.

### Monday: the arrival and the first night

1. The ferry (0:00). First image, new: the letter in his hand over grey water, the last line readable: "Every fare goes in the book." Then the landing. Nobody meets him. Twenty seconds, skippable. He has £23.50.
2. Quay Street (1:30). A line each, in passing, as written: Darren, friendly and owing; Ron, "boss"; Ada at her window; June. New second half for June: "I don't want anything out of that office. I'd check what you're getting, if I were you."
3. Sheila's walk-round (1:45). Her six lines as approved. "New management." The fare book: "Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule, and it's mine now."
4. The first fare (2:30). New passenger: Nora Cronin with the priest's shopping, to the house by the chapel. Pauline gives the way by landmarks. New, Sheila's first test: she takes a driver's badge from the drawer. "Mickey's. Wear it till the council sits." If the player asks whether that is allowed: "No. Mickey wouldn't have asked." Nora, in the back: "Your uncle put a roof on that chapel twice and never once sat under it." At the door Father Walsh takes the bags and looks at him: "You missed a good funeral. Forty-one envelopes on the plate, and every one of them empty. They wanted him to know they'd come." No choice; a drive, and a line still with him at minute ten.
5. The rest of the office (4:30). The locked door ("Not today"), the flat, the debt book: five names, £156. New: the sum bites. Friday is rent, £38, and wages for four. Mickey's bank account is frozen until probate. An honest week's takings just cover the wages. The rent is the gap, and one night's carry is exactly the gap.
6. The first deed (5:30). Darren's £22: front him up, take what he offers, strike it out. Somebody sees.
7. The first ask (7:00). Ron, the coat, the envelope, as approved. New, for the player who types the obvious: "Who's it for?" "Tallyman. Counted cargo on that quay forty year. Now he counts this. That's all I'll say on a pavement, boss."
8. The carry (9:00). As written and as section 12 finishes it.
9. The landing (11:30). "Mickey's?" The thumb, the water. "Right. Wednesday, same again." A thin packet comes back and Ron counts £15 out of it for Tom: the carrier's money. Home by the yard. The tin. Sleep. First Ledger page.

### Tuesday to Thursday

As the designs have them: Darren says the night to his face; the count with Sheila; fares with wrinkles; Wayne's £30 in goods; Lee Drewery standing on the quay with people stopping to watch; a night with no ask; Ada's invitation; Julie Kettlewell's £9; the tea against the landing with her window on the street; "Nowak" on Thursday; Iveson's £80 three ways; Ellis if the street is loud; the two dockers by car two.

Added or changed:

- Ada on Tuesday morning: "What were you doing in my alley?" A nosy neighbour's question, and it stays that for five weeks.
- "How did Mickey die?" has an answer worth hearing. Sheila: "At that desk, at ten to six, with the kettle on and the book open. His heart. He'd been told. Ron found him at six. If you're after a story there isn't one. There's a man who didn't rest."
- Darren, the first time Tom asks anyone about it: "You're not going to go round asking who did for him, are you? Because nobody did. He just stopped. Like the docks."
- Alison in the cab on Wednesday, to the warehouse row. Changed: she does not mention a cab. "The watchman swore he walked his rounds. The brigade's times say nobody was on that row for the best part of an hour. I'd like to know where an hour went." She will quote his answer back to him, word for word, weeks later.
- At Iveson's yard on Thursday, among what he bought out of the burned building: desks, a clock, a box of office papers he has never looked through. A plant; nothing remarks on it.
- The first time Ron rides in the passenger seat is Thursday night, to the two dockers by car two. Afterwards, in the car: "You never ask for owt, do you."

### Friday: rent, wages and the round (new)

- Morning. Dawn Pybus with her clipboard, apologising. If he cannot pay she says who she collects for: Mr Agar's agents, in the Exchange.
- Noon. Wages by hand from the drawer: Pauline, Kevin, Terry, Ron. Each takes the packet with a line. Sheila will not take hers until the others are paid. If he is short he chooses who waits, and that person remembers.
- Night five, the third ask. Not an envelope: a holdall. The Hook's half of the club goes round by hand on Fridays, twenty doors, and Ron walks it. "You'll want to see where it goes, boss. Or you won't. Mickey always walked it." A carry with faces: Winifred Duggleby; Albert Suggitt; Ada, at her own front door, who takes hers like the rest and says "You'll have had your tea"; a door on Tannery Row where Ron says "Knock soft. Her girl's police"; and one door where Ron takes the packet himself: "Not that one. That's Mickey's own." The name on it is Cammack. Then two of number nine on the quay wall. Choice: walk it, send Ron alone, or refuse it. What the player now believes: the envelope is a pension, the tallyman runs it, and Ada is the first name on the list.
- If he said no on Monday: Ron walks a short round alone, and can be seen on the wall afterwards with one portion.

### Saturday: the match (new)

The street fills at three and at five. Danny Cammack is on it, loud, friendly, buying chips: the first rival, met as a mate. The boxing club behind the chandler's is open. At the landing Gordon Leckenby has a parcel: for years Mickey gave him letters to post from the far bank so they would not carry a Meridian postmark, and they have all come back to Gordon marked "not known". They are to June. Give them to her unopened, read them first, or leave them.

### Sunday: the first lesson, the question, the passenger

- Early. The empty dock road, car two, Ron at the wheel for the first time. The bollard.
- Ten o'clock. Sheila comes in on her day off, as written. "I don't come in Sundays." If she trusts him: "Mr Nowak." It is the first time she has used his name, and the real book is on the counter. "That's Mickey's real book. Everything he ran, in his own hand. You've had your week. So which is it going to be?" If not, the day-book, and "new management" still. Wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say; asked twice, as built. The street learns the answer.
- Night seven, the fourth ask: a passenger. If the arrangement stands, the tallyman is at the rank after ten and gets into Tom's cab. "Warehouse row. The far end." At the burnt plot he stands a while. "Your uncle stood here with me the morning after. He said, that's the last one, Cyril. It wasn't his to say." Then: "Forty-one go out. What comes back keeps the forty-one going out. That's all it ever was. Blenkin. You may as well have the name."
- If Tom has ended it, the passenger is June, late for her shift. "You've stopped Winifred Duggleby's coal money. Did anybody tell you that was what you were saying no to?" A pause. "He never could. I'm not saying you were wrong."

At the end of week one every player knows what the envelope is, has stood on the doorsteps or refused to, and has answered Sheila knowing what the answer costs. What he believes: an old club, run by an exact old man at the landing, did one bad thing last year. What he does not know: whose table, what the bad thing really was, and why Mickey sent for him.

## 7. Act II: The Rise and the Squeeze

Monday 22 October to Sunday 2 December. Eleven to fourteen hours. Scenes fire on states, never on sequence, with two exceptions the town itself names: the calendar's days (Bonfire Night, Remembrance Sunday, the fire's anniversary) and dates that arrive in letters. Those happen whether Tom is ready or not, so a slow player is pulled forward. Between the scenes runs the game as designed: fares, jobs, the count, the board, recruits, side stories. No week passes without one scene that costs somebody something.

Two roads run through the same weeks. The keeper's road (K) is for the player who took it over or would not say. The straight road (S) is for the player who wound it down. Where a scene differs, both are written.

The act asks one question in each half. Before the midpoint: can he make it his? After it: whose book is it?

### First half

Scene 1. Monday's wages. The office, before the first call. Pauline: "Am I still wanted, then?" Kevin and Terry are listening and Sheila is not looking up. At stake: four people's work, and what Sunday's answer means to them. Choice: what he tells them, and whether it is true. Comes back: by noon the gate has it word for word, because Kevin said it there.

Scene 2. The long way round (week two's cost). Kevin's day fares come in short against his mileage; Sheila sees it in the book before anyone says it. Followed, he is carrying parcels for the gate in Mickey's car, on a promise: the Widow will get him a plate of his own. Fronted on the rank, in front of the street, he does not deny it: "Twelve years I've driven his car. Twelve years. She asked me what I wanted." Choice: sack him there (a car without a driver, and the drivers learn the new owner reads the book); keep him and feed the gate what Tom wants it to hear; take a cut; or pay the gap and say nothing. Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990. Staged that night if the firm has reached its second rung (K) or lasted that long (S); otherwise on the first evening after. "Then I'll ask Darren."

What comes back: Terry is on June's ward. Hal Metcalfe was filming the fireworks from his shop door. Ellis has a burned car on Quay Street and a reason to stay. From tonight Ada says "Tom", before Sheila ever has.

### Second half

Scene 10. The third lesson. The Sunday after. Ron drives Tom to the infirmary to see Terry, and back, without being told a thing. Ron's own hands are bandaged. He does not talk. At the dock road he stops the car properly, handbrake on, and says: "She's offered me the gate, boss." (If the player has not ridden with him, this scene does not happen, and scene 13 arrives cold.)

Scene 11. Remembrance Sunday, 11 November. The chapel. Father Walsh reads the port's dead: the wars, and by the Hook's custom the men the docks have taken since. Alan Jensen. This year, Michael Suddaby. Everybody is in one room for the only time in the game: Ada, out of her house; Maureen across the aisle; Agar with the Harbour Board's wreath; Frank Cammack and his ruined hand, Danny standing at the back; Ellis beside her mother; Sheila; June. Two minutes of silence in which the player chooses only where Tom looks, and sees who looks back. At Alan Jensen's name six men of Ron's age look at the floor. Afterwards, at his kitchen table, Father Walsh says the one thing he may: "Your uncle asked me, sat there, whether a man can repent of a thing and go on paying for it out of the profits. Scene 12 (K). The lesson. The chandler stops paying the parade's rent and says why, in his own doorway, to Tom's collector, with customers in the shop. Ada: "The row wants a lesson." This is the thing of the kind Mickey swore was his last, and it is now Tom's to decide the size of: a window put in by hand; a beating he sends and does not watch; a fire; or the chandler's share paid quietly out of his own tin so that nobody learns a man refused and nothing happened. Scene 12 (S). Where Ada's money comes from, and the meeting. The forty-one are still being paid, around him. Scene 13. The gate, and Ron. Her second and third offers. Ron's old gang stand him a breakfast. Scene 14. The anniversary, and the night of the book. Sunday 18 November, a year to the day. Norman Dent has stopped sleeping. Darren offers to sell what he knows about the watchman. (K) The real book cannot be in the office tomorrow. ## 8. Act III: The Ledger Comes Due

Monday 3 to Saturday 15 December. Two to three hours.

### Why the inspector comes

Three causes, and the player can read all three.

1. The name on the form changed. When a registered trader dies, Customs and Excise look at the business before they register the next owner. Every player gets the letter, whatever he built.
2. Tom's own wash. Every week the books were off is a week read twice. Takings a firm this size could not have earned are not a tax owed; they are real money from an unreal source, and a reason to set fuel and mileage against the book and, if they do not fit, to pass a copy along the corridor to people who are not polite.
3. A telephone call from whichever rival came out of Act II hurt and standing. Agar names this year's takings and the dead man's badge, and nothing earlier, for reasons the player learns on Wednesday. The Widow names the drivers' sheets, which Kevin copied. Cammack names a cab firm's man seen carrying cash to the landing at night. Reese's first questions show which, and the Ledger says so.

The letter comes on Monday 3 December and names Monday the 10th. It is courteous. It is signed T. A. Reese.

### The week before

One job. Ada wants the real book back on her table before any man from the government sits in that office. Whoever holds it (scene 14) gives it up or does not; if Alison has it, she has read it. Around that: the Widow bids openly for his drivers; Agar's agents serve notice on the yard; Garbutt asks Tom for a meeting, for the first time, and names his favour. Les Coultas can put Garbutt's name to the fire as well as Mickey's. Garbutt wants Les kept across the water and will pay in the only coin he has: a true read of all three rivals, free, and one truce.

### Five mornings, five nights

The days are Reese at the counter. Each night is one job.

Monday. The item: the fare book since October, with the drivers' sheets. Tom's own wash, read aloud by a mild man with a pencil. If Kevin's sheets are with the Widow, they are missing. Night: get them back from the gate hut. A break-in on the docks; or Ron's word, if Ron has the gate; or a deal.

Tuesday. The item: fuel and mileage. "I like to ride a route or two. Mileage is a truthful thing." Reese rides in car one all morning with his flask, and Tom drives (section 13). Night: Ada asks to be driven. She has not been in a car since 1986. "The dock gate." Maureen gets in at the gate. Two widows in the back of a minicab, the meter running, Tom's eyes in the mirror. They talk first about the list. Then Maureen asks what she has had four years to ask. "Cyril gave you his tally on the Monday. Alan went in on the Saturday." Ada: "I didn't put him in. I showed the men what he'd had off them. Then the water did what the water does." Maureen: "And I stood there with them. That's the only thing you and I ever did together." If the player has already had it from Gordon Leckenby, who pulled Alan out and counted seven people standing still, he can say it before she does. Tom may speak once. What he says, and what he has done with the list, decides whether they make a peace, a war or a sale. What the player now knows: the club pays its own, and it lets its own drown. He holds its office.

Wednesday. The item: the account customers, 1988 to 1990. Reese goes quiet over two things and makes two small marks. First, the importer's account, six runs a week, written up every week until the eleventh of November 1989. "A good payer, this one. Right up to the week of his fire." Second, a column of Saturday-night runs from the warehouse row to a cottage and back, no charge, beginning in September 1989. "Your uncle was a generous man on Saturdays." Night: the row. Norman Dent, asked, says what he has understood for a year: Mickey's cab took him home on his break every Saturday that autumn, a kindness, and on the night of the fire no cab came, so he walked, and was gone twice as long. At Iveson's yard, in the box of office papers out of the burned building, is the importer's rent card, initialled by Mickey every week to the eleventh of November. The importer never stopped paying. Mickey told Ada that he had. What is in the real book is what Mickey wrote for Ada to read. Late that night there is a knock at the office. Geoffrey Agar has walked down Quay Street for the first time in his life, in the rain, without a hat. He does not threaten. He asks. He lent Mickey four thousand pounds against the yard; in September of last year Mickey came to him and said the importer's plot could come free; he said he could not possibly ask how; he bought it in the spring for a third of its worth. "I have a wife who believes we are selling the house to be nearer her sister. I should like her to go on believing it until January." He wants one line in a fare book left alone or lost. Ron, if he is there, looks at Tom.

Thursday. The paper is on every counter by eight; Alison brought him the proof the evening before and asked one question, and what he answered is what has run. The item: "The book for the quarter to December 1989, if you'd be so kind." Sheila fetches it. Reese turns to Saturday 18 November and reads, in Mickey's hand: twenty to midnight, car two, landing to warehouse row and return, two fares, wait, no charge. "Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak." He takes the books away for the night, locked in the boot of his car outside Mrs Rennison's. Midday: Ellis, at the burnt plot. Her trade is honest: a rival and a page, and she closes the fire and leaves the rest; the price is that the street learns who talks. The card and the line give her Agar. The lock-up and Terry's hands give her Cammack. The gate gives her the Widow. He can refuse and still reach the best ending through what the town believes. He can put her mother's name to her; she answers by offering to hand the file up to people who know nobody in the Hook. Night: the landing, after ten, the walk of the first night with an envelope of his own. Les Coultas is waiting where the tallyman stands. Paid, or frightened, or simply asked, he says the last of it: "He come over to us in the September. Two month before. Had it all worked out, which night, the watchman's break, the lot. Said there was an old girl who'd need to think it was her idea." Then what to do with one line in a book in a car boot: leave it; alter it (a car to open, a pen, and a man who notices ink; Sheila will not sit beside a book that has been scratched); give it to Ellis first; or burn it, a second fire, after which the books cannot hold. And where Les goes: back over the water paid, into Ellis's hands, or into Garbutt's.

Friday. Reese has the office from nine, and the telephone in it is the one everybody hears. Before the calls, Sheila: "He wrote that letter the night before. I found it by his hand, sealed. I posted it. I've asked myself every night since whether he'd have torn it up by morning." Then the phone box on the corner and the coins in his pocket: five calls, for a man who asks nobody for anything. Each is one request: a word to Reese, a statement changed, a door held, a car moved, a debt called in. Who picks up, and what they say before he has finished asking, is the campaign he played. Back in the office the player watches what he caused: Reese takes a word, in front of Tom, from whoever has come in (Sheila always; then a driver, Edith Stockill with a folder, a constable with a message from Ellis, or nobody). Only then does he close his notebook. "That all seems to be in order, Mr Nowak." Or: "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak."

Signs before every cost, as designed: Reese's manner, Sheila's words, Ellis asking and then telling, the remark that stops as he passes, the friend's unasked act, the successor's offer and its withdrawal.

## 9. The endings

States, never a menu. Three shown, five beneath, in the built order. Each has Ada's last scene, a last scene of its own, a line that says who he became, the fate of everyone who worked for him, where the truth about Mickey has come to rest, and a last image: a line written in the fare book, and under it a line in Tom's Ledger.

### Ada's table, in every ending

Friday evening, her front room, the tin on the table. He can tell her what Mickey did, or keep it. Told, she sits a long time. "Thirty-seven years I've known when a man was lying to me from that window. He sat in that chair." Then, by what the player is to her: she gives him the tin; or she puts it away; or she asks him to leave and stands at the glass until he has gone. Not told, she dies believing the fire was hers, and Tom is the only one who knows it was not. Either is a thing he chose.

### The Kingdom

Reached when the business stands and the books hold.

The last scene. Saturday 15 December, after ten, the landing under the customs lamp. A carrier in a dark coat that does not fit him walks the envelope down the lit quay past the constable, who looks at the coat and looks away. The tallyman steps up and asks, for the first time: "Nowak's?" Across Quay Street Ada's window is dark and her chair is gone from it. The camera climbs to the flat above the office. A figure at the glass. Tom has the watch.

Below, Ron brings car one in off the rank, by himself, and parks it straight. Sheila locks the office. On the plain turn she says, "Goodnight, Mr Nowak," and has given her notice for the end of the quarter; June has gone back over the water; the forty-one are paid by rounds and fronts with nothing of a club left in them; Agar is his, held by a rent card; Les is across the water on a wage. Ada, earlier, from her back room: "Mickey kept the book. My Walter kept the street. You'll do for both."

On the better turn (the built Both: one friend counts him, the street does not hold his rackets as fact, Ellis's case is answerable, the books hold and make sense), Sheila stops at the door. "Night, Tom." A careful first player can reach it. It is hard, not hidden.

Last image. The fare book: "22.10. Landing. Account." The Ledger, in a hand that has started to look like Mickey's: "41 out. T.N."

### The Straight Life

Reached when no business of his stands at the close and one friendship holds.

The better turn (the built Quiet: handed to someone who can hold it, never while hunted for a killing). Friday, the chapel hall. The Quay Street Friendly Society pays out for the first time, in the open. Edith Stockill has the pen. "Sign there, dear. And there." Forty-one signatures, Ada's first, in a book anybody may read. Father Walsh and June are trustees. Agar is in Ellis's hands or bought off with his own yard; the Widow has her eleven and has shaken on the rest; Les has given his statement or gone. Then the three days of watching, as built: Julie Kettlewell has the firm, with Ron driving days, and the board, the rank and the radio hold without Tom or do not. On the last evening he gets into the back of car one. Ron is at the wheel. "Where to, boss?" "Just round." Pauline, on air: "Car one's got the old gaffer. Be nice." At the office Sheila writes the fare herself. "Mind how you go, Tom."

Last image. The fare book: "Nowak, T. Round the Hook. 80p. Paid." The Ledger: "Ron drove. He didn't hit anything. Told him so."

Somebody passing says, "Morning, Tom." The fare book's last line: "Closed." The Ledger: "Stayed."

### Burn Both

Reached when nothing else fits. Two closes.

The Reckoning, for the man who went for both and lost them. Friday: "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak." Ellis has stopped asking. Sheila is asked for her keys by a constable half her age and hands them over without looking at Tom. Ron is on the pavement with his coat on and nowhere to be. Saturday night is the first night again: the quay after ten, the lamp, no envelope. The constable turns round. He does not call "You there." He calls, "Nowak!" The top of the name ladder from the wrong side: everybody knows who he is. The player has the two inputs of night one, stand or run, and the close cuts on the press. After the credits the town is the Hook on the morning he is bailed: Customs' seal on the door, the forty-one unpaid, Ada's window lit and nobody at it. The fare book: Sheila closes it unwritten and puts it in the drawer with Mickey's. The Ledger: the last page torn out.

The Suitcase, for the man who let it drift. No judgement, because nobody thinks enough of him to hold one. A grey afternoon. He walks down the gangway he walked up. Darren asks him for a pound. Ron, driving the gate's van, passes and does not look across. Ada watches from her window the whole way, as she did when he came. The fare book: "Ferry landing. One. No charge." The same charge Mickey wrote on the night of the fire. The Ledger: a date, and nothing under it.

### What every ending settles

Where the fire-night line is; who holds the forty-one; who still has work (Sheila, Ron, Pauline, the drivers, Terry's hands); where each rival and Les Coultas stand; who knows what Mickey did (Ada, June, Ellis, the paper, or nobody but Tom); and what Ada is doing at ten at night. No ending redeems what was done. Reloading is never stopped.

## 10. What was true all along

The truth in the order the player finds it. The street never has it: the street says the importer burned his own warehouse for the insurance and went south.

Layer one, week one: the club. In 1953 Walter Jensen, a tally clerk, started a sick club on the quay with a tin and a notebook. Under the dock scheme it lasted and grew a second purse: cargo short-tallied at the gate, held on the row, moved in cabs and sold; a weekly "rent" from every business on the row and the quay so that nothing of theirs went missing. Most of the money went back out in envelopes. Three seats: the gate (Walter, then his son Alan), the row and the watch (Ada), the office and the book (Mickey, from 1962). When the scheme ended the gate stopped paying in, the rent dried up, and forty-one names were left: £4 a week each, half on each bank. The far bank's half crosses by envelope to the tallyman; what its few payers still give comes back; the carrier gets a flat £15.

Layer two, the midpoint: whose table. Ada's. She has run what is left since Walter died in 1979. Cyril works for her. She let her upstairs room to the reporter to keep her where she could be watched. The first tea was an interview.

Layer three, the midpoint: the fire, as Ada believes it and as Mickey's real book tells it. The importer stopped paying; she said the row needed a lesson; Mickey got two men from across the water through Keith Garbutt; on Saturday 18 November 1989 the warehouse burned, and nobody was hurt because the watchman was away.

Layer four, Tuesday of the last week: Alan. In March 1986 Cyril gave Ada his tally of what her son had taken from the club. She showed it to his own gang. On the Saturday he went into the dock between a ship and the quay, and six men and his wife stood still. Gordon Leckenby pulled him out too late. That is the thing the Widow and Ada share and cannot speak of, and it is what the club is.

Layer five, Wednesday and Thursday: Mickey. For a year he had been making up the club's shortfall, and his own, with money borrowed from Agar against the yard. In September 1989 he went to Agar and said the importer's plot could come free. He began sending a cab for the watchman every Saturday. He went on collecting the importer's rent and initialling the man's card every week, wrote in the real book from October that it had stopped, told Ada the man had refused, and let her give the word. He met the two men off the ferry himself. He wrote the lie in the real book, where Ada would read it, and the truth in the fare book, where nobody would look: the run, the waits, the free Saturdays. Whether he wrote it there to be found is the one thing nobody can know. Then he could not live with it: he told the priest, told Ada it was the last, paid the two men twice when they came back for more, and sent for a stranger. Agar did not order the fire. He knew, said nothing, and profited.

Layer six, Friday: the letter. Les Coultas came to the yard a third time on 22 September 1990 and Ron put him out. Mickey wrote to Tom the next night and died at the desk before six. Sheila found the letter sealed and posted it. Why the nephew: June would have shut the book the day she got it, and Mickey could neither shut it nor hand it to her.

## 11. Side stories that are whole stories

The thirty-two drafts of 8 November stay as the short ones, found on foot. Eight are grown here into whole stories: a person with a want, a turn the player does not see coming, and an end the town keeps. Each runs across acts, brings its people back, and touches the main story without being needed by it. Most of the forty-one are met this way first.

1. The watch (Ron). Found: Mickey's watch in Rita's window the week he died. Rita will not say who pawned it until it is redeemed; Hal knows; there is a florist's bill. Turn: Ron pawned it himself, for the funeral flowers, because the office was frozen and he would not ask Sheila. He wanted Mickey to have had a proper send-off from somebody. Ends: redeemed and handed back without a word; asked about, and he is ashamed; or left in the window for the street to see. The town keeps: what kind of boss Tom is to a proud man.

2. Nothing moves but me (Norman Dent). Found: the watchman on the row, glad of company and a flask of tea. Over three acts: his round; the lights in an empty warehouse that are Cammack's crates; the anniversary, when he stops sleeping; Darren's price. Turn one: he was not paid to be away, as the detective has believed for a year. Turn two, which he has told nobody: he was not away by chance either. Mickey Suddaby gave him a lift home on his break every Saturday that autumn, and he thought it was the kindest thing anyone on that row had ever done for him. Ends: Tom stands beside him while he tells Ellis; buys his silence and keeps him his cottage; or uses him. The town keeps: whether the new owner spends an old man.

3. One way (Karen Grummitt). Light. The ferry's ticket clerk wants a car at five on Thursday, with her case, and not a word to Mrs Rennison, whom she owes a fortnight. The case is a case, a wardrobe and a budgerigar. A cab with a wardrobe roped to the roof must pass Sylvia Rennison's front step, where Sylvia sits in her slippers from four. Turn: Karen does not get on the boat. She stands at the rail she sells tickets for and cannot do it. Ends: he drives it all back, past the step again, and Sylvia says only "Friday means Friday"; or pays the fortnight and Karen goes; or tells Sylvia. The town keeps: the wardrobe, twice, and a postcard that comes to the ticket window or does not.

4. Two of number nine (the Tangs). Lily remembers Mickey's Friday order. Peter says the shouting at closing time is nothing, as he always does. Steps: be at the counter at closing, three nights; the young men are Danny's hangers-on. Turn: Danny stops it himself, in a sentence, the night he sees Tom standing there, then tells the street he did. "My dad ate here before you could find it on a map." Ends: let him have the credit; say who stood there first; or it was Ron. On the keeper's road it crosses scene 8: the man standing at her counter at closing is also the man whose collector calls on Monday, and Lily knows it. The town keeps: who the Hook thinks protects it. Racism here is a fact about the men, never voiced, never rewarded.

5. Lilies (Winifred Duggleby). The chapel flowers; her husband's anniversary; "nobody gives lilies any more". Steps: the market in Copper Row by a car from the rank; the altar; Sunday. Turn: in a short week she tells him, not knowing who he is in it, that "the bit from the club" did not come, that she has gone without before and will not make a song of it. Ends: he makes it up from his own tin and does not write it down (Sheila finds it in the count; this is one of the acts that earns her "Tom"); he does not; or he tells her whose envelope it is. The town keeps: a kindness told after the service, or a woman who now knows what her coal money is made of.

6. The tape (Hal Metcalfe). Hal keeps a coin shop that sells no coins, "for the company", and filmed the fireworks on the Fifth with the only camcorder in town. Want: to be asked round. Nobody has asked Hal anything since he retired. Steps: he invites Tom to watch it, with tea and a plate of biscuits arranged in advance. The tape shows Cammack's lads at car two; then, as the camera swings, two figures in Ada's window; then four minutes of Hal's own feet. Turn: Danny, Ellis, Alison and Ada each want it, for different seconds of it, and for one week Hal Metcalfe is the most visited man in Meridian and happier than he has been in years. He will give it to whoever stays for a second cup. Ends: bought, copied, given, or wiped by Hal himself the day he understands what he has. The town keeps: a picture in somebody's hands, and whether Tom still calls on Mondays when there is nothing to get.

7. The master and the building (Philip Danby). A light in the closed school at night. Steps: the library list; Agar's surveyor measuring the yard; the hall lent for a meeting; chess, at which Philip is merciless and apologetic. Turn: he has had a post inland in his pocket for a month. He is not staying for the building. He is staying because leaving feels like agreeing. Ends: the hall becomes the society's and he stays; the building is sold and he goes, leaving Tom the chess set; or Tom tells him to take the post. The town keeps: whether one more person who could leave the Hook did.

8. Mickey's mooring (Gary Mawer, the Widow, Agar). A mooring at the yard in Mickey's name, three months unpaid, with no boat on it. Gary wants the bill paid; the Widow's man asks about the berth the same week; so does Agar's surveyor. Turn: there was never a boat. The mooring carries the one right of way across Mickey's yard to the old basin, and Mickey rented it so that nobody else could. It is why the yard matters to everyone and what the men with bolt cutters come for. Ends: keep it, sell it to one or the other, or let it lapse and watch them race. The town keeps: who now thinks Tom is in the boat trade.

## 12. The scene in the first half hour a player retells

The carry, minute nine, kept as written. Three routes with honest costs: the lit quay, quick and seen; the yard and the lane behind the warehouses, dark and slow; the cab, quickest, with the firm's name on its roof. The constable on his beat can turn round. Next morning the woman at the window asks what he was doing in her alley, and Darren says it to his face.

Finished here: what happens if the constable takes the envelope. He calls out; Tom stands; the constable opens it. The player sees the constable's face, then a glimpse: small packets, a name pencilled on each. He reads them for longer than a search needs. He is honest, so he keeps it and takes it to the station; Ellis has it by morning. Nobody comes for Tom with a hammer. Sheila, at the count: "Twenty people over the water are short this week. You don't know what that means yet." Ron: "It'll want making good, boss." And in Mickey's debt book, under the five names, Sheila writes a sixth in her own hand: "Nowak, T. £82." The new owner's first week ends with his own name among the debtors. A failed carry is the fastest way in the game to learn what the envelope is.

The second retold scene is the tea, minute thirty-nine, kept exactly. Nothing in it or around it points past a lonely woman with a good view. On a second playthrough it is a different scene.

## 13. Humour and the mix of tones

Grounded noir carries the main line. The comedy is dry, local and attached to people, with seaside-postcard cheek used sparingly. Never satire of the American kind. With no drink in the game, the pub's jobs are shared out.

- The radio is the bar. Pauline's commentary goes to every cab at once and is the game's running joke and its chorus. "Car one, the chapel, and the lady says not the way you went last time." "Kevin, wherever you are, you are not in Copper Row, because I can hear the gulls." "Two real minutes, love."
- The caff is the snug. Grace Rinaldi bars, forgives and feeds. Brenda Laverack does the postcard lines: "Thirty years with my hands in cold fish. My Derek says it shows."
- Tom's Ledger is the straight man. He writes down what nobody would say aloud. "Mrs Rennison booked a car to go forty yards with a ham. Asked no questions. The ham had a seat."
- Ron's driving lessons are the game's physical comedy, and they are allowed to stop being funny.
- Darren is the clown with a cost. Forty transistor radios off the back of a lorry, every one of which picks up only the pirate station, which is playing one record all week. By Thursday the whole parade can hum it. They were Cammack's.
- Three set pieces built for laughing. Karen's wardrobe. Pauline's hour: she takes her first lunch out in nine years and Tom has the radio; the drivers test him at once (Kevin reports himself at the lights on Weighhouse Lane while eating a sandwich in plain view outside the office window), a caller will give her name only to Pauline, and Sheila corrects every call he makes without looking up from the book. And Mr Reese's ride-along: Brenda Laverack gets in with a box of live crabs that does not stay shut ("Are those declared, Mrs Laverack?" "They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in"), Darren tries to sell a Customs officer a radio, Mrs Rennison and the ham, and everything that happens is perfectly legal.
- The dry line is everybody's. Sheila: "Sit down, you're making the place look untidy." The tallyman: "You're four minutes early. I'll not hold it against you twice." The Widow paying what is on the meter to the penny. Agar's letters, each sorrier than the last. Reese: "I've never met a cab firm with a tidy mileage book. I live in hope."

The mix is kept by the evening's own shape: an easy day, a tightening dusk, the job, the relief, the count, and next morning the sting or the laugh. The darkest scenes (the second tea, the two widows, Agar in the rain, Thursday at the landing) are played quiet and never joked across. The lightest sit in daylight, in the weeks before the Fifth and again between the hearing and the letter, so the game has somewhere to fall from.

## 15. How play carries it

- Hands first. Every scene is a fare, a carry, a round, a tail, a break-in, a fight, a drive, a lesson or a count, with talk where the consequence lands. The last week is five nights of jobs behind five mornings of paper.
- Nothing is on rails. Scenes fire on states; the calendar's days and the letters' dates belong to the town. Every turn of the truth can be found early by a player who follows the right person: Ron at dusk, Cyril home from the landing, Gordon's story, the box at Iveson's.
- No game over. A lost envelope is a debt. A lost fight is Grace Rinaldi's kitchen. A refused licence is a harder game. A burned book is an ending, shown coming.
- Tom cannot die. A car hurts and never kills. Nobody is killed by the story; a killing is the player's doing, rare and permanent.
- Guns are rare and late and come only through people: the dial first moves if Tom takes Ada's answer on the Fifth.
- Talk decides nothing alone. Every answer that cannot be undone is asked twice.
- No drink, no gambling, no children, anywhere. The bonfire and the bout are grown people only and nothing is staked. Nobody in the game speaks of real people or real news.
- What is asked of the systems, and how plainly each is met. The hearing: each speaker says one written line chosen by what the town's memory holds of Tom, in an order the Core sets; no new system. Two passengers: a written scene with one live choice. The ride-along: an ordinary morning of fares with a fixed passenger. Five calls: five written exchanges chosen by state. The lessons: the car's own handling with the roles swapped. The Friday round: a carry with twenty doors.
