# LEDGER story bible, version 4: script editor's note

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8

1. Tom: 8. Want, flaw and fear are each something the player does, and the Ledger gives a silent lead a real voice. His line and his need are stated more cleanly than any one scene tests them.
2. The cast: 8. Sheila, Ron, Pauline and Terry, Reese and Ivy are people players would talk about; Terry's fifth word is the best beat in the bible. Too many names have a job and no face, and Alison is nearer the stock reporter than the rest.
3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow, who prices and never threatens, is a fresh antagonist, and Danny's page is a fine turn. Agar's motive goes soft in the last week, his "hands" are hired men in jackets, and the true author of the fire is dead, so nobody alive can be confronted for it.
4. The central mystery: 7. It has a truth, it is found with the hands, and it changes what the letter, "that's the last one, Cyril" and Ada's confession meant. It rests on one fact the bible has not squared: Ron collected the importer's money every week and the whole outfit believed the man had refused.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The badge, the priest's forty-one empty envelopes, the sum that bites and the carry make a strong first two hours; a failed carry that puts "Nowak, T." in the debt book is what players retell.
6. Structure and pace: 7. The calendar and the rivals' peaks in turn shape Act II. Act III crams Agar, Les, Ellis, Alison's proof, the altered book, Sheila's page, the run and Ada's table into three to four hours: the hurried last act the bar warns of.
7. The middle: 8. It turns twice, at the Fifth and at the anniversary, and both roads have work for the hands. The four dig scenes all end on "in his hand", which will read as a checklist by the third.
8. Surprise against cliché: 6. The old woman at the window who runs it all, the rival framed by an obvious crime, the dead benefactor who did it, the reporter love interest and the developer with a marina are all familiar pieces. The arrangement is clever (Mickey did to Ada what Ada did to Tom) but a genre-wise player will call two of the three turns.
9. The endings: 8. Every close has a last scene and a two-line last image, and the echoes ("Nowak!", "No charge", "Ron takes his test in March") are earned by play. The Kingdom's dark window and missing chair leave Ada's fate unclear in a way that reads as a gap, not a choice.
10. The side stories: 8. Ivy and her daughter, each protecting the other from a thing both know, is a whole story in a page; Ron's watch and Norman's walk are close behind. The Tangs' story leans on the main plot to have a turn at all.
11. Tone and humour: 8. The comedy comes from people and set pieces (Pauline's hour, the crabs, the ham with a seat, eleven miles an hour) and is allowed to turn. The Ledger lines are genuinely funny.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The offers paying off at the hearing and the phone box is the best design in the bible. The climax is mechanically a timed fetch for paperwork, and Act III's state matrix is far larger than section 15 admits.

## 2. The five weakest points

### 1. Ron must have known the importer paid

What is wrong. The outfit believes the importer was burned for refusing to pay (section 0, item 5). Ron collected that rent every Monday until 13 November 1989 (scene 17, "the big fellow with the docker's hook"). So for five weeks before the fire Ron was carrying money from a man everyone in the club was told had stopped paying, and for a year after it he has said nothing. The bible treats his answer ("What got wrote after, I never saw") as clearing him. It does not. It makes him the one man who could have stopped the fire or exposed the lie, and did neither.

Why it matters. The anniversary asks the player to believe his friend before he can prove it. A sharp player will ask the obvious question, and the bible has no answer.

Fix. Make it Ron's guilt instead of a hole. He did ask; Mickey told him the Monday money was old arrears, and to leave it. Ron took Mickey's word, as Cyril did, and has known since the fire that it did not add. He says so at the wheel after the anniversary: "I knew it didn't add, boss. I've known a year." Believing Ron then means believing a man who kept quiet out of loyalty, which is harder and better, and rhymes with Sheila adding up round a line for a year.

### 2. The truth lands before the climax, and the climax is a fetch

What is wrong. The two books are compared on the Monday of Act III (section 8, "The week before"). From then on the mystery is solved. The Friday run, the last thing the player does, is ringing round for Kevin's sheets, Ron's pocket book and Edith's folder. Meanwhile Act III carries a night for Agar, a night for Les, Ellis's trade, Alison's proof, the boot of Reese's car and Sheila's page.

Why it matters. The climax should test what the story built. This one tests his friendships (good) but not what he now knows about Mickey, and the fire's dead author cannot be faced.

Fix. Move Les Coultas to the anniversary, when the dig is hottest. Then put the truth in the run: Reese's 1989 item can be answered with Ellis's note, or with Mickey's cut page from Sheila that morning. The page clears Tom and puts Mickey's name, and Ada's part, on a government file the street will hear of; keeping it means finding Ellis by five. The last choice becomes the truth against the people it would hurt.

### 3. Agar's ruin does not add up

What is wrong. A third-generation man who "holds paper on half the port" is selling his house over a £4,000 handshake (section 8, Tuesday). He also says he needs the book because nothing was in writing, which sounds as if he wants to claim the debt, while everything else says he wants his pages buried. And after the hearing his threat becomes men with bolt cutters, the most generic thing in the bible.

Why it matters. He is a rival and the main red herring. If the player cannot tell what he wants from the book, his scene in the rain loses its weight.

Fix. Agar lent Mickey money that was not his, from a client account, and the bank's thirty-first of December is when the hole is found. He needs the pages destroyed because they show the interest he took and his vote on the plot. Give his "hands" a face: his surveyor, at the yard gate on the Friday.

### 4. Two of the three turns are guessable

What is wrong. The midpoint reveal that the widow at the window runs the club (section 7, the Fifth, beat 4) is a known turn, and the bible frames it with the tallyman standing "the way a man stands in the house of the person he works for", which is staged rather than found. Danny's innocence is almost announced: "Nobody believes him, because nobody would. He is telling the truth." In play the loud rival blamed for an obvious crime is the first man a player clears.

Why it matters. A plot the player has seen before is punished, and the real surprise, that Mickey wrote the lie, needs a player not already braced for it.

Fix. Let Ada's headship be findable early (section 15's back gates allow it), so the Fifth's surprise is that she had the firm's own car burned. Make Danny look guilty on evidence: a yellow car on Quay Street at nine that night, Darren known to have run errands for him. Then the tape overturns evidence, not just reputation.

### 5. Set-ups without pay-offs and pay-offs without set-ups

What is wrong. Agar's solicitor offers June money to contest the estate (section 5) and it never returns. June stays at the boarding house "and nobody knows why" (section 4); her three envelopes explain her money, not her room. "The round's twenty this week, not twenty-one" never says whose packet was dropped. Julie Kettlewell inherits the firm in the Straight Life but is set up only on the keeper's road (scene 3, K). The man at the counter in scene 8 is unnamed, so it is unclear whether he is Les or the brother nobody sees. Garbutt goes on paying the Coultas brothers for a dead client, unexplained.

Why it matters. Together they are what a careful player notices on a second run, and the bible's strength is exact pay-offs.

Fix. June brings the solicitor's letter to Tom, and whether she signs it is the price of her goodwill in the last week. Her room: she cannot sleep where her father's book was kept. The dropped packet was Frank Cammack's, taken off the club's count when Mickey died, which is why Ron carries it himself. Give Julie an S-road job as the society's first driver. Name the counter man Ray Coultas, and say once that Garbutt pays because he supplied them.

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Ellis cannot tell Customs to ignore records (Act III, Thursday); a police note has no force over a VAT officer. Fix: Ellis seizes the 1989 books as evidence, so they leave Reese's hands lawfully.
2. The licensing hearing plays like a liquor-licence bench, with a landlord's objection and the public standing to speak; private hire licensing in 1990 did not work that way. Fix: Agar's solicitor makes a written representation on fitness, and the committee asks if anyone present will give a reference.
3. Alison is told to file the anniversary piece on 18 November or clear her desk, yet her page about it runs on Thursday 13 December. Fix: she files a holding piece in November and the full story is held for the inspection week.
4. Mickey posted June's letters from the far bank to hide a Meridian postmark, but she lives across town in Meridian and knows he is there. Fix: she moved inland years ago, and the letters were to that address.
5. The lost envelope costs £82, but "twenty people over the water" at £4 is £80. Fix: write £80, or make it twenty-one.
6. The non-digging player is said to learn the truth on Wednesday; Reese reads the line aloud on Thursday. Fix: say Thursday.
7. Ada "sold her house long ago" yet sits at her own window on Quay Street. Fix: she sold the big house on the hill and kept her mother's terrace.
8. Alison lodges in the house of the woman who ordered the fire, for months, unremarked. Fix: one line from Ada at the second tea: "I like to know where the paper sleeps."
9. "The parish for the rest" is a hundred years out of date in 1990. Fix: "the social for the rest".
10. Maureen picking dockers at the gate needs a frame. Fix: she runs a registered labour contractor, an employer, not a union post.
11. Danny "grew up" watching his father wait, but was twenty when Frank was struck off. Fix: "spent his twenties watching".

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review

No, not yet, though it is close. The bones are better than most game stories: four books and a state official who asks which is true, a friendship told by which seat a man sits in, a flaw the player plays, and side stories with turns of their own. What holds it back: two big reveals built from familiar pieces, a hinge with a hole a player will find (Ron and the importer's money), and a last act that crams a dozen threads into three hours and climaxes on paperwork, not on the truth it has just uncovered. Fix my first two points and tighten Act III, and it goes in the review's first paragraph.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. The two books side by side. "Importer a/c. Paid." against "Importer refused", the free Saturdays in Sheila's hand, the twenty-to-midnight run squeezed under her last line: the plainest book is the only true one, the player reads it himself, and mild Mr Reese is the right man to open it.
2. Ron and the passenger seat. A docker who cannot drive, three lessons that stop being funny, the gate offered at the wheel, and a verdict given by which vehicle he is in and whether he looks across. It is the Henry and Hans of this game.
3. The offers. Grace's tea and Danny's fifty-pound note looking the same, paid off at the hearing's silent bench and the phone box, where Tom can ring only people he once let give him something. The player learns that a name is a debt by getting it wrong.
