# LEDGER, story bible v6: a player's review

Read as a player who finished GTA V, RDR2 and KCD2 for their stories. I read sections 1 to 15 first, then section 0, and I judge it with section 0's changes in place. I played it twice in my head: once taking the business over, once winding it down.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8

1. Tom: 8. The flaw is something I would actually play: every gift is a press, take or pay, and nothing marks the hooks, so I would get it wrong and feel it at the hearing. The suitcase under the bed and the Ledger's dry voice make an unvoiced man somebody; the thirty cut pages are a mystery I would poke at forever and never be paid for.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Pauline, Terry and June are people I would talk about afterwards. Too many others are names with one job (Garbutt, Lee, Kevin after scene 2, Les Coultas, Philip), and the regulars blur into a list.
3. The antagonists: 7. Danny is the best written: innocent of the thing everyone blames him for, and his page takes his father from him instead of healing him. Agar in the rain is a great scene; the Widow works mostly by offers relayed through other people until the two widows in the cab, and Ada's motive for the Fifth is thinner than her reveal.
4. The central mystery: 8. It has a real truth found by play, and it changes what the opening meant: the free Saturdays, "Importer refused", the tea, the letter. Sheila writing the line herself is the best turn in the bible. Five layers of reveal are a lot, and by the anniversary I had guessed Mickey.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The priest's "forty-one envelopes on the plate, and every one of them empty" is a superb first fare, and the failed carry ending with "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book is the kind of thing I would tell a friend. The first night still depends on how good the carry plays.
6. Structure and pace: 6. Act I is tight and the midpoint lands hard, but Act III crams about a dozen major beats into five days and three or four hours. The second half of Act II stacks ten scenes into three weeks, with clashing dates.
7. The middle: 7. The calendar dates pull a slow player forward, and the order (paper, street, people, table) gives it a shape. Between the scenes, though, the week is the same fares, dusk job, count and Friday round seven times over, and the keeper's rise barely gets dramatised after its third rung.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. "The sweet old widow is the boss" is a trope I have seen before, and the clues give her away early. "Mickey did to Ada what Ada did to Tom", the innocent rival and the bookkeeper on the wall are genuinely fresh.
9. The endings: 8. Ron in a vehicle as the verdict on the friendship, "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four", "Nowak!" from the constable and "Tom has the watch" are all earned last images. The fate lists hanging off each ending are long, and three shown over five underneath will confuse anyone not holding the chart.
10. The side stories: 8. The watch (Ron pawning it for the flowers), Ivy and her daughter protecting each other from the same truth, and Norman's walk are whole stories with real turns. Karen's wardrobe is light and good; Lilies leans on a beat the main line has already used.
11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline on the radio is a real running chorus, and the ham with a seat, the crabs and the eleven-miles-an-hour lesson made me smile. A lot of the funny is in single lines rather than situations, and the second half runs almost entirely dark.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The offers system feeding the hearing and the phone box is the best idea in the document, and almost every scene is a carry, a tail, a break-in or a drive. The trouble is that the mystery is mostly solved by reading lines in books.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The truth arrives as reading, and the man who lit the fire is barely in it.**
What is wrong: almost every turn of the mystery is a line on a page. The real book (midpoint), the Harbour Board minute (scene 7), the ironmonger's book (scene 12), the rent card (scene 16), Danny's page (scene 18), the 1989 fare book in the boot and Mickey's cut page (Act III). Meanwhile Les Coultas, the actual arsonist, gets one scene (scene 8) and a clause in each ending; Ray never appears.
Where: sections 7, 8 and 10.
Why a player feels it: in a game sold on hands, the big payoffs become me holding a torch over handwriting. Seven document reveals in a row feel like auditing, and the boot, which should be the peak, is one more page.
Fix: keep the boot, Sheila on the wall and the cut page, because those are the heart of it. Turn two of the others into people. Give the ironmonger a counter scene where he remembers Darren's face rather than an entry. Give Les a second, physical scene in the second half: he comes back for money on the anniversary night, Tom tails him onto the last ferry, and on the far bank he says one true thing under pressure ("Your uncle was a careful man. He never let us walk"). That points at Mickey driving without giving away Sheila's line.

**2. Act III is crowded, and its verdict is announced before it comes.**
What is wrong: in five days Act III fits Reese's questions, Agar under the lamp, Alison's proof, Ellis's trade, the boot, Sheila's confession, the cut page, the phone box run, every called-in hook, the rival at the door, Ada's table and the endings. Then section 8 says of Reese's verdict: "Neither is a surprise: since Monday the Ledger's reading has said... which way each thing is going."
Where: section 8, "The week" and the close of Friday.
Why a player feels it: this is the GTA V complaint, a last act that hurries. Agar's best scene and Ellis's trade get squeezed between paper days, and if the Ledger has already told me the outcome, the last door opens on nothing.
Fix: move Agar in the rain to the Monday after Garbutt's table (the end of Act II), where it pays off scene 7 and leaves room. Fold Ellis's trade into Thursday night: she is at the end of the boarding-house street when Tom closes the boot. Let the Ledger forecast risks, not results: "Mileage won't add. Don't know if he'll care." Reese's closing line should be the one thing still in doubt.

**3. Ada can be guessed, and her reason for the Fifth is thin.**
What is wrong: the clues are loud. Cyril "tells me what's what", "Everything says he runs it", Ada asks why the round is twenty and not twenty-one, she questions Tom about her alley, Alison lodges over her ("I like to know where the paper sleeps"). An experienced player sees "harmless old woman at the window is the boss" coming by the Wednesday tea. Then her reason for burning her own car to set Tom on Danny by Friday is never clear: what does Danny threaten that she cannot wait?
Where: section 0 change 1; section 6 (Tuesday, Wednesday, the Friday round); midpoint beats 4 and 5.
Why a player feels it: a midpoint reveal I saw coming lands as confirmation, not a blow, and an old woman burning a car over a fare war makes her look like a plot device.
Fix: give Ada one week-two scene where she looks vulnerable: Danny's lads put her window in with the office's, and Tom fixes it with his own hands, so the turn costs me something. Give her a planted stake: Frank has started talking about the round again since his son bought Akers, and Danny has said to the street that he will "find out who's had the club's money". Now she has a reason to want Danny broken before Christmas. Make Cyril's command land once on screen, with Ada absent, to keep the decoy honest.

**4. The middle loops, and the keeper's rise is told rather than played.**
What is wrong: between authored scenes, every week is fares, a dusk job, the count and the Friday round, with "one door different each week" doing the work of variety. On the keeper's road rungs 4 and 5 (the cold-store contract, a man at the landing) have no scene of their own. The second half also has continuity knots: scene 11 is "the Sunday after" the Fifth, which is 11 November, Remembrance Sunday (scene 13); the lock-up is due by Friday the 9th but the plan uses Norman's Saturday hour.
Where: section 7, the ladders, "What a week is", scenes 11 to 13.
Why a player feels it: the KCD2 middle complaint, the same loop at hour twelve. A GTA player taking it over wants to feel the empire grow; here the rise is mostly a list.
Fix: give rung 5 a real scene: Darren's trial carry at the landing, watched by Tom from the cab, going half wrong. It sets up the Kingdom's carrier and the Fifth's betrayal both. Make the Friday round escalate as a story: by the fourth week Ron starts naming the doors that have gone. Put scene 11 on Wednesday the 7th, at the ward, with the third lesson; keep Sunday for Remembrance; move Norman's hour in the lock-up plan to his Thursday.

**5. Alison and Philip take more room than they repay; Garbutt is a name.**
What is wrong: Alison is defined by her two drawers and four dig scenes, but the romance never gets a moment that belongs only to it. The supper is interrupted by the Fifth, and after that she is a choice about what to print. Philip is "a friend, if Tom lets him be one", with a chess set and one fight, and no turn. Garbutt exists to host a table in someone else's shop.
Where: section 4 (Alison and Tom; Philip), scenes 14 (S), 16, 20.
Why a player feels it: the love interest should be someone I miss in the Suitcase ending; as written I would miss Ron and Sheila and forget her. Philip I would forget by hour six.
Fix: give Alison one scene with no work in it after the Fifth: she reads the Ledger over his shoulder, laughs, and asks for a page of it. What Tom lets her keep is the romance's beat. Give Philip a turn: he has been quietly selling the library's best books to Agar's agents to pay the school's heating, and the hall Tom defends is half empty of what it should hold. Cut Garbutt and let Hal host the table; Hal already owns the room.

## 3. Would I single this story out

Yes. Not for the plot first, but for Ron in the passenger seat. A docker who cannot drive, learning on an empty dock road at eleven miles an hour, finishing the Bishop story for the first time with the handbrake on, and then being in a vehicle in every ending, with whether he looks across as the verdict: that is a Henry-and-Hans relationship built out of driving, which only a game can do. Then there is the night of the boot, with Sheila sitting on the boarding-house wall with her handbag on her knee, saying "Nobody reads a fare book", and the player holding a pen and a match. I would tell a friend about both. What holds me back from a 9 is the reading-heavy reveals, a guessable midpoint villain and a last week that tries to do too much in too little time.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron and Tom: the passenger seat, the three lessons (the third not funny), "Car one, you on your own?", and the vehicle in every ending.
2. Sheila's line in the fare book: she wrote the run when Mickey would not, posted the letter to get a reader, and waits on the wall. The fare book as alibi and noose rests on her, and "Mickey did to Ada what Ada did to Tom" is the real twist.
3. The offers system: gifts and hooks look the same, and they pay off at the hearing (the full bench where nobody stands) and at the phone box on the last Friday. Tom's flaw is played, not told.
