# LEDGER story bible, version 5: script editor's note

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8

1. Tom: 8. His want, the name said by people who mean it, is clear from the postscript. His flaw is played as a mechanic, the take-or-pay press, and that mechanic later decides who stands at the hearing and who answers the phone box. What holds the score down: his fear, the suitcase, gets one press and one line from Sheila.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, June, Pauline and Terry are specific and warm, and each wants something of their own. Alison is the weakest of the main people: the reporter love interest is a familiar figure.
3. The antagonists: 8. There are four, each with a motive, a personal wound, a scene of their own and a different method: Ada uses reasons, the Widow prices, Agar works by the calendar and Danny competes in daylight. Ada's plan is let down by one blunder nobody explains (point 3 below), and Cyril is a mechanism, not a person.
4. The central mystery: 7. It has a real truth in layers, and the boot turns "Importer refused", "That's the last one, Cyril" and the first tea into different scenes. The rules about access leak, though: the 1989 fare book can be read too early, or taken away before the climax. Too many of its turns also arrive as someone's speech.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 7. The badge, the priest's empty envelopes, the arithmetic of Friday and the failed carry ending with "Nowak, T. £80" are strong. But Act I loads a lot of set-up into two hours (Gordon's letters, the hearing letter, Alison, the round, Sunday's question). Much of it also leans on "as written" text not in this bible.
6. Structure and pace: 7. Anchoring the act to the calendar (the hearing, the Fifth, Remembrance, the anniversary, the letter dates) is the best structural decision in the document. Act II is overfull for a game that is not story-first, and Act III piles four night jobs on top of three monologues.
7. The middle: 8. It really turns at the Fifth, then turns again at the tape, Remembrance, the anniversary and the table. The weekly Friday round is the risk of a repeating loop, and it is only partly met by the faces changing.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. The widow at the window who secretly runs things, the gentleman developer with a marina, the honest copper and the fixer with a moustache are all types a British viewer has met. What lifts it is that Danny is innocent, Mickey manipulated Ada the way she manipulates Tom, Sheila's hand is on the line, and Ron kept quiet.
9. The endings: 7. There are clear states, a last image in every close, and the Ledger and fare-book pairing. But one last line contradicts the truth, the Kingdom's relation to Mickey's page is unclear, and Ada's empty chair is never explained.
10. The side stories: 8. Ivy and her daughter, each shielding the other from what both know, is the finest of them. The watch, Lilies and Norman all touch the main line without being needed by it.
11. Tone and humour: 8. The radio as chorus, the crabs on the ride-along, the radios that only pick up the pirate station and the dry Ledger lines are actually funny.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The hearing bench, the phone box that only rings people who gave Tom something, the lessons as the car's own handling, and the carry with a tail all work. It loses points where truths are delivered by talk instead of found with the hands.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The climax's key evidence can be read early or taken away before the boot.**
- What is wrong: Section 15 says "the fare books are on a shelf behind Sheila from the first day" and that every turn can be found early. Act III says Sheila keeps the old books locked in the cabinet, and that this is the only time she keeps a book from Tom. A player who opens 1989 in week two finds the fire's run on day nine of the game. Separately, on Thursday midday Ellis offers to seize the 1989 books "so that they leave Reese's hands lawfully". A player who accepts has no books in Reese's boot that night, and so never reads Sheila's hand or the Monday runs.
- Why it matters: The boot is the moment the whole mystery changes meaning. If it can be skipped, or spent early, the climax has nothing to test.
- Fix: Only 1990's fare book is on the shelf. Sheila took 1988 and 1989 home the week after the fire, and the cabinet holds an empty space that Tom can notice. Move Ellis's seizure to Friday at nine, after the boot, so that every player reads the book on Thursday night.

**2. Sheila's year of silence is thin while a detective is working the same file.**
- What is wrong: Sheila wrote the run, knew what it meant the Monday after the fire, and "waited a year for somebody to read it". Meanwhile Ellis, born two streets from the row, kept the file open after her inspector closed it. "Nobody reads a fare book" is a good line but not a reason. In the last week Sheila also keeps the books from Tom and hands them to a Customs officer, and "she does not say why".
- Why it matters: Sheila is the character the owner most wants kept. If her central act has no motive, a player will feel the plot needed her to wait.
- Fix: Give her two lines on Friday. She is in it herself: she booked the watchman's free Saturdays in her own hand, so going to Ellis puts her in the dock and stops forty-one envelopes, Ivy Leadley's among them. She wanted a reader who was neither police nor her, and in the last week a reader nobody in the Hook could bargain with. "I couldn't hand it to you. You'd have had to choose."

**3. Ada, the cleverest person in the story, leaves a signed trail.**
- What is wrong: Section 10 says "she never does a thing; she finds somebody a reason". Yet the paraffin goes on "Mrs Jensen's account" at an ironmonger who writes everything down. A yellow car is seen pulling away and one of Danny's lads boasts in the Tangs' queue. Neither is explained: who drove the yellow car, and why would Danny's man boast of a crime Danny did not commit?
- Why it matters: The tape and the ironmonger's book catch her. If the catch rests on her stupidity, the antagonist shrinks at the exact moment she should be largest.
- Fix: Ada gave Darren cash. He put the can on her coal account and pocketed the money, the way he skims the lock-up's radios, so his own greed betrays her. Have Darren borrow one of Akers' old cars before it went for paint, or simply cut the yellow car. Make the boaster a hanger-on Danny later slaps in public.

**4. Ron's part in Alan Jensen's death is set up and never paid off between Ron and Tom.**
- What is wrong: At Remembrance six men look at the ground at Alan's name, and Ron is one of them. Gordon counted seven standing still. Ron's gang (Tapper, Shiner, the Bishop) have "a story for none of them that he will finish". Scene 16 gives the reckoning to the two widows, and nothing ever passes between Ron and Tom about it. Ron's confession at the anniversary then reads as his first silence, when it is really his second.
- Why it matters: This is the relationship the game is meant to be remembered for, and it has no scene where Tom learns the worst thing his friend has done.
- Fix: Let lesson three carry it. Handbrake on, Ron finishes one of his stories: the Bishop was Alan's man, and they all stood on the quay. Then "believe your friend" at the anniversary carries real weight, because the player knows this man has stood still before. Tom's one press there feeds which seat Ron is in at the end.

**5. Act II is overstuffed, and too many truths arrive as speeches.**
- What is wrong: Ten to thirteen hours hold twenty scenes, two five-rung ladders, four dig jobs, seven grown side stories and a weekly round. Norman tells, Garbutt tells, Ada tells in six sentences, Darren tells "all of it in one breath", Ron tells, Agar gives his sentence, then Sheila gives a Friday speech that repeats what the boot has just shown.
- Why it matters: The bar asks for few short scenes and a truth uncovered by play. A player will feel the run of confessions as the same scene with different faces.
- Fix: Merge scenes 15 and 16 into one night at the gate. Make the bout in scene 19 the only form of the Danny scene, not a branch. Turn two confessions into objects: Garbutt's payments to Les become a stub book in his car, and Darren's confession becomes the ironmonger's ledger plus his silence. Cut Sheila's Friday speech to the cut page and two lines.

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Section 0 says Ellis's file was closed as undetermined. Section 4 still says "as the owner's own work". Fix section 4, because the insurers paying the importer depends on it.
2. Ada "sold her house long ago", yet she has a front room, a front door and a lodger. Fix: she sold the house on the hill and rents Quay Street from Agar's agents, which also gives him a hook in her.
3. Mickey posts letters to his daughter, who lives across the same town, from the far bank to hide the postmark, at an address she left years ago. Fix: she trained and worked inland, came back four years ago, and he never had the nerve to find the new address.
4. The Suitcase's last line is called "the same charge Mickey wrote on the night of the fire". Sheila wrote it. Change the name.
5. Ada's "You'd go for Terry" reads as intent, which contradicts "Nobody said a man". Make it "You'd go for a burned car."
6. Ron drives to the infirmary on public roads with two lessons and no licence. Give him a provisional licence and L-plates in lesson two, with a line from Pauline on air.
7. Tom sells the mooring and right of way before probate, while Agar holds the parade's freehold. State that the yard and mooring are Mickey's own freehold in the estate, sold under contract with completion after probate.
8. "What can jail him is in Mickey's book" is never named. Name it: voting on a sale to his own company and taking a tenant's rent knowing whose it was. Or soften "jail" to "ruin".
9. Cyril heard the importer say he paid and sent him away. Rule whether Cyril told Ada, or kept it to cover Mickey. One line in section 10.
10. Ellis's warrant in scene 18 has no trigger. Tie it to Les Coultas's statement or to the anniversary piece.
11. "Wages for four" in Act I, but five packets once Sheila is counted. Make the numbers agree.
12. A two-car minicab office may sit under the 1990 VAT threshold. Say Mickey's firm is above it on account work, or registered voluntarily. Have Reese call "no charge" entries a suppression flag aloud, so his interest in a free fare is plain.
13. "Eleven weeks' rent taken after" on the cut page should read "taken before".

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review

No, not yet, though it is close. The design ideas are ones a reviewer would quote: a flaw you play as a press; a hearing where only those you let help you can stand; a phone box that only rings people who gave you something; a Customs officer as the last reader; a fare book that is both alibi and noose. What stops me today is that the mystery's access rules leak, the cleverest antagonist is caught by her own carelessness, and the friendship lacks its own worst-truth scene. On top of that, the middle runs on confession more than discovery. Fix the five points and I would.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. The flaw as a mechanic, and its pay-off. Take or pay, with no marking of gift from hook, called in at the hearing and in the Friday phone box. The climax tests exactly what the story built.
2. Ron and the passenger seat. The lessons, the man who names every building by what it was, number nine on the quay wall, and the verdict on the friendship given by which seat Ron is in at the end.
3. The mirror at the heart of the truth. Mickey found Ada a reason as Ada finds everyone one, and Sheila wrote the line he would not. It is paid off by a mild man with a pencil, in the plainest book in the office. With Tom's line offering him only other people's hands, theme and plot are the same thing.
