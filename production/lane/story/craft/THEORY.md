# THEORY: what makes a great story, in craft and in games

Researched 23 Nov 2026 from written sources only. Numbers in square brackets point to the Sources list at the end. Quotes are short; everything else is paraphrase.

A note on reach. I did not reach the full text of McKee's or Truby's books. For them I used the summaries listed, and where a point below comes from general knowledge of the books rather than a summary I read, it is marked "unverified". Where sources disagree or only one says something, that is stated.

## Part A. Established story craft

### The lead: want, need and the lie

Truby's seven key steps start with weakness and need, then desire, then the opponent, the plan, the battle, self-revelation and a new equilibrium [2]. The order matters: the flaw comes before the goal, so the goal is shaped by the flaw.

Weiland puts the same idea plainly [4]. The Lie is a flawed belief the lead holds, and it is the root of the arc. The Want is the outer, plot-driven goal, which the lead thinks will cure the trouble the Lie causes. The Need is the Truth, usually a realisation, and it may cost the Want. Her examples are Scrooge, who wants money and needs the love of others, and Lightning McQueen, who wants fame and needs people in his life. She calls the clash between Want and Need a "silent war" that feeds the outer conflict [4].

Egri, writing in 1945, starts earlier: a character is known in three dimensions, physiology, sociology and psychology, and the writer picks a pivotal character whose actions force the conflict [5]. The point for a team: the lead is built from a body, a class and a mind, not from a plot role.

### Desire and opposition

McKee, as summarised [3], treats each scene as a small version of the whole: the lead pursues a goal, meets an obstacle, and the result is different from what was expected. That gap between expectation and result is what makes a scene a scene.

Truby's opponent is a structural element, not a villain by default [2]. In his teaching a true opponent does not only block the lead's desire but competes with the lead for the same goal [33], so that only one can have it. That pressure is why the two keep colliding.

### Stakes that climb

Per the McKee summary [3], scenes build into sequences, sequences into acts, and each level ends on a larger change in the lead's situation. Egri says the same from the stage: conflict must rise, not stay still or jump, and characters swing between opposite poles until a crisis comes [5]. The practical rule is that each complication should cost more than the last and close off an easy way out.

### Inciting incident, midpoint, crisis, climax

The McKee summary [3] says the inciting incident is the early scene that first upsets the lead's life and sets the goal. It raises the central question of the story. The climax is "the final, most extreme, and irreversible change" and it answers that question [3].

The midpoint and the crisis as a choice under pressure are standard in McKee and in most structure teaching, but the summary I read does not cover the crisis [3]. McKee's "true choice" is a dilemma, either between irreconcilable goods or between the lesser of two evils [34]; the common teaching places that choice at the crisis, which the climax then acts out (the placement unverified here). The midpoint, said to turn the lead from reacting to acting, stays unverified.

### Theme as a question argued through action

Egri's premise is a short claim the story tests, with the example "ruthless ambition leads to its own destruction" [5]. His point is that character, conflict and conclusion together prove it. The McKee summary says the climax "proves" the theme, and does not state it in speech [3]. Truby's self-revelation step is where the lead learns it [2]. The consistent advice: a theme is a question the story puts to the lead, and the ending is the answer the action has earned.

### Setups, payoffs and the ensemble

Pressfield, working from McKee's axiom that the inciting incident determines the obligatory scene, puts it plainly: the payoff's content must be embedded in the setup, and payoffs are only as good as their setups [35]. Anything the ending relies on must be planted earlier; that the plant should not look like one is general craft, unverified. The ensemble is thinner. Egri comes closest: the other characters are arranged to serve the story around the pivotal one [5], and a firm bond between opposed characters is what keeps the conflict alive [5]. The common extension, which I take from Truby's teaching and have not checked, is that supporting characters each show a different answer to the theme's question.

## Part B. What is different in games

### The lead is also the player

The central problem is who owns the lead. Hocking's 2007 essay on BioShock names it ludonarrative dissonance: the rules tell the player one thing ("seek power and you will progress") while the story says another [6][7]. He argued the player cannot commit to either, and that the late reveal mocked the player for having gone along [6]. Note that the term comes from one designer's complaint about one game. Later writers have widened it a great deal, so use it as a test, not a law.

Disco Elysium's lead writer Helen Hindpere gives the positive form: stories give meaning to mechanics, so the game uses resource management to speak about poverty and passive skill checks to speak about addiction and mental health [9]. She also says games should stop copying film, because cutscenes alone cannot carry the story [9].

Rockstar's route is a lead who bends without breaking. Dan Houser says he wants characters who can "veer to be nice, veer to be nasty" [10]. Roger Clark says no two players' Arthurs are the same and that keeping a player-driven character consistent is, in his words, impossible; he still reads Arthur as a man learning to strive for goodness, "Ebenezer Scrooge with a six shooter" [13]. That is Weiland's Lie and Need [4] with the player allowed to resist it.

Warhorse and CD Projekt differ here, and it is worth seeing why. Vavra said in 2013 that the main story stays more linear and the side quests are where freedom lives, and that the player cannot do things that would break the story, such as killing important characters [17]. CD Projekt's Stachyra said the game never imposes a path and that there is "your Geralt or my Geralt" [15]. Both can be true: Geralt is a fixed person whose attitude the player shades, while the plot's fixed points are protected.

Druckmann on The Last of Us Part II says he wants players to feel complicit in what happens [12]. That is deliberate use of the dissonance Hocking complained about.

### Story through play, not only cutscenes

A Rockstar developer, quoted at GamesBeat, says the team wanted players to build relationships with characters "not in cutscenes, not in watching it passively" [11]. Druckmann describes designing for a bond made through play where dialogue cannot be relied on [8]. CD Projekt's Tomaszkiewicz warns that stories needing custom cutscenes for everything produce long sequences with no gameplay between [14]. Sasko says the Bloody Baron alternates play and story so the player keeps their purpose [16].

The shared rule: keep the player's hands busy during the story, and save stillness for the moments that earn it.

### Pacing over many hours

Druckmann says every section must move the story to its ending, and that a cool idea that exists for its own sake should go [12]. Sasko's GDC 2023 advice is to withhold information so the player wants the next beat, to cut recap conversations, to give emotional moments room, and to deliver key information in focused moments while lighter talk happens during other activities [18]. He also says the Baron's botchling burial gives a large release of tension after a run of hard scenes [16].

Rockstar moved missions around to shape the story. The example given: a mission was placed before a rescue so that Arthur would not seem to rush to save someone he did not care about [11]. Houser says breaking a story into missions with designers has its own art and skill, because the missions also unlock features and parts of the world [10]. Note the order of events in the world serves character logic, not just difficulty.

On the slow middle, one written source, from outside the games studied: Baldur's Gate 3's lead writer Adam Smith says its second act was made deliberately more contained, with a stronger main quest, villain and stakes, because repeating the first act's wide open map "would get exhausting" [36]. The lesson: change the shape of play in the middle and tighten it, rather than offer more of the same.

### Companions and time together

Druckmann says the Joel and Ellie arc started from a man "pretty much dead" inside who is changed by the bond, with roles flipping so the girl saves him [8]. He also says he usually writes The Last of Us from the middle, with the pair already together, as the level and gameplay take shape [19]. That order suggests the bond is built in the long middle, not the opening.

For the RDR2 gang, cast members describe years of working together on a motion-capture stage, performing scenes with bodies, faces and voices captured together [20]. Clark says the cast's trust mirrored the characters' [20]. A Rockstar developer says the gang had to be large and fluid, with people coming and going, to be believable, and that its money builds over time through jobs, which explains why the gang looked poor after the heist [11]. The craft point: the camp is both a place and a ledger, and the bond is made of repeated small contact, with the economy backing up the story.

On talk during rides, a 2022 Game Studies paper notes that RDR2's story missions often pair Arthur with others who share exposition as they ride together, and that these long horseback dialogues bind the game's mechanics to its narrative [37]. That is the scholars' reading, not a Rockstar statement; no developer source was found.

### The world as a character

Houser says he wanted an underbelly of New York and an immigrant experience for GTA IV, and that the game worked once the lead had comedy and tragedy [21]. In 2009 he called Niko's story a game about "an immigrant discovering himself" [22]. Hindpere says the Disco Elysium setting was grown over years of tabletop world-building [9].

Warhorse says Kingdom Come 2's city has about a thousand NPCs on daily cycles, meant to be worked with, not exploited, and that actions such as poisoning food have consequences [23]. Vavra's earlier interview says the team built terrain from satellite data and recreated real sites, and that almost no games are set in actual history [24]. Sasko says the Baron was built to personify Velen, "with all of its beauty and troubles" [25]. That last is the clearest statement of a world given a face.

### Side quests that carry the theme

The Bloody Baron is the standard example, and Vavra himself named it (as "the Red Baron quest") as his model in 2015 [24]. What the sources say about how it works:

- It began as a short design with an outline from Marcin Blacha, and a first script of about 40 pages with no dialogue [26].
- The Baron mirrors Geralt: two fathers who lost families and have blood on their hands [25].
- The designers show both sides of him so the player hesitates; his care for Ciri is meant to make players doubt he is simply bad [25].
- Themes of trauma and domestic violence were drawn from real human experience, and Sasko says nothing like it existed in games when he wrote it in 2012 [27].
- Folklore, the botchling, gave it a body [25][26].

The wider CD Projekt rule set: every quest must have some impact [15], fetch quests were "declared war" on, with an item delivery acceptable only if it leads to a good conversation or choice [14], and some characters need whole stories or should be cut [14].

### How choices come back

Sasko's advice: write real dilemmas, set up stakes before big decisions, and make consequences visible, with delayed payoffs landing hardest [18]. Stachyra says decisions were made hard so outcomes often differed from what players expected [15]. The Baron's endings range from redemption to tragedy, and each branch had to be directed and performed to match [25]. Warhorse says the sequel's quests give more player agency [23] and that consequences are real [23].

The cost is volume. Disco Elysium's team found that writing too much, not too little, became their problem, and that editing time was the bottleneck [29].

### Endings

Houser says he first wanted Niko dead at the end of GTA IV and held back; I read only the headline of that report [30]. A Rockstar developer says RDR2's ending was "the right ending for the game" because it had to link to the earlier game as a prequel [11]. Clark says the game has no good ending in the sense fans want, and had his own canon one; I read only the headline [31]. Sources are thin here.

### Humour beside violence

Houser says comedy needs narrative and heart under the cynicism, and cites The Office [10]. He says GTA IV worked because Niko carried both comedy and tragedy [21]. Warhorse describes its sequel's quests as deeper and darker, with some humour [23]. Druckmann is on the other side of the line, arguing the first Last of Us gained power from dilemmas without judgement [12]. Disco Elysium makes the humour the voice of the lead's mind [9][32].

## Where games and film craft agree and disagree

- Agree: the lead needs a flaw and a want that fight each other [2][4].
- Agree: each scene or mission should change something and go somewhere [3][12].
- Agree: the theme is a question answered by the ending, not by speeches [3][5].
- Agree: cut what exists only for its own sake [12].
- Disagree: film owns its lead; a game shares the lead with the player, so the lead needs room to bend [10][13].
- Disagree: film controls the viewer's eyes; a game's story must survive the player looking elsewhere, so key information is placed with care [18].
- Disagree: film pacing is cut in the edit; game pacing is set by missions, rest and play, and missions may be reordered for character logic [11].
- Disagree: film shows the bond in scenes; games build it by time spent together and shared tasks [8][20].
- Disagree: film has one telling; games branch, so every branch needs tone and performance to match [25].
- Agree and disagree: both need stakes that climb, but a game's climb is also a climb in what the player can do [10] (my reading, a guess).
