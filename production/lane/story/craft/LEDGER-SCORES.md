## What each lacks (the readers' words, shortened)

The Burial Club. The want is to avoid something ("to owe nobody"), and the lead is never in danger: "Tom is a pallbearer, not a man taking risks." The structure is a weekly funeral with a score, which is the principle's own named fail, and it never climbs. The tone is elegy and service, not tension or a rise, and the central person dies before the last act. Kept: reader 1 names the writing, the comedy, the stamp, the Man in Black's voice and the turnout as the town's record ("the best of the three, but they serve a different game"); all three gave its comedy, its turn and its one question full marks.

The Arrested Ship. Tom is the quietest person in his own story and rarely risks his own skin. The middle is a nightly fare on one dock; the ship never moves until the last night. The loss falls on a light character, a seaman the player has only ridden with a few times. Kept: an RDR2-shaped spine, a want that is a little wrong ("the ship makes him somebody"), an opponent who wants exactly what Tom wants and is better at it, an early routine deed that returns as the turn (Tom drove the brass out himself), and a last act that pays off what was planted.

The Knowledge. The want is again an avoidance ("to be nobody's passenger"), and the plan builds Tom as lost and led. The worst thing happens to Ron while Tom stands outside a door. The play rests on hidden tallies earlier readers already called dense and hard to follow. Kept: the warmest bond of the three (readers 1 and 2); the driving lessons and "You'll have to do the door, boss" were praised by the earlier v3 readers, not by these three.

One habit all three share (reader 1): in each, the player's suspicion turns out to be a hidden kindness, and each ends with Tom giving something up. None of them is a rise to power.

So, plainly: The Burial Club and The Knowledge cannot reach the bar by revision; every reader says so. The Arrested Ship could, by the readers' count, but only at 60%, and every one of them names the same doubt: it is a story about letting go, and the owner asked for a rise to power. To pass it would need a louder Tom in danger, the nightly fare cut to one thread, a real climb in weeks one to four (the ship as the base of the outfit's night trade) and the loss moved onto someone carried for hours. The readers judged those fixes to fit inside the premise; this report's own view, not theirs, is that together they come close to a rewrite.

## The recommendation: rethink the spine, keep the best parts

The story needs a fundamental rethink of three things: Tom's want, the shape of the seven weeks, and what the central relationship is for. The setting stays (1990, the minicab office, the Hook and its docks, crime), and so does the end of the docks, as the pressure under everything rather than the plot.

The strongest evidence is not the marks but the sketches. Asked separately what this game's story should be, the three readers drew strikingly similar stories, and they share with the outline he approved on 28 September its rise and its fare book lying since day one, which v3 left behind (though with one opponent where the outline has three equal rivals, and no inspection). The likeness is weaker evidence than it looks: all three read one brief quoting his "rise to power", principles that reward an inside opponent and a returning deed, and bibles with one cast. Where they agree:

- Tom's want: to make Mickey's dead office the firm that runs the Hook's nights (two said by Christmas): active, loud, a little wrong, and the rise to power his pillar asks for. His need differs by reader: one person who stays because he chooses to, not because he is owed or afraid (reader 3); to stop treating people as routes and assets (reader 2); that the town remembers a kept word, not fear (reader 1).
- The bond: Ron Kirby, on every collection, carry and break-in, rides and fights together, his dry talk in the car carrying it; for two readers Ron keeps an old code (reader 2: you never sell a man to the law; reader 3: no widows, no old dockers) and that code is what the rise strains. This report adds The Knowledge's driving lessons for comedy.
- The opponent: inside the circle and wanting the same chair. Two readers chose Danny Cammack as Mickey's old driver, back in week one as Tom's best earner, gaining a driver, a round or a debtor each week where the player sees it; one chose the Widow, Mickey's oldest partner, backing Tom to own him.
- The early deed: in week one Tom chooses how hard to lean on a docker, or writes one false line in the fare book to cover a job; it comes back in week six as the cause of the fall (the outline's books "lying since day one" are exactly this).
- The shape: weeks one to three the rise (collections, the first runs off the dock, taking a rank or a gate), each job watched by a town that talks; week four the midpoint, a big job against the Widow (a bonded warehouse, the night gate) that turns play from taking ground to holding it; week five the strain, with Ron seeing what the rise costs; week six (week five for reader 1) the fall, in reader 3's version Ellis reading the book, the office lost, Ron taken by the law in Tom's place and keeping silent; week seven the last act, Tom winning men back door by door by what the record says he did to each, the opponent faced in person at night on the quay, the game's first gun.
- The ending, taught by every squeeze-or-spare choice before it: keep the firm and leave Ron inside, or give Ellis the true book and his own liberty to get Ron out; Ron's last word at the cell door, the prison gate, the office step or on the quay (readers 1 and 3).

What to carry over from v3, because readers praised it, these three or the earlier v3 readers (v3/measure/RESULT-P2.md): from The Arrested Ship, the opponent who wants what Tom wants and the routine deed that returns; from The Burial Club, the Man in Black's marks, the coffin "like a wardrobe on a staircase", and the ending read from the town's record; from The Knowledge, the driving lessons and "You'll have to do the door, boss"; from all three, the dock's end shown in counts (the pen shrinking, the cranes falling) while Tom's own fortunes rise against it.

Why this and not the revision of The Arrested Ship: the rethink answers the seven weighted principles at the root (a loud want, a lead at risk, a climbing middle, stakes up to his liberty), where the revision would fight its own premise; it matches his own words of 18 Nov; and it is nearer the baseline canon already cites in its rise and its lying book, though it drops the three equal rivals. The doubts: the readers' sketches carry the brief's lean, so their agreement is not independent proof, and every reader thought The Arrested Ship could be revised. The cost: it is a new bible, about a day of writing and a round of the checked readers, and it may land in the same place if written in the same quiet voice. A guard for that: score its one-page premise against these fifteen principles before any full bible is written.
