# Kingdom Come: Deliverance II (Warhorse Studios, 4 February 2025): story anatomy

Read on 23 Nov 2026 from written sources only. Numbers in brackets point to the Sources list; unconfirmed claims are marked. Quest order comes from guide sites, not the game, so placements are approximate. Quest numbers follow Gamepressure's list [4], which counts two wedding task quests separately and leaves out the opening prologue.

## 1. Shape and length (the acts or chapters, with how many hours each takes a typical player from HowLongToBeat or guides, and where the inciting incident, the first turn, the midpoint, the second turn or fall, the climax and the ending fall, by chapter and rough hour)

Total length. HowLongToBeat was not reached. GamesRadar's guide gives 40 to 50 hours for the main story, 50 to 70 with some side content, 90 to 110 for everything (its own estimates, not HowLongToBeat's) [1]. GamesRadar's review says "well beyond 60 hours" [2]; PC Gamer's reviewer played "75 hours or so" [3].

Shape. A flash-forward prologue ("Last Rites", the Suchdol siege, which serves as the tutorial), then two big regions, Trosky and Kuttenberg, then a closing siege at Suchdol. PowerPyx's walkthrough groups the main quests under two headings, Trosky Region and Kuttenberg Region, with the break after "Storm" [16]; Gamepressure lists 33 main quests in the same order [4]. The Kingdom Come wiki on Fandom was found by search but not reached.

- Act one, Trosky (quests 1 to 13, "Easy Riders" to "Storm"). A bandit ambush, tasks to earn a place at a wedding, the wedding ("Wedding Crashers"), Capon saved from the gallows ("For Whom the Bell Tolls"), a feast at Trosky and an ambush ("For Victory!"), rescue from bandits ("Divine Messenger"), a siege survived ("The Finger of God"), and "Storm": Henry, tortured by Istvan Toth in Trosky Castle, escapes, kills Istvan and takes back his father's sword [4][17]. No source gives hours per act. RPG Codex says "all the fun" is in the first region [5]. Estimate, a guess: 15 to 25 hours of the main line.
- Act two, Kuttenberg (quests 14 to 27, "The Sword and the Quill" to "Civitas Pragensis"). A mine investigation ("Via Argentum"), a plan against Sigismund ("The King's Gambit"), a pogrom Henry can try to stop ("Exodus"), then "Oratores", "The Italian Job" and "Civitas Pragensis", which PowerPyx still files under Kuttenberg [4][16]. Wikipedia's summary has Henry free the lords Sigismund holds, partly by stealing his silver, until Sigismund withdraws [6].
- Act three, Suchdol (quests 28 to 33, "So it begins..." to "Judgment Day"). The defence, hunger ("Hunger and Despair"), a return to the prologue ("Last Rites") and "Judgment Day" with an epilogue [4][16]. The ending varies with choices, including whether Henry accepts his father's sword [6].

Story points, the file's reading, not any source's labels:
- Inciting incident: the first hour. Henry and Hans carry a letter to Otto von Bergow meant to show whether he backs Sigismund or Wenceslas [7][6]. Bandits take their gear.
- First turn: imprisonment at Trosky and the failed wedding infiltration (quests 6 to 7) [6][4].
- Midpoint: no source names one. The end of act one, where Henry kills Istvan and recovers the sword, is the natural hinge (guess) [17]. PopMatters says the game turns from "hardcore" into "a AAA, Ridley Scott-level blockbuster" as the story goes on [7].
- Fall: a betrayal at Nebakov, after which Henry allies with Jan Zizka [6]. In Kuttenberg Capon must be freed again ("Taking French Leave") [4][6].
- Climax and ending: Suchdol under siege, then the epilogue with dreamlike appearances of Henry's mother and a dead friend [6].

Pacing complaints are about the front: "It's a long old prologue" [8]; the early game is slow, with no horse and no fast travel [7].

## 2. The lead (what he wants at the start; what he needs underneath; what forces the change and when; how and whether he changes; how the player is made to feel it in play, not only in cutscenes)

Carried over from the first game. Henry of Skalitz begins the first game as a smith's apprentice. His parents are killed in an attack by a warring lord, and he wants revenge and his father's sword [9]. Wikipedia's summary now calls him a man-at-arms serving his natural father, Sir Radzig Kobyla [6].

Want at the start of the sequel. The want is now a job: deliver Bergow the letter [6][7]. PopMatters says Henry's unfinished personal goals "loom" only "somewhere in the background" [7], and the old want is paid off early: he kills Istvan Toth and takes back the sword at the end of act one [17]. So revenge gives way to duty to a lord, then to staying alive and keeping a friend alive.

Need underneath (the file's reading). To stop being a servant who cleans up another man's mess and become someone who decides. VideoGamer states the flip side: Henry is "essentially a glorified task boy running from one avoidable problem to the next" [8]. PopMatters reads the story as "working your way up from the bottom and coming of age" [7].

What forces the change. The game takes status away early: "after the first plot twist", guards douse him and he goes "all the way down the food chain in no time", rebuilding by theft, junk-selling and odd jobs [7]. Later pressure is the siege and the people he cannot save.

How he changes. Mostly by capability and station, less by outlook. RPG Codex says Henry ends up "all peasant/scholar/knight/commander" and the parent dream scenes "feel out of place" [5]. PC Gamer calls him "a plucky, likeable lad" with a West Country accent [3]; GamesRadar, "likeable and occasionally bumbling" [2]; VideoGamer, "a bit of a sap and often flavourless" [8]. Critics split on whether he has an inner arc or only a rising career.

How play makes it felt. The daily rules do the work. He starts poor, cannot read well, and cannot get into places; as skills and gear rise he gets through doors, wins arguments, and survives fights [7][2]. Persuasion depends on appearance as well as stats, and a missed lockpicking skill can later close a theft route [2]. RPG Codex says "once you reach Kuttenberg you're an unstoppable powerhouse," so the felt climb flattens in the second half [5].

## 3. The relationships that carry it (the three or four that carry the story. For each: who, what each wants, the beats of the relationship in order with the chapter or mission where each falls; and how it is built in play: missions together, rides and drives with talk, camp or home or hangout talk, small optional moments, phone calls. Name concrete missions or scenes as examples.)

No scripts or transcripts were reached, so beats come from quest names, summaries and reviews. No source reached describes camp scenes between Henry and Hans.

Henry and Hans Capon (the central bond).
- Who: Henry, a commoner; Hans, a young noble, arrogant but decent. PC Gamer calls the pairing "very well done indeed" and the story's central thread [3].
- Wants: Hans wants to prove himself to his father and the court (guess, from the lord's-errand setup in [6]); Henry wants to do right by the man who raised him from servant (guess).
- Beats, in order: they ride out together on the errand [6]; bandits rob them [6]; the wedding infiltration fails and they are imprisoned at Trosky [6]; Henry saves Capon from execution ("For Whom the Bell Tolls", quest 7) [4]; they are betrayed at Nebakov [6]; in Kuttenberg Henry frees Capon again ("Taking French Leave", quest 19) [4][6]; in the siege Henry finds food for Hans ("Hunger and Despair", quest 30) [4] (the last two as Gamepressure titles them; contents unconfirmed).
- In play: Hans is a companion on the road and in missions, so the player is with him when things go wrong. Quest 2 ("Fortuna") has Henry help brew medicine for him [4]. Henry cleans up Hans's mistakes; one critic reads this as a gripe [8], another as the bond itself [3].
- Tom McKay says the "element of choice" is "ramped up massively"; the article reports a romance option with Hans [10].
- Carried over: PopMatters asks why "a nobleman like Hans Capon" is "so patient with you, an ordinary commoner" [7]. The sequel starts after that bond exists (guess: it spends its time testing it).

Others, without beats reached: Katherine, a spy, and Father Godwin, a playable priest [2]; Jan Zizka, Henry's ally after Nebakov [6]; Musa of Mali, defended in "The Lion's Den" [4], whom VideoGamer calls "perfunctory" [8]. Radzig, Rosa and Mutt the dog were not reached.

## 4. How the story is delivered (cutscenes against talk during play: any published figure for cutscene hours or line counts, dialogue during drives and rides, ambient and optional talk, letters, journals, radio, phone. Give numbers where a source gives them and say where none exists.)

Numbers.
- Script: Warhorse said on 13 January 2025 that the script is over 2.2 million words, as Vavra had in August 2024 [11][6]. A studio claim, not independently counted; Guinness verified Baldur's Gate 3's record, not this one [11].
- Cutscene hours: a GamesRadar piece (19 April 2024) has the subheading "The long-awaited sequel has 5 hours of cutscenes", and the body does not expand on it (checked by the reviewer) [12]. A Czech film festival write-up of the cutscene process gives no runtime [13]. So the figure is reported once, by a secondary source, unconfirmed by the studio. No line count was found.

Delivery.
- Cutscenes are engine-rendered, made with full mocap and facial capture, with three directors splitting expression, movement and language cohesion [13]. Facial capture was new for the sequel [6].
- Quests run in long chains with cutscenes between; RPG Codex says the story "can put you on the rails for hours," and objects that "you can't press space to speed up the boring long sequences" and to repeated stripping of kit [5]. The Steam counts (section 9) show the same worry, smaller.
- Talk during play: quests lean on dialogue checks where appearance and reputation matter [2][3]; "NPCs like to overshare" [5]. No source gives counts of ride talk.
- The opening errand is a letter [7]. Reached sources do not discuss journals or other channels.

## 5. The stakes ladder (what can be lost, step by step, from the first hour to the end: money, safety, a person, a home, a way of life, the lead's soul; where each step rises)

Built from the quest list and summaries; the rungs are the file's arrangement.
1. Gear and money (first hour): bandits rob the pair, and Henry starts with nothing [6][7].
2. Standing and liberty: imprisoned at Trosky; Henry goes "all the way down the food chain" [6][7].
3. A friend's life: Capon faces execution at quest 7 [4]; RPG Codex says Capon is "accused of poaching" [5].
4. A mission's failure: the betrayal at Nebakov [6].
5. Many other people: the pogrom against Kuttenberg's Jewish community (quest 22, "Exodus") [4][6].
6. A home and a company: Suchdol is besieged; food runs out; the game returns to the prologue's siege ("Last Rites") [4][16].
7. The lead's sense of self: dream appearances of Henry's mother and a dead friend close the epilogue [6]; RPG Codex says they make "little sense" for his development [5].

Weakness: consequences are often softened. PC Gamer says the game "pulls its punches" on choices [3]. RPG Codex: "every crime is eventually forgotten" after a few days, and "crucial NPCs" are immortal [5]. The ladder rises on the page, less in the rules.

## 6. Theme (the question the story asks, in the developers' or critics' words with sources; how the play itself makes the player feel it: systems, mission design, repetition, choices)

Critics' words. "Working your way up from the bottom and coming of age", the fall down "the food chain", Dickens as a model, the dull labour of survival [7]. VideoGamer: "haunted by the same demons as the original" [8]. Studio words: "meaningful storytelling" is a pillar carried from game one, and the quests are "deeper, darker, but also more humorous and goofier" [14]. No writer was found stating the theme as one question.

The question (the file's guess): what does a man owe the lord who lifted him, the friend who stands beside him, and the strangers he can save only by breaking the first two?

How play makes it felt.
- Rules that block and open. Reading, clothes, reputation and the law decide who will talk to Henry and where he can go; people who saw him near a robbed place "report you to the guards" [3]. A skipped skill can close a later job [2].
- Mission design. Quests give several solutions: stealing, bribing, persuasion, fighting [2][3]. Deep Silver describes "Forbidden Fruit", where allying with the miller carries risk from his reputation for deceit, and a weekly Kuttenberg tournament that runs whether or not Henry joins [15].
- Failure without a game over. Quests often continue after a bad result and branch into other storylines [2]. A torture mission can be avoided [2]. Deep Silver says a long production quest lets tasks run in any order or be skipped [15].
- Repetition. One critic: "a game of excess and repetition" [8]. The tedium is also read as the theme [7].

## 7. Side stories and the main line (how optional stories feed the main line's theme or people; two or three named examples)

- "Forbidden Fruit" involves the miller, whose tasks earn entry to the main line's wedding [15][4]; trading a favour or a lie for entry rehearses how far a commoner will bend to get in (link partly a guess).
- "The Mouth of Hell" is a mine quest the designer calls the only true survival horror quest, with mystery, investigation and several outcomes (rational inquiry against giving in to superstition, via the characters Marian and Vladimir) [15]. No source ties it to the main line (guess: it stands alone, a tale about fear and reason).
- VideoGamer calls the Roma feud "one of the best early game quests" and says the Cumans "are humanised" [8]. These widen the world's people rather than the main plot.
- PC Gamer: "about 50 hours in, the game suddenly sprung a whole Thieves' Guild questline" [3]; the file reads it as a late refresh of a long game (guess).

Critics split on quality: "wildly inconsistent," with errand chains beside memorable quests [8].

## 8. Humour and tone (where the comedy comes from; how it sits beside the violence)

- Source of comedy: Henry's excuses when caught, "numerous and hilarious, especially when you fail" [5]; failed pickpocketing as "silly fun" [8]; Henry muttering after each brewing session [3]; Czech idioms put into English by writer John Comer, such as "Are you pulling my pizzle?", per Tom McKay [10]; a skeleton on the game-over screen [2]; RPG Codex says it "feels like a Guy Ritchie movie" [5].
- Register: bawdy, with "colorful language" [2]. Critics also object: "laddish misogyny" [8]; repeated use of "wench" [2].
- Beside violence: actor Luke Dale says the script balances dark moments with humour [10]; the studio calls the quests "deeper, darker, but also more humorous and goofier" [14]; PopMatters credits "George R. R. Martin-flavored plot twists" beside the humour [7].

## 9. What players praise most and least (start from the Steam study counts given below, then add critics' and players' written opinions with sources)

The Steam counts are the team's own study: production/research/lane/STORY-PATTERNS-STEAM-2026-11-20.md (hand reading of story reviews, 20 Nov 2026; the larger figures from the players study of KCD2, GTA V and RDR2 as quoted in production/lane/story/ASPECT-BARS-2026-11-21.md).

Steam hand reading, 43 reviews that speak about the story (small sample): story praised in general 30 (70%); characters worth caring about 10 (23%); missions on rails (complaint) 5; writing and dialogue 5; weak or forgettable story 4; side stories 3; too much talk or cutscenes 3; plot holes, a surprising turn, predictable 2 each; weak ending, moments that hit hard, humour 1 each.

Larger script-coded study, about 3,900 KCD2 Steam reviews: story and characters 32.2%; choices that change what happens 7.1% (RDR2 1.7%, GTA V 1.2%); side stories 13.7% (RDR2 4.1%, GTA V 2.1%); period detail 10.0%; a living world 24.5%.

Reading the counts. Players praise the story as a whole far more than any person or scene. Choices and side stories come up several times more than in the two rival games, fitting section 6. Rails and cutscene complaints are few but match critics.

Critics and writers.
- Praise: PC Gamer, 90/100: plot "tense and intriguing," Henry and Capon "very well done indeed" [3]. GamesRadar: quests "thoroughly written and open-ended," actions "believable and consequential" [2]. PopMatters: dark twists and humour, side quests well made [7]. Metacritic 88 on all platforms, per Wikipedia [6].
- Criticism: PC Gamer, choices softened [3]. VideoGamer (Tom Bardwell): "the dryness of the narrative," Hans an "insufferable oaf," quests "wildly inconsistent" [8]. RPG Codex: the main storyline "the weakest part," rails for hours, fake moral choice [5]. GamesRadar: slow progression, some cringe-inducing dialogue [2].
- Disagreement: the same Henry and Hans pair is the heart of the story for PC Gamer [3] and a chore for VideoGamer [8].

## 10. What a team should take from it (five to eight craft lessons, each one sentence, each tied to a section above)

1. Let the lead's opening want be a job somebody else gave him, then let events take the job away and leave the real need showing (section 2).
2. Build the key friendship by putting the pair in trouble together on every leg, and let the lead rescue the friend at each act's turn (section 3).
3. Make the period's rules (clothes, reading, reputation, the law) decide which doors open, so the lead's rise is something the player does and not a cutscene (sections 2 and 6).
4. Give each quest several routes and let failure continue the story, because players name choices and side stories several times more often than comparable games (sections 6 and 9).
5. Keep chains of cutscenes between quests short and let them be sped up, since rails are the commonest story complaint in the Steam counts and a main charge from RPG Codex (sections 4 and 9).
6. Let consequences bite and stay remembered, since critics fault forgotten crimes, immortal key people and punches pulled (section 5).
7. Pay off the lead's old want early, as the sword and Istvan close act one, so the long middle runs on the new need and the friendship (sections 1 and 2).
8. Use period-flavoured, self-deprecating humour beside the violence, and keep it from sliding into crude jokes at women's expense (section 8).
