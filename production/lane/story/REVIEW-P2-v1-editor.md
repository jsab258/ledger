# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 1: a script editor's note

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7 out of 10. Good people, a real motif and a superb last input, sitting on a third act whose legal machinery does not work and a middle that is described as scenes but will be played as a loop.

1. Tom: 8. The flaw is played rather than stated (the offers, the question, the suitcase), and the want sits in the letter's postscript. He is a little too perfectly the theme's instrument.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Pauline, Terry, Ada and the Bishop are distinct, wanted, and economical. Kevin, Alison and Pauline all deliver the same line ("nobody ever asked me what I wanted"), which turns three people into one idea.
3. The antagonists: 6. The Widow is a genuine creation: lawful, honest, priced and right about half of it. Danny is a charming nuisance and Agar is a letter, a phone call and one good speech; this is exactly the GTA V complaint the bar names.
4. The central mystery: 7. The second-order turn (nobody threatened anybody) is fresh and fair. The first-order truth (Ron drove) is signposted so early that a crime-drama audience will have it by scene 7.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The first night, the carry, Ada's window and Sheila's walk-round are kept and strengthened; the failed-carry branch ("Nowak, T. £80") is excellent. Lesson one as the second retold scene is right.
6. Structure and pace: 6. Three acts and three movements are sound on paper, but Act II carries nine and a half hours on eighteen scenes, and the third act's week is talk at a counter framed by one strong day of doors.
7. The middle: 6. The Empty Seat is the best-planned stretch, with its two set pieces. What fills the half hour between scenes is one paragraph of generic weekly shape, which is where the bar says games are punished.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. It dodges three clichés on purpose and walks into a fourth: the comic sidekick with the secret, found by a glove under a seat.
9. The endings: 8. Seats as verdicts and the handbrake the player may leave alone are as good as anything I have read in a game outline. The legal outcomes inside them are wrong (see section 3), and the Kingdom's plain turn is muddy about why Ron is back.
10. The side stories: 7. The watch and Ivy Leadley are proper stories with turns that touch the spine. Karen's wardrobe is a sketch, and Alison's is a romance with the romance left out.
11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline's hour, Terry's fifth word and the crabs are real laughs built from set pieces. The swearing is almost absent for a 1990 dock town.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The offers press, the typed question and the fixed passenger are systems that carry meaning. The free-typed question is a big promise on the systems, and the last act asks the player to choose a name on a form, which is a menu however it is dressed.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The climax rests on law that does not hold. Where: section 8, Thursday and five o'clock; section 9, both better turns. Driving without a licence or insurance is a summary offence with a six-month time limit, so nobody can be "fined for driving unlicensed" for a night thirteen months gone. What a name on Reese's paper really confesses is the getaway car in a burglary that caused a fire and maimed a man, which is indictable and has no time limit. The bible both overstates the small charge and understates the large one, and Tom's bail is for an offence that cannot be brought. Why it matters: the game's last choice is weighed on these costs, and a reviewer who checks will feel the floor go. Fix: make the stakes the burglary. Reese's note goes to the police; Ellis and the Crown Prosecution Service decide. With Friday's papers (the Bishop, June, the gate) the file reads as an attempted theft and an accidental fire, and the CPS declines on the public interest or Ron takes a suspended sentence; without them it is a charge. Tom's signature then becomes a confession to burglary, which is heavier and better.

2. Ron's reason for selling himself contradicts the world. Where: section 5 (her rule), scene 14, section 10 point 6. Every docker knows the Widow "would as soon talk to the tide", and Ron has known her and her husband for thirty years. The turn requires him to be the one man on the dock who does not know it. Why it matters: it is the hinge of the midpoint, the heart of the friendship and the moral of the theme, and as written it is a lapse of character, not silence. Fix: keep "I never said I'd tell", but make what Ron feared not the police but Tom. She never named who she might tell; Ron, who had just been taught to drive by a man who was pleased with him, sold himself so that the nephew would not learn. The silence was for Tom. That ties the turn to the friendship and keeps every line of scene 14.

3. The first-order mystery is given away. Where: Act I ("Mind that wing", Ron "cannot drive"), scenes 6 and 7. A wing done once, a doorman who cannot drive, driving lessons and a road he will not go down: a viewer of British crime drama solves this in Act I and then waits nine hours to be told. Fix: give the first truth a credible rival. Kevin, twelve years in Mickey's car and already fiddling mileage in scene 3, is the obvious suspect: let the 1989 sheets show his miles short that month too, let Terry's seven words protect him, and let the player follow Kevin before he follows the third portion. Cut "Mind that wing" to a look Sheila gives the wing, and hold Shaun's "second time" until after the Fifth.

4. The middle is a schedule, not a story. Where: section 7, "What a week is, between the scenes", and the budget. Eighteen scenes over nine and a half hours means one scene every half hour, and the paragraph meant to fill the gaps is days of fares, a dusk job and a count, repeated for seven weeks. Why it matters: the bar's commonest complaints (boredom, the same loop, a main line that slows) all live here. Fix: write the gaps as small chains that return. Give each week of Act II one recurring fare who changes (Ivy on Sundays is already the model), one dusk job tied to whichever of the three opponents is moving that week, and one count where Sheila finds something in the books. Name them, as scenes are named.

5. Danny and Agar are pressures without lives. Where: section 5, scenes 10, 16 and 18. Danny has a joke, a lock-up and a fair-enough; Agar has letters and a single walk down Quay Street. Neither is ever in a room with the Widow. Fix: one scene each that is not about Tom. Let Agar need the Widow (his basin needs her labour, and she has priced him too), and let the player see Danny refused by her at the gate: the young man who thinks he is what happens, learning there is something that happens to him.

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Time bar: driving offences of November 1989 cannot be prosecuted in December 1990. Fix: the stakes are the burglary, as above.
2. In 1990 Customs and Excise could assess VAT back six years, not three (the three-year limit came in 1997). Fix: "every year I can reach".
3. Tom signing "T. Nowak" is said to be a confession to driving unlicensed, but Tom holds a licence. Fix: he confesses to driving for a burglary; the operator's licence fails on fitness.
4. Customs and the police are separate services; there is no corridor between Reese and Ellis. Fix: "I am obliged to pass it to the police."
5. Ron says "A year. I could have asked her", but Shiner only told the Widow in October. Fix: "A month."
6. Four men went in, but only Tapper, Shiner and the Bishop are named and accounted for. Fix: three went in, and Ron drove.
7. Ellis's mother lives on Tannery Row, the same row as a man burned on the night of Ellis's fire, and Ellis has not heard of him in a year. Fix: the Bishop lives with his sister over the water, and Cyril takes his packet.
8. Severe burns with no hospital would leave a doctor's record or a dead man. Fix: Mickey paid a retired doctor, who is one of Friday's doors.
9. Dock tallies were numbered to men and employers; a detective could match one in a week. Fix: the register went in the skip with the scheme in July 1989, and says so.
10. Mickey's drawings pay £164 a week from a firm that cannot meet a £38 rent; Reese would see that before the two lines. Fix: the drawings are small and the row's watch carries most of it, and Reese marks the drawings too.
11. Alison's "best part of an hour" does not fit a job that began after ten to eleven and a call at half past. Fix: the brigade is called at a quarter to midnight.
12. Why the men went ahead when the planner had just collapsed is never said. Fix: one line from Ron: the club could not last the winter, and they were already at the landing.
13. Tom drives fares in a dead man's badge from day one, which is itself the licensing offence. Fix: the hearing is about exactly that, and the member who owes Agar says so.
14. Mickey was not a docker, so the memorial reading him among "men the docks have taken" needs a reason. Fix: Walsh reads him because the dockers asked.

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review?

No, not as it stands. Ron in the passenger seat, Sheila's two lines in the fare book and the handbrake the player may leave alone are things I would quote, and the Widow is the best antagonist idea in the bible. But a reviewer who sees the driver coming in Act I, sits through a middle of fares, and then meets a climax built on a charge that cannot be brought will call the story clever and thin. Fix those three and it is a yes.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. The passenger seat as the story's grammar: who sits where, the Widow in Ron's place, Reese in Ron's place, and every ending decided by which seat Ron is in.
2. The handbrake and "I go, you stop": the lessons, the third lesson on the row, and a last input the player can decline. That is play and story as one thing.
3. The Widow who never lies and never threatens, and the turn that nobody threatened anybody. It is the one surprise in the bible that a player has not met before.
