You are a seasoned games writer who has shipped story-driven open-world games. Read the whole of the file PACKPATH and nothing else; open no other file and search nothing. It holds nine story pages. Each is a summary of a game's story, written in one format at one length, with names changed and the setting moved. A summary strips performance, music and play alike for all nine.

The question is not how well made each story is as writing. It is how the players who play it will rate it, one aspect at a time. Players and writers weigh different things. Players reward people they love above all (a lead who is somebody, people they would miss, a bond), then the writing and dialogue, moments that hit hard, humour, an ending that lands, a turn that surprises; they punish a story with no one at its centre, missions that force the way, a rushed or weak ending, a padded or repetitive middle. They care little for neat planting for its own sake, and do not mark a story down for being loud, funny or broad if they love the people. You may recognise some pages; set any reputation aside and judge only what the page shows the player will meet. Judge every page alike: what the page does not show, the story does not have.

The fifteen aspects, judged one at a time and each alone (a story weak overall can be best at one aspect):
A1 The lead: a want, a change or the cost of not changing, a reason to care; not flat, silent or swappable.
A2 The people around him: others with wants of their own, each told apart by what they do.
A3 The central bond: one relationship a player would miss, shown across the story and tested, broken, mended or ended.
A4 The opponent: an antagonist with a want and a presence, faced by the lead, not thin or disposed of off stage.
A5 Writing and dialogue: lines and scenes as far as the page shows them (quoted lines, voice, what is planted and paid).
A6 Moments that hit hard: a loss or reckoning that lands, prepared, after which things are different.
A7 Humour and tone: real comedy, and a tone that holds together.
A8 The turn: a turn that changes what came before and could not be guessed early; the ending not seen coming.
A9 The ending: follows from who the lead has become, settles the bond, a last scene that lands; not rushed.
A10 Pacing and holding together: no sagging or repeating middle, no dropped people or threads, no string of errands.
A11 How the story uses play: the player's hands carry the story; missions not on rails; not told in long talk.
A12 Choices that come back: what the player chose returns and changes later scenes or the ending.
A13 Side stories: optional stories with turns or choices of their own, not errands.
A14 Setting and period: a place and time that feel specific and lived in, and matter to the story.
A15 Mystery and clue-work: a question the player works out from clues, by his own reasoning.

Return, as text:
A. For each aspect A1 to A15: a mark from 1 to 10 for each of the nine stories, as players would rate that aspect of it alone, in one line exactly in this shape:
SCORES A1: X=7 Y=8 ... (all nine letters)
then the best story for that aspect (two letters if you cannot separate them) in one line, BEST A1: X, and one or two sentences on what the best does that makes it best, put as a standard a plan could be tested against (what it does, not its plot).
B. Then, for each story, one line naming its strongest and its weakest aspect.
C. Only after A and B are written and without changing them: if you believe you recognised any page as a particular game, name it and how sure you are (per cent); if none, say none. Name no game anywhere before section C.
