# LEDGER: the story bible, phase 2, version 6

The Passenger. 20 November 2026. Canon outranks this file; nothing here is built; canon.md and RULINGS.md are untouched. Spoken lines are samples of the voice.

How to read it. Section 0 is for the owner and gives away every turn; read it last to meet the story as a player would. Sections 6 to 9 are the story as scenes, in the order played; section 10 is what was true all along.

## 0. Changes to canon or rulings, for his ruling

Each with its reason and the plain alternative.

1. The warehouse fire. The ruling of 30 September stands as the street's version (the owner burned his own warehouse and went south) and "the truth is never the street's". The truth here is new: on Saturday 18 November 1989 three old dockers went into the importer's warehouse, a bonded store, to lift cigarettes for the dockers' sick club; a paraffin heater went over; and the car that took them was one of Mickey's. Mickey was to drive and was on a hospital ward; the one who took the keys was Sheila Dunn. Nobody paid for a fire and nobody meant one. Sheila is otherwise exactly as written, and this is the one line in her that is new. Because the stock was under bond, the night belongs to Customs and Excise, and the VAT inspector of the last act is an officer of the department that was robbed. Alternative: the fire as phase 1 had it.
2. The outfit. Canon: Mickey left "a half-dead criminal outfit". Here it is an old dockers' sick club that pays forty-one families £4 a week, fed for thirty years by what Ron's gang lifted at the dock gate, and since the scheme ended in July 1989 by a weekly payment from the warehouse row and by Mickey's own pocket. Nobody is its secret head.
3. Ada stays what canon says: a widow at her window, on the club's list. This undoes phase 1's first proposed change.
4. Ron's old gang is minted: Tapper, Shiner and the Bishop. Ron was in the building that night, not at the wheel; the Bishop was burned, and Mickey paid his keep every Friday for the rest of his own life.
5. One opponent and two pressures: the Widow is the main opponent; Danny Cammack and Geoffrey Agar are smaller. Canon gives the three equal standing.
6. One love interest, and small: Alison Sedman. Philip Danby is a regular; Keith Garbutt only carries the Widow's offers.
7. Sheila's name for him has two steps ("Mr Nowak", then "Tom"). Tom's line (QUESTIONS 2) is adopted as recommended. His letter has words and his Ledger a voice.

8. The rule about his past is touched once. In the last act Tom can decline to name a driver and answer as the owner; he tells no lie and nobody asks him anything. DS Ellis remarks that he is the one man on the street she knows nothing about. Alternative: she says nothing.
9. Mickey's first heart turn was on the night of the fire. The ruling on his death stands.
10. Three endings shown, five states beneath (QUESTIONS 1); Burn Both has two closes. The inspection has causes: the change of owner, Tom's own wash, and Agar's telephone call.

11. Small things minted: the old fare books on the shelf; the hearing on Tuesday 30 October; the dockers' own fire at the gate; a clause in Agar's lease.

## 1. The spine in a paragraph

In October 1990 Tom Nowak, a stranger with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter from an uncle he never met, takes over a minicab office in a port whose docks have died. With it he gets an old docker who cannot drive and rides in his passenger seat. For three weeks the two of them work the Hook from one car, and on Sunday mornings Ron learns to drive at eleven miles an hour. Then, on Bonfire Night, he is standing at the dock gate in a foreman's coat with another firm's name on the back. The port's labour contractor, a widow who never threatens and prices everything, seems to have taken him the way she takes everyone: she asked him what he wanted. The rest of the game is the player finding out, with a steering wheel and a mileometer, what was done with one of Mickey's cars on the night of last year's warehouse fire, and why a man who would not cross the road for money asked for another firm's coat. Then a mild man from Customs and Excise reads two lines in the fare book and asks to be shown eleven miles. What the books hold, what is written on the paper and who is in which seat decide how Tom ends.

## 2. The theme

You never ask for owt. LEDGER is about what people owe each other in a place the official books have written off, and about how much of it goes unpaid because nobody will ask.

- The club's book is the old answer and the Widow's gate is the new one.
- Tom takes nothing and asks nothing. So does Ron. The story's one real crime was a theft that went wrong; its lasting damage was done by a year of good mornings.

The story never says which answer is right. It makes every answer cost a named person something the player can see.

## 3. Tom Nowak

Tom is 32, the son of a Polish post-war settler and Mickey's sister. He has never been to the Hook. His life before it is unsaid: nobody asks and he never tells. He is not voiced.

The letter. In his hand in the first shot and pasted inside the cover of his Ledger:

"Tom. You don't know me. I was your mother's brother. I have a cab office on Quay Street in Meridian, two cars, and a good woman who keeps the books. It is yours when I am gone, and the doctor says that is sooner than I would like. There is more to it than cabs. There is a book. People are owed. Come and look before you say yes. There is a man on the door called Kirby. Keep him if he'll stay. If you cannot stomach it, shut the door, and nobody will blame you, least of all me. Every fare goes in the book. M. Suddaby. P.S. Don't let them call you Mickey's nephew for ever."

His voice. Tom speaks aloud only in the words the player types or picks. Who he is lives in the Ledger, his notebook: a used one, hard-backed, with the first thirty pages cut out close to the spine. Every entry is short, dry and written from what the player did.

- After the Fifth: "Car one. On my own."

What he wants. A place that is his, and his own name said by people who mean it. The letter's postscript is the want.

His flaw. He lets nobody give him anything, and he asks nobody for anything: not a favour, not a reason, not what they want. He pays for tea that was on the house and carries alone what two could carry. It looks like pride and it is fear: a man who owes nothing and is owed nothing can pick up his suitcase.

The flaw is played, two ways.

- The offers. All through the game people offer Tom things for nothing, and each is one press: take it, or pay for it. Some are gifts (Grace's tea, Terry's flask, Ron's supper) and some have a hook in them (Danny's fifty-pound note, the Widow's lift) and nothing marks which is which. To take is to owe. A man who takes nothing is owed nothing and known by nobody: at the hearing on 30 October only people he has let do him a turn feel entitled to write for him, and on the last Friday only they will open a door. A man who takes from everybody is everybody's, and after the hearing teaches him to take, the hooks get better hidden: the gate's packing shift, a landlord's solicitor, a widow's offer to pay a debt for him.
- The question. At the gate in week two the Widow asks Tom what he wants, and from that morning his Ledger has a page headed "Asked", and the player can put the same question, in his own words, to anyone. The game never suggests it. It is not a key: each person can be asked once; some will not answer (Terry: "Nowt"; Sheila, the first time, goes on writing); some answers cannot be met; and an answer he does not meet stays on the page, in his own hand, where he has to look at it. The Widow never forgets what she was told, either.

The load the flaw cannot carry. Ron is the same man. Two men who never ask sit eighteen inches apart for three weeks, and one of them is carrying something the other could lift if either would open his mouth.

His line. He lays hands on no woman, nobody of sixty or over, and not the priest. The game never offers him the blow.

What he fears. The suitcase. It stays packed under the bed in Mickey's flat until the player unpacks it, one press. Sheila notices the day he does: "You've unpacked."

His past. Ada says it for the street on the first Tuesday: "I'll not ask where you were before. Nobody on this street will. We've all been somewhere."

## 4. The people, as the player meets them

Twenty-one who matter. Nobody on this list is secretly anybody else. What each is carrying is in section 10.

- Ron Kirby, 58. A docker for thirty years, on the door and the rank since the scheme ended. He calls Tom "boss". He cannot drive: "Never needed to. The work was at the end of the street." He wants to be of use the way he was when he had a hook in his belt and a gang.
- Sheila Dunn, 53, the bookkeeper, at Mickey's since she was 22. Exactly as written: "New management", the walk-round, the fare-book rule, the locked door, the Sunday question. She has never been heard to say a thing that was not so. She is sizing him up, as she promised Mickey.
- Maureen Jensen, 50, the Widow. Broad-shouldered, dressed like a harbour master. Her husband ran the dock gate; she runs it better. Since the scheme ended she is the port's labour contractor, registered and lawful: sixty men stand at the gate at six and she picks twenty. She never threatens. She prices, she puts each offer once, and she has kept every deal she ever made.
- Tapper and Shiner, of Ron's old gang, on the Widow's gate now. Tapper says little. Shiner cannot stop.
- The Bishop, 60, of Ron's old gang, who is on nobody's gate. Ron's stories about him stop soonest.
- Pauline Kitching, 44, on the radio: the firm's running voice, heard in every cab at once. Nine years without a Saturday off.
- Terry Hodgson, 52, the night driver: four words a shift, always the right four. Pauline keeps a tally of her attempts to get a fifth out of him on air.
- Kevin Needham, days, twelve years in Mickey's car, who wants a plate of his own.

- Darren Milner, 25, who sells what he hears and wants to be somebody's man.
- Ada, 74, the widow at her window. Walter Harker's widow, on the club's list, lonely, sharp, the best witness on Quay Street. Her tea is tea.
- Also: Cyril Blenkin, the tallyman at the landing; Julie Kettlewell, laid off from the cold store, who can drive; Keith Garbutt, the Fixer, who carries the Widow's offers.
- June Suddaby, 34, Mickey's daughter, night sister at the infirmary; Father Brendan Walsh, who buried Mickey.

- Danny Cammack, 26. Hook Cars, two yellow cars, £1.50 a ride, pagers for his drivers. Loud on purpose, quicker than he lets on.
- Geoffrey Agar, 61, the landlord: silver, unhurried, apologises before every blow. His agents hold the freehold of the parade and half the warehouse row.
- DS Carol Ellis, 44, born two streets from the row. The fire was her file; her inspector closed it as undetermined; she has not stopped. All she has is an hour when nobody was on the row.
- Alison Sedman, 31, the paper's port reporter. One question: where did an hour go on the row last November?

- Mr T. A. Reese, 57, Officer of Customs and Excise, who comes at the end: a flask of tea, a bad knee, a propelling pencil. He is sorry, truly, when a book does not add up.

### Ron and Tom

The friendship is the plot, and it is written for the hands.

- The passenger seat. From the first Thursday Ron rides unless told not to. He names every building by what it was ("That was Stannard's ropewalk. That was the Seamen's Mission. That's still a wall"), and the player learns the map as a list of things that shut. Five times in the game he starts a story about Tapper, Shiner or the Bishop, and each stops one sentence later than the last. The fifth is finished.
- The lessons. On Sunday mornings on the empty dock road Tom teaches a fifty-eight-year-old docker to drive car two. Ron has the wheel, folded behind it with his knees at his ears, because car two's seat sticks; the player has his voice and the handbrake. Ron sets the terms himself: "That's yours, boss. I go, you stop. That's the arrangement." Lesson one: if the player is quick, Ron is stopped a yard short of a bollard; if not, the bollard. Lesson two: he drives the whole dock at eleven miles an hour, talking, and asks to go round again. Lesson three comes late and is not funny. He is really learning, and what the player teaches him is used in the last act.
- Fridays. The Tangs', and the quay wall. Ron buys three of number nine and puts one inside his coat. "Feller I know." Ron pays, if Tom lets him.
- How it pays off. In every ending Ron is in a vehicle, and which seat he is in is the verdict.

## 5. The opponent, and two pressures

### Maureen Jensen, the Widow: his people, by asking

- Want: every job at the gate through her hands; Mickey's as the gate's cab firm; and the old club's book shut. "A pension for men who'll never work is a tax on men who do." Eleven on the list could still work, and will not sign with her while the four pounds comes.
- Why she is right: sixty men in work, a club that is dying anyway, and Ron a better foreman than doorman.
- Why she is dangerous: she takes a firm apart by offering each of its people exactly what they want, once.
- How she comes, four ways. Offers to his people, carried by Keith Garbutt. The gate's custom: fifty pence on every fare through it, then the cold-store shift run, given or withheld. Her own body: when he will not pay, her men stand across the cold-store road at half past five and she stands in front of them, hands in her coat pockets, and his line means he can do nothing but wait, pay or turn round. And twice she books car one and sits in Ron's seat.
- Her rule, which the story never lets her break: she keeps every deal; she tells nothing unasked and no lie when asked straight; she holds nothing over anybody; and she has never spoken to a police officer about anyone, which every docker knows. The player can test it in week two: asked straight whether his day driver is carrying for her, she says, "Yes. You didn't ask before."

### Danny Cammack: his cover, in daylight

- Want: the Hook, and the end of the old lot. "You've got the keys. I've got the accent. One of us is in the wrong house."
- How he comes: not with a brick at first. He puts Hook Cars' number on the freephone in the ferry terminal. He rides in a Mickey's cab and pays a two-pound fare with a fifty: "Keep it. Call it rent." Taken, it is in the Tangs' queue by night that Mickey's pays Cammack rent, and it is read out at the hearing. He takes whatever the street leaves undefended, and says so: "I'm not doing owt to you, Nowak. I'm just what happens when nobody's on a door."
- He is the first to work out who the two strangers in car coats were (scene 13) and that there is something about car two (scene 17), and he is the one opponent who hurts somebody. He has an opponent of his own: the gate will not hire a Cammack, and the player sees him told so, and learns why on the last Thursday.

### Geoffrey Agar: his clean money, by the calendar

- Want: the yard behind Mickey's, which carries the only right of way to the old basin. His loan on the basin falls due on the thirty-first of December.
- How he comes: by letters, each with a date in it. A rent review, "with regret". A referral of Tom's licence to the sub-committee. And he has held one thing back for a year: his watchman saw a cab on the row the night his building burned.
- He needs the Widow, and cannot afford her; the player drives him to the gate to hear her price (the week of the letter).
- His clock is real. If the yard is not his by the thirty-first, the bank has the basin; so he spends what he was saving, and his agents lock the yard on the one day it bites.

## 6. Act I: The Inheritance

Monday 15 to Sunday 21 October 1990. About two hours. The most scripted act. The beats of the approved first week stand; what this version adds is marked.

### Monday: the arrival and the first night

1. The ferry (0:00). The letter in his hand over grey water, the last line readable: "Every fare goes in the book." Nobody meets him. He has £23.50.
2. Quay Street (1:30). A line each, in passing: Darren, Ron, Ada at her window, June.
3. Sheila's walk-round (1:45). Her six lines as approved. "New management." The fare book: "Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule, and it's mine now." Added, on the forecourt: "Car two was Mickey's own. The seat sticks. Kevin's given up." Kevin, six foot, drives it all game with his knees round the wheel, and says so on the radio. Ron, of the coat still on the hook: "Small man, Mickey. Sat on a cushion to see over the wheel."
4. The first fare (2:30). Father Walsh, from the infirmary to his house by the church, Pauline giving the way by landmarks. Sheila hands him Mickey's badge: "Wear it till the council sits." The priest, at his door: "You missed a good funeral. Forty-one envelopes on the plate, and every one of them empty. They wanted him to know they'd come."
5. The rest of the office (4:30). The locked door, the flat, the debt book: five names, £156. Friday is rent, £38, and five wage packets.
6. The first deed (5:30). Darren's £22. Somebody sees.
7. The first ask (7:00). Ron, the coat, the envelope. "Who's it for?" "Tallyman. That's all I'll say on a pavement, boss."
8. The carry (9:00). As written and as section 13 finishes it.
9. The landing (11:30). "Mickey's?" The thumb, the water. "Right. Wednesday, same again. And tell Kirby the round's twenty this week, not twenty-one." Ron counts £19 carrier's money into Tom's hand.

### Tuesday to Thursday

The approved beats, in order: Darren says the night to Tom's face; the first count; the other four debts; Ada's "What were you doing in my alley?" and her one invitation; Wednesday's second envelope against her tea.

Added or changed:

- Grace will not take money for his first tea. Take it or pay: the first offer the game counts. Terry's flask, on Tuesday night's rank, is the second.
- Ada on the first Tuesday, on her step: "I'll not ask where you were before. Nobody on this street will. We've all been somewhere."
- The tea. Ada tells him plainly what he has been carrying: the sick club her Walter started in 1953, forty-one names, four pounds, half over the water. "The tallyman keeps the far side. The gate used to fill it. I get mine like the rest." Then, of his doorman, as a recommendation: "He's a good man, Ron Kirby. He came home at two one morning last November with no sleeves to his coat, and I never asked. You'll find I don't."
- Alison in the cab on Wednesday, to the warehouse row. "It was a bonded store, did you know? The brigade were called at a quarter to twelve. The watchman swore he was on his rounds. Nobody was on that row for the best part of an hour. I'd like to know where an hour went." She leaves her umbrella on the back seat. Give it back, or keep it till she comes for it.
- "How did Mickey die?" Sheila: "At that desk, with the kettle on. His heart. He'd had one warning, a year ago, and went back to nights the week after."
- A letter from the council: he has been driving on a dead man's badge, and his own application will be heard on Tuesday 30 October.
- Thursday night, two dockers waiting by car two: the first time Ron rides. Afterwards, in the car: "You never ask for owt, do you. Mickey was the same. Dead at a desk with the kettle on."

### Friday: rent, wages and the round

- Rent to Mr Agar's agents; wages by hand. From this Friday Sheila says at every count whether the week's books would stand a stranger reading them.
- Night five, the third ask: a holdall. The Hook's half of the club goes round by hand on Fridays, twenty doors, and Ron walks it. "You'll want to see where it goes, boss. Or you won't. Mickey always walked it." A carry with faces: Winifred Duggleby; Albert Suggitt mending a net; Ada at her own door; a door on Tannery Row where Ron says "Knock soft. Her daughter's police"; and, last, across the Hook on Ropery Lane, one door where Ron goes up alone with a packet that did not come out of the holdall. "Not that one. That's Mickey's own."
- Then the Tangs', three of number nine, and the quay wall.

### Saturday and Sunday

- Saturday. Danny Cammack on Quay Street, buying chips, friendly.
- Sunday, early. The first lesson, in car two, off the road and off the clock. Ron has a provisional licence in his coat, taken out the week after the funeral ("There should be two can drive in a firm, boss. Time was there weren't"), and L-plates he has cut from a biscuit tin. "That's yours, boss. I go, you stop." The bollard, or a yard short of it. The bollard has a stripe of old paint on it already, the firm's colour.
- Sunday, ten o'clock. Sheila comes in on her day off. If she trusts him: "Mr Nowak." The first time. The real book is on the counter, shut. "That's the club's book and what Mickey ran beside it. You've had your week. So which is it going to be?" Wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say; asked twice, as built. The street learns the answer.

## 7. Act II: The Rise and the Squeeze

Monday 22 October to Sunday 2 December. About nine and a half hours.

Three movements: the Seat (about four and a half hours), the Empty Seat (about three), and Two in the Car (about two). Seventeen scenes. Three things the player does in these weeks belong to nobody's job and nobody asks for them: following the third portion, driving the eleven miles, and having a seat mended.

What a week is, between the scenes. Days are fares, dusk is a job, night is the count, and Friday is wages, rent and a round that is never the same twice: twenty doors, then nineteen, then eighteen, as people die, sign with the gate or send the packet back. No week repeats: each has its own returning fare, a dusk job set off by whichever opponent is moving, and one thing Sheila finds in the count.

### First movement: the Seat

Scene 1. Tapper, and the freephone. Danny's number is on the freephone in the ferry terminal, and the boat's fares go to yellow cars. Ron brings Tapper, who works the ferry's ropes and knows when the ticket hall is empty. A night's work with a screwdriver, a word with the clerk, or a price war. After: "Tapper could tap a tea chest so it sang the weight. One time him and the Bishop had a crate of..." Rain by four. He does not finish.

Scene 2. The gate at six. The Widow books a car herself and gets into the front. "Save you turning round." Ron has to get into the back, and does, without a word. Sixty men in the dark, twenty picked; Shiner first, talking. One man of sixty with a ruined hand stands in the line every morning and is never picked, and nobody says why. Her first offer, put once: fifty pence on every fare through the gate. Then, to the driver, the first person in the Hook to do it: "And what do you want, Mr Nowak? I'd guess nobody's asked." The player types an answer, or does not, and she remembers which; her last offer is priced on it. Getting out, not looking at Ron: "You're wasted on a door, Kirby. You always were." Ron moves back to the front before he speaks. "Shiner. Shiner could talk a customs man out of his own hat. He was talking the night we..." He stops. Choice: pay the fifty pence, or do not.

Scene 3. The long way round. Kevin's day fares come in short against his mileage; Sheila sees it in the book before anyone says it. Followed, he is carrying parcels for the gate in Mickey's car. The player can ask the Widow straight, and gets a straight answer ("Yes. You didn't ask before"), or front Kevin on the rank, in front of the street: "Twelve years I've driven his car. She asked me what I wanted. Nobody here ever did." Choice: sack him; keep him and feed the gate what Tom wants it to hear; or ask him the question now. Asked, he tells: a plate of his own. It can be met, and it costs: an owner-driver is less a man's own. Comes back: Kevin is the firm's leak, his or hers, and from here the player thinks of Kevin whenever a car has been somewhere it should not.

Scene 4. The hearing, Tuesday 30 October. The council's licensing sub-committee, one room in the town hall. A member who owes Agar has referred the application over the dead man's badge, and every hook Tom has taken is in the papers. Agar himself is in the back row with his hat on his knee, and in the corridor after, whatever the result: "I do apologise. It is nothing personal. I should like your yard." Only people Tom has let do him a turn have written a reference. If he has paid his way with everybody, the clerk says "No references," and waits. Then the door at the back opens, and the public bench fills: twenty old dockers in funeral suits, caps on knees, Tapper and Shiner among them. Nobody asked them. Ron is standing by the door. "I can't write you one, boss. I can fetch them as'll sit." It is the biggest gift in the game, and he was never offered the press. Licensed; licensed with conditions; or refused, after which he owns the firm and may not drive a fare, and every one he drives is a risk. None ends the game.

Scene 5. The cold-store road. If the fifty pence was refused, or left three days, it is answered: the Widow's men stand across the cold-store road at half past five, and she stands in front of them. Tom's line leaves him four things: wait; pay; turn round and lose the shift; or send Ron. If Ron goes, he gets out, walks to his old gang, says six words the player cannot hear, and the line opens like a gate. The Widow watches him do it. That is the moment she decides what she wants, and the player sees her decide. If it was paid, she asks Ron to settle a row between two gangs at the gate, and he does, in six words.

Scene 6. Danny at the gate. A fare: Danny Cammack, to the dock gate, in a good mood. He wants the gate's custom for his yellow cars and has come to offer the Widow sixty pence where Mickey's pays fifty. She does not get down off her pallet. "I'll not hire a Cammack and I'll not ride with one. Your father knows why. He's stood in my line every morning since the gate was mine, and he knows why." It is the only time in the game Danny is quiet in public. In the cab going back: "You saw nowt." That week Keith Garbutt is seen twice at the rank after dark, talking to Ron, who listens with his arms folded. Asked: "Fixer. Fixing."

Scene 7. The second lesson. Sunday 4 November. L-plates.  Ron drives its whole length at eleven miles an hour, talking the whole way, and asks to go round again. At the far end the warehouse row opens ahead of them. He stops short of it. "Not down there, boss. Surface is bad." It is not. Asked about it: "That's all I'll say in a car with a radio in it." Then, getting out, a thing he has never done: "I'll want tomorrow night, boss. Summat I've to see to." He has never asked for anything.

What the player believes going into the Fifth: Kevin is the man he might lose, and Danny is the man to watch on Bonfire Night ("I'd park them cars indoors Monday, if I was you"). A watchful player has more: a coat with no sleeves, a third portion, a road Ron will not drive down, and a Fixer on the rank. Nothing stops him asking Ron, on the quay wall on Friday, the question he has by now asked Kevin. If he does, the midpoint is a different scene.

### The midpoint: the Fifth

Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990. The quay is full for the fireworks off the old coal jetty, and at the dock gate the dockers have their own fire, as every year: pallets and dunnage, a blaze the size of a van. It is the busiest night of the cab year, and the first Tom has driven without Ron: twenty minutes of the best fares in the game, the cars kept moving because Danny said to park them.

1. The fare. In Pauline's ordinary voice: "Car one, the dock gate, on the Widow's account, and she says bring a coat, there's a fire." At the gate the Widow is standing on a pallet with a clipboard, reading the winter's hire list by firelight. Beside her, straight-backed, in a foreman's donkey jacket with THE GATE across the shoulders, is Ron. His old gang is round him. He sees the cab. He does not come over.
2. The fire. On top of the dockers' bonfire, where the guy goes, somebody has sat a figure in a car coat with a cab's roof sign hung round its neck. The sign says MICKEY'S. It is Danny's lads' joke. Ron does not laugh. He does not take it down either. He watches it burn with his hands in the pockets of her coat. Half the Hook sees who stood by.
3. The radio. On the dock road, going back, Pauline, to every cab at once, kindly, which is worse: "Car one. You on your own?"
4. The window. With nobody on the door, Danny's lads have put the office window in while the street watched fireworks. Ada is on her step in her coat. "You'd better come in, Tom." It is the first time anyone on the street has used his name. Tea, and an old woman talking to fill a bad hour: who did the window; how Mickey taught half the street to drive on that dock road: June, before they fell out; Pauline, who put him in the dock at low water and never tried again; Sheila Dunn; Father Walsh's curate; and gave Ada up as a bad job. Then the thing she has been turning over: "He'll not have gone for money. I've known Ron Kirby forty years. He'd not cross the road for money."

What is lost: the seat. In the morning his door key is on the office counter.

What the player believes: his friend has been bought, like Kevin, by the same woman, and he did not see it coming. Ada's sentence is the first crack.

If the player asked first. Ron has never been asked what he wants, and it takes him until Sunday to answer: "To be let alone, boss. That's all. She's offered me the gate. ### Second movement: the Empty Seat

About three hours. Ron is never out of sight. Every morning at six he is across the dock road, picking twenty men. On the second morning he picks something else, without looking across: "Mickey's. Two cars for the packing shift. Six sharp." A gift from a foreman to his old firm. Take it or pay.

"22.50. Car one. Office to Infirmary. One. No charge."

"Night. Car two. Out and back. Eleven miles. No sheet. No charge."

Then it came back." The player has found, with a steering wheel and a dial, what nobody has said: whoever was driving left the row with the fire behind them, and turned round.

He has a coat with no sleeves, a bollard, and a man who will not go down the row. He is wrong, and everything he has found is true.

If Ron never left (the player asked first). The movement plays with the seat full, in three scenes of its own, and it is harder. ### Third movement: Two in the Car

About two hours.

Scene 15. The third lesson. Sunday 18 November, a year to the day, and a ship in on Sunday overtime. The player goes to the gate at six and holds out the keys. "I go, you stop." The player has a route in his notebook and means Ron to drive it. Ron drives, knees at his ears, at eleven miles an hour, and he is good now, as good as the player has made him: the landing, the dock road. At the mouth of the warehouse row he brakes, by himself, for the first time in three lessons, and puts the handbrake on with his own hand. "I've never been down here in a car, boss. I went down it on my feet and I come up it with a man on my back, and there was no car. It had gone." A long time. "Then it come back. I'll say that. It come back." He reaches down and tries the seat, which does not move. "Still where she left it." He has said one word too many, and he knows it, and he does not take it back. The man was not pretending. He was learning, so that there would never again be a night when the only one in the firm who could drive was a bookkeeper of fifty-two.

Then the question the player was never given the chance to answer: "I went down there to keep one man's mouth shut. What do you think, boss?" Come back: he comes, and the gate has lost the best foreman it had, and Shiner is off the night gate by Monday, with a name in his mouth. Stay: he stays as a free man, which is the best thing for Ron, and the seat is empty for good. Or tell him it is his to decide, and ask him what he wants. He does not know. That answer comes in Act III.

Scene 16. The Widow in the passenger seat. She books car one and sits in front, in his seat. I'd as soon talk to the tide as to that sergeant." Then one thing he does not have, because her men drink their tea with the row's watchman: "He saw a cab on that row. He told Mr Agar a year ago. Mr Agar has been saving it." And an offer nobody could call a threat: the gate will pay the Bishop's Fridays, as its own, for good. It is generous, and it would make the firm's oldest debt hers.

Scene 17. Car two is gone. The anniversary has put the fire back on every counter. Ellis walks the row again with the watchman, then comes to the yard and looks at car two's wing, wet with new paint. "Whose is this car to drive?" She asks while a thing is answerable. That night the yard gate is forced and car two is not there in the morning. Danny has it in his lock-up, and the price is the freephone and the rank. He knows only that a sergeant looked at it. Refused, he does the first thing in the game that hurts somebody: a yellow car crowds car three off the dock road into the wall, and Julie Kettlewell, three weeks in the job, has her arm in plaster and says she will be in on Monday. Fetching car two back is a break-in with three keys: a key cut from one look at the padlock; the roof light; or the door, with whoever will stand beside him: Ron, if he is back; Darren in a coat too big for him, brave and no use; Terry; nobody. Danny, after: "She weren't meant to hit the wall." It is not an apology.

The act ends on a letter: courteous, dated, signed T. A. Reese. It comes on Monday 3 December and names Monday the 10th.

## 8. Act III: The Ledger Comes Due

Monday 3 to Saturday 15 December. About two and a half hours. The ledger comes due as a drive, not a desk. Two weeks: Agar's, then the inspector's.

### Why the inspector comes

The firm is registered for VAT because of its account work, and the name on the form changed when a registered trader died, so every player gets the letter. Tom's own wash, if he has one, gives it teeth. And a gentleman has telephoned to say that one of the firm's cars was out on a certain night with nothing written.

It matters more than Agar knows, for a reason in plain sight since Alison's first fare: the warehouse was a bonded store, its cigarettes under the Crown's lock. The night the bond burned belongs to Customs and Excise, and the mild man who is coming about the takings is an officer of the department that was robbed.

### The week of the letter

A fare for Mr Agar. Geoffrey Agar has never been in a minicab. In the week of the letter he books one, by letter, to the dock gate, and sits in the back with his hat on his knee. Four hundred berths want building and every pair of hands on the quay is the Widow's. He asks her price standing in the mud. It is fair, and it is more than he has until the basin is his, and the basin is not his without the yard behind Mickey's. Both of them look at the driver. At Quay Street it is raining, and he gets out and stands in it, and leaves the hat on the seat. "I have never had to burn anything, Mr Nowak. Things on that quay fall down by themselves, and I buy what is left. My watchman saw one of your uncle's cabs on that row, and I kept it a year, because it was his heater and my insurance. The insurers have paid, and on the thirty-first the bank buys what is left of me. So I have telephoned the Crown. I do apologise." Then, in another voice: "Somebody of yours may want a solicitor. I keep three. That is a gift." Take it or pay.

The yard. On the Thursday Agar's surveyor comes with two men and a padlock: the lease lets the freeholder close the yard for a survey, and he never has. Every car the firm owns is behind that gate. The lock comes off for a signature on an option to buy. Sign; force it, with a sergeant across the road; or let Ron's old gang do what dockers do with an obstacle, and lift a ten-foot gate off its hinges and lean it against the wall with the padlock still locked. "We never touched his lock."

### The week of the inspector

Monday 10 December. Reese at the counter with the book since October. "A fare at no charge is either a kindness or a hole, Mr Nowak. I'm paid to ask which."

Tuesday. The ride-along. "I always ride a route or two. Mileage is a truthful thing." He gets into the front passenger seat without asking. "My father drove a cab. I always rode in front." He sits where Ron sat, with his flask. If Ron is back, he gets into the back, and the two of them discuss the weather with great seriousness. Brenda gets in with a box of live crabs that does not stay shut. "Are those declared, Mrs Laverack?" "They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in."

Wednesday. The old books. He reads both lines aloud. "Ten to eleven, car one, office to infirmary, one, no charge. A kindness. And the same night, car two, out and back, eleven miles, no sheet, no charge. Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak." He takes off his glasses. "I know that date. Every officer in this collection knows it. I was not sent about a bond, and I had hoped not to find one." Sheila has not moved. What he needs, by Friday: who drove, in the driver's own hand; and the eleven miles, shown to him on the road. "If it is one night, and I can see where it went and that nothing came away from that building, I shall write what I saw and what I recommend, and they generally take my recommendation. If nobody will say, the Investigation Division will ask instead, and I must assess six years of this firm as though every unsheeted night were a working shift. A little under four thousand pounds. I should not like to do either."

Wednesday night: Sheila's kitchen. Tom has never been to her house and has to find it. The 1989 book is on the oilcloth with the slip in it, and beside it a driving licence that has lain in a drawer since 1961. The player already knows. She says four sentences, with her back to him. "He went down at ten to eleven and the men were at the landing. There were forty-one in that book, so I took them. I wrote it down on the Monday, because every fare goes in the book. Nobody reads a fare book." What happened on the row she does not say here; she has never said it aloud. There is a pen on the table and a fire in the grate. He can leave both lines. He can strike the first, the true one, Mickey's alibi, so that a dead man drove. He can put the book on the fire, after which the books cannot hold. She does not ask him to do any of them; she has waited a year and will wait until Friday for somebody else to decide. Or he can open his Ledger at the page headed "Asked". If she went on writing the first time, weeks ago, she turns round now. "To have it read. And to write my own name to it, and not have it written for me." It is the answer the best ending is made of. On the pavement outside is Ron. "She's not to sign it, boss. I was there. And I can drive now, near enough. You've seen to that."

Thursday: two doors, and somebody in the way at each. The phone box on the corner, five coins. He can ring only people he once let give him something, and every call that is answered is a door. What he brings back is what Reese and Ellis will have in front of them.

- Ropery Lane, for the Bishop's own account. Danny's yellow cars are parked nose to tail across the end of the lane. Round, through, or the question. Asked what he wants, Danny is a long time answering, and it is not the rank. "A day's work at that gate for my dad before he's past it. He talked to a customs man once, and she's not picked him one morning since the gate was hers." It can be got. It means asking her.
- The gate, at the six o'clock pick, in front of sixty men, for Tapper and Shiner's word on paper, and now for a day's work for Frank Cammack. It is the Widow's last scene. Her men's names on a Customs paper puts her gate beside a burned bond. If Tom has taken her lifts and left her fifty pences owing and answered her question with nothing, she prices it once, aloud: the firm. Mickey's for two signatures. If he has dealt straight with her all game she looks at him for some time. "Go on, the pair of you. Tell his father Monday, six sharp. I'll not have the lad." It costs her, and the player sees it cost: by Friday the cold store has given its labour contract to a firm from over the water, because her gate's name is on a paper about a bond. She does not mention it.

Friday: the eleven miles. Reese is on the forecourt at two with his flask and a clipboard. Whose hand the name is in decides who drives, and the player has had since Wednesday night to decide whether to let either of them write it.

In the back are an officer of Customs and Excise and, because he will not be left behind, Ron, who has to be folded in. She drives the night: the landing, the row, the burnt plot; and then, with one man in the back writing it down and the other looking out of the window, the four miles to the bollard. The prompt is under the player's hand as it was on the dock road in October. I was here before I could hear what they were shouting. Then I turned round." Ron, to the side window: "You come back, though." It is the first thing either has said to the other about it in a year. - "R. Kirby", in a docker's capitals. It is a lie, told for her by the man she left. ## 9. The endings

States, never a menu. Three shown, five beneath, in the built order. Each has a last scene, a line written in the fare book and a line Tom writes in the Ledger. In each the verdict is which seat Ron is in, and each says where the three opponents stand.

### The Kingdom

Reached when the business stands and the books hold.

The plain turn. A name that is not the driver's is on the paper. If it is Mickey's, the alibi is struck and Sheila's keys are on the counter on top of the fare book. If it is Ron's, she is still at the counter and has not looked up since, and Ron is answering in January for a thing he did not do. Last scene: the landing after ten. A carrier in a dark coat that does not fit him walks the envelope down the lit quay; it is Darren, if he was given the door. The tallyman asks, for the first time, "Nowak's?" Her own name is on the paper in her own hand, because Tom did not write one for her and did not let Ron. She drove the eleven miles and stopped herself at the bollard. ### The Straight Life

Reached when no business of his stands at the close and one friendship holds.

The better turn (the built Quiet). His own signature is on the paper and no driver's name. Customs have assessed, and the council will not license an owner with that night standing open, so the firm is Julie Kettlewell's, arm out of plaster, on a loan Father Walsh signed for; Sheila keeps Julie's book, in a small upright hand, and Tom drives for them both. On the wind-down road the society pays out in the open that Friday, forty-one signatures, Ada's first. Last scene: Saturday, the rank. Car two is at the kerb with L-plates on and Ron at the wheel, knees at his ears, and Ron looks across. He drives him round the Hook at eleven miles an hour, and what he says on the way is the truest line in the ending: "You never ask for owt. You've just done it again. She'd have signed it. So would I. You never asked." It is a gift, and it is the flaw, and it is both. Sheila has found out for herself what he gave up and for whom; it is the thing she says "Tom" for. Danny says "Morning, Tom" in the Tangs' queue. Fare book, her hand: "Nowak, T. Round the Hook. No charge." Ledger: "Ron drove."

Sheila clears a desk of thirty-one years and gives him the last fare book: "It's a full one. I'd not throw it out." Nobody has read it. The ferry goes out without him. The gate's van passes with Ron in the passenger seat, and Ron lifts one hand. Somebody passing says, "Morning, Tom." Fare book: "Closed." Ledger: "Stayed."

### Burn Both

Reached when nothing else fits. Two closes.

The Reckoning, for the man whose own books did not hold whatever he wrote. "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak." He calls, "Nowak!" The player has the two inputs of night one, stand or run, and the close cuts on the press. Fare book: closed unwritten. Ledger: the last page cut out, as the first thirty were.

The Suitcase, for the man who never asked anybody anything. A grey afternoon. He walks down the gangway he walked up. Darren asks him for a pound. ## 10. What was true all along

For the builders: the truth in the order the player can find it. The street never has it. The street says the importer burned his own warehouse and went south; his insurers, finding nothing either way, paid.

1. The club. Walter Harker's sick club of 1953 grew a second purse under the dock scheme: cargo short-tallied at the gate by Ron's gang and moved in Mickey's cabs. Forty-one names at £4 is £164 a week. Since the gate stopped in July 1989 the row's tenants have paid a watch of about £90, and Mickey found the rest by drawing no wage and driving nights, which is why his heart went.
2. The night. By November 1989 the club could not last the winter. The importer's bonded store held cigarettes due to ship on the Monday. Mickey planned it: three men in (Ron, Tapper, the Bishop), Shiner on the corner, twenty minutes, the watchman on his break, himself at the wheel. He is not innocent; he is only not the driver. At ten to eleven his heart turned at his desk, and Terry took him up in car one. The men were already at the landing. There were two people left in the office who had ever held a licence, and one of them was on the floor. Sheila, who had kept the club's book for thirty years and knew what a winter without it meant, hauled car two's seat as far forward as it would go, and took them.
3. The fire. The watchman's paraffin heater went over among the cartons. Nothing was taken. Tapper and Shiner ran for the car, shouting, and Sheila drove. She was at the bollard on the dock road, two miles off, with the wing gone, before she could hear what they were shouting. She turned round. Ron had gone back in for the Bishop and had him on his back at the mouth of the row, with no sleeves to his coat. She drove them to the Bishop's sister's on Ropery Lane, because an infirmary asks questions and Mickey was in it; an old ship's surgeon did what could be done, and the street was told a stove went up. Eleven miles. They laid the Bishop across the back seat; his tally slid forward into the driver's seat runner and jammed it, and the seat has been a short woman's ever since. She has twice struck its repair off the garage's bill.
4. The year. On the Monday she wrote what the car did, because every fare goes in the book, and could not write who. Mickey came off the ward, paid the Bishop's keep every Friday, and told nobody. Ron said good morning to her every day and never once spoke of it; neither did she. She has never driven again. Agar's watchman, back from his hour, saw a cab leaving the row and told his employer, who kept it, because it was his own man's heater and his own insurance.
5. The letter. "People are owed" meant the forty-one and one more. "A good woman who keeps the books" was all Mickey could write of her.
6. "New management." She has been waiting a year for a reader and has been afraid of one. It is why she keeps his name at arm's length.
7. The lessons. Ron cannot drive, and wants to, for one reason: so that there is never again a night when the only one in the firm who can is a bookkeeper. He does not say so.
8. The coat. In October the Widow offered Ron the gate. He said no. Then Shiner said in the Tangs' queue that he could tell them who drove a Mickey's cab to the fire, if he had a mind. Ron went down to the gate that Friday and asked for the coat, the first thing he had asked anyone for in his life, because a foreman says who works where. He left the only friend he had made in ten years to keep a name out of a queue; and it was the name of the woman who drove off and left him. The Widow asked no reason and holds nothing over anybody.

The turns, in the order they land. That it was Ron is what the first week is built to make every player believe; Kevin is there for the player who wants someone else, and Mickey, a small man with a seat set forward, for the one who reads furniture. The turns are that the car went and came back (scene 12, on a dial); that Ron was not bought (scenes 13 and 14); and that it was never Ron (scene 15: a seat, a road, one word).

What the truth changes: "Every fare goes in the book", said in minute two by the one person with a line of her own in it; the name she will not use; the seat; a year of good mornings; the effigy Ron watched burn; and "You never ask for owt", said by a man who has not asked one question in a year of the woman at the next desk.

Why it surprises. The reader is handed a suspect in minute seven. The one nobody suspects is the story's fixed point, who has never said a false thing, and still has not.

Why it is fair. The seat is named in the walk-round and is a joke for nine hours; Mickey was small, so it tells nothing until his own fare book puts him on a ward. The repair is struck off the garage's bill twice, in ink. Ada lists who Mickey taught, and June says who never drove again. The Bishop says the car came back, and who was on foot. The tally is in the runner. And Ron stops at the mouth of the row.

## 11. The story of the player who winds the firm down

On the first Sunday he tells Sheila it is a cab firm and nothing more. The carry, the round and the watch stop. June, in his cab on the Monday, says what this road is built on: "It doesn't have to be shut. It has to be lawful. There are friendly societies older than the docks. You'd want a room, a bank book and the names."

The friendship, the gate and the night are the same story on both roads: Ron's coat, the seat, the dial, the row, the kitchen and the eleven miles all happen here. What differs is the job each week holds, who is in the way, and what the Fifth costs.

What the Fifth costs here. He holds the hall, and while he holds it nobody holds the office: the window goes in and the effigy burns. Then Ada stands up in a room he has just fought for and says, "A club is a thing you're in. A charity is a thing done to you. I'll not be means-tested by Mickey's nephew," and half the room follows her out. Then the fare to the gate, and Ron in the coat.

Its last act is the same officer, the same line and the same eleven miles: he washed nothing, and the night can still close him. Its good ending is the Straight Life's better turn with the society paying out in the open, or the Kingdom's better turn with the firm and the society both his.

## 12. Side stories that are whole stories

The thirty-two drafts of 8 November stay as the short ones. Four are grown into whole stories.

1. The watch (Ron). Found: Mickey's watch in the pawnshop window the week he died. Turn: Ron pawned it himself, for the funeral flowers, because the office was frozen and he would not ask Sheila. Ends: redeemed and handed back without a word, or left in the window. Turn two, after scene 15: the wreath was paid for by the man Mickey's plan left in a fire.

2. A ride for the sergeant (Carol Ellis, Ivy Leadley). Found: rain, night, the sergeant's own car dead by the fish market, and Pauline, dry: "It's the police. She wants a car." The address is the door on Tannery Row where Ron said to knock soft. After that Ivy Leadley, seventy-one, books Mickey's every Sunday for chapel. Turn: Ivy knows exactly what her four pounds is. She has told her daughter for years that it comes from the chapel, because her daughter would stop it; and her daughter, who is nobody's fool, has chosen to believe her. Each is protecting the other from a thing both know. Ends: he keeps Ivy's confidence; tells Ellis; or lets Ivy know that he knows. It decides how Ellis reads a folder on the last Friday.

3. One way (Karen Grummitt). Light. The ferry's ticket clerk, a wardrobe roped to a cab roof, a landlady's step it must pass twice, and a boat she cannot make herself get on. She is the clerk who is on, or not, the night two strangers come off the last ferry.

4. The umbrella (Alison Sedman). Three beats: her fare to the row and the umbrella left; an evening on the quay wall when she reads his Ledger over his shoulder and laughs out loud, the first time anyone in the Hook has found him funny; and a fare to the row that she stops halfway. Her editor has offered her the news desk to leave the fire alone, and she asks Tom what she should do. Turn: the person being bought in her story is her, and the man she asks knows who drove. Ends: she takes the desk; she keeps the row and loses it; or he tells her, and she holds it until he says.

## 13. The scene in the first half hour a player retells

The carry, minute nine, kept as written: three routes with honest costs (the lit quay, the dark yard and lane, the cab with the firm's name on its roof), a constable who can turn round, and next morning Ada asking what he was doing in her alley and Darren saying it to his face.

If the constable takes the envelope, he is honest, so Ellis has it by morning. Sheila, at the count: "Twenty people over the water are short this week. You don't know what that means yet." And in Mickey's debt book, under the five names, she writes a sixth in her own hand: "Nowak, T. £80."

The second is lesson one.

## 14. Humour and the mix of tones

Grounded noir carries the main line. The comedy is dry, local and attached to people; with no drink in the game, the radio is the bar.

- Pauline's commentary goes to every cab at once. "Kevin, wherever you are, you are not in Copper Row, because I can hear the gulls." On her Saturday, with Tom on the radio, Terry is asked his position four times and uses a fifth word: "Tell Pauline it don't count."
- A set piece built for laughing in every stretch: the lessons and Kevin's knees; Pauline's hour and the two strangers; Agar in a minicab with his hat on his knee; the crabs.

The darkest scenes are played quiet and never joked across.

## 16. How play carries it, and the budget

Nothing is on rails, and every turn of the truth can be found early: the third portion can be followed in week one; the books are on the shelf and the dial is on the dash from the walk-round; and Ron, asked what he wants before the Fifth, does not go. No game over; Tom cannot die; a car hurts and never kills; nobody is killed by the story, and no gun is needed by it. No drink, no gambling, no children, anywhere.

The budget, kept as it was written.
