# The story bar

20 November 2026. Written once, in round 1 of the story loop, for every reader of every version of the story bible. It says what players and critics praise and punish in the stories of the three reference games, and what "exceptional" means here.

Where it comes from. The figures are Kingdom Come: Deliverance II's, from the project's review study (production/research/aaa-reference/PLAYERS.md: 5,701 English Steam reviews and 12 critics, read 12 October 2026; the sample holds about as many negative reviews as positive, so a share ranks themes and is not a share of all players). The study's data for Grand Theft Auto V and Red Dead Redemption 2 had not landed when this was written; what is said of those two is the broad public record of their reviews, marked "public record", unmeasured. Mafia is not a reference. Other games are known only from the outside.

## What players and critics praise

1. A story and characters worth caring about. The most praised thing in KCD2: 32.2% of sampled reviews, 8 of 12 critics. Players name the writing, the long side stories and one friendship, Henry and Hans. Public record: RDR2 is remembered for Arthur Morgan, a lead who reckons with what he has been and changes; GTA V for three leads who each want something and for scenes that put them in one room.
2. Side quests that are whole stories, not errands. 13.7%, 9 of 12 critics. They have their own turns, bring people back and touch the main story.
3. Choices that change what happens. 7.1%, 8 of 12 critics. Quests bend to what the player did, stole or said; the town reacts. Critics rate this highest of all.
4. Humour and a mix of tones. 4.7%. It comes from set pieces, not from lines alone, set against a serious main story.
5. An ending that lands. Rarely named when it works; KCD2's draws almost no complaint (1.7%). Public record: RDR2's last chapters are the most praised part of its story because the ending is the sum of the man.
6. Period and place. 10.0%. A world specific enough to be believed.

## What they punish

1. A story that is predictable, clichéd, rushed or thin. 8.3% in KCD2, and the commonest story complaint there is: a main line that slows after its first stretch, a plot the player has seen before, an ending guessed in the first hour. A familiar plot sinks a short game faster than weak mechanics.
2. A slow opening, long scenes, too much talk. 10.7%.
3. Boredom: nothing to do, or nothing that matters, for minutes. 10.9%.
4. The same loop over and over. 6.8%. The reviewers who say it had played a median of 42 hours: it is a complaint about the middle.
5. An ending that is forced, abrupt, or sad for no reason; one the player could not see coming from his own play.
6. Choices that change nothing; a town that knows what it could not know.
7. Public record, GTA V: antagonists thinner than the leads, and a last act that hurries. RDR2: a slow middle stretch and missions on rails that ignore how the player wants to play.

## What "exceptional" means

- A reviewer would single the story out as a reason to play the game.
- People talk about the characters afterwards: at least one relationship as remembered as Henry and Hans, and a lead who is somebody, even unvoiced and with his past unsaid.
- No stretch that is predictable, clichéd, rushed or thin. That includes the middle, which is most of the game, and the road of the player who refuses the crime.
- A mystery with a truth, uncovered by play, that changes what earlier scenes meant and pays off at the climax.
- Antagonists with motives, scenes and different ways of coming at him.
- Every ending earned by the player's own play, seen coming, with a last scene and a last image.
- Side stories that are stories.
- A real mix of tones, with things in it that are actually funny.
- Story and play feeding each other: every beat is something the game's systems can carry, talk is where consequences come back, nothing is on rails, there is no game over.

## The frame the story must keep

These are the owner's rules. A story that breaks one is not better for it; mark it down.

- Britain, a port town, October to December 1990. No alcohol or gambling, shown or spoken of. No children anywhere, in any line. Tobacco is allowed. Full period swearing is allowed; no slurs. Religion is part of life, never mocked, never a mechanic. Police are corruptible as individuals, never as a thesis.
- LEDGER is not a story-first game. The player mostly acts with his hands: carries, drives, break-ins, fights, working the street. Talk is where consequences surface. Few short scenes; no long cutscenes.
- Tom Nowak's life before the town is unsaid: nobody asks and he never tells. He is not voiced. He cannot die. A car hurts and never kills. Guns are rare and late. There is no game over and no story on rails.
- Three things the first readers said to keep: Sheila Dunn as written, with her withholding of his name; the first night and Ada's window; the fare book as alibi and noose, paid off by a VAT inspection.

## How to score

Score the bible as a story a player would live through in fifteen to twenty hours, at the bar of the best game stories you know. Be hard. A 9 or 10 means you would single it out in a review as a reason to play; a 7 means good and unremarkable; a 5 means it would be punished as thin or familiar.

Give one overall score from 1 to 10 and one for each of these twelve:

1. Tom (want, need, line, fear, change)
2. The cast
3. The antagonists
4. The central mystery
5. The opening and its retold scene
6. Structure and pace
7. The middle
8. Surprise against cliché
9. The endings
10. The side stories
11. Tone and humour
12. Story and play feeding each other

Then name the five weakest points, most important first, each with a fix that stays inside the frame. Then say plainly, yes or no, whether you would single this story out in a review, and why.
