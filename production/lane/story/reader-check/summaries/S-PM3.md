## What it is about

In October 1990 a stranger comes off the ferry into a northern port town, in an invented republic, whose docks have closed, with one suitcase, twenty-three korun fifty and a letter from an uncle he never met. The dead uncle has left him a two-car taxi office, an old docker on the door who cannot drive, a bookkeeper who walks home every night sooner than be driven, and a rule: every fare goes in the book. Beside the cabs runs an old dockers' sick club the official books do not show, paying forty-one families four korun a week by hand. Closing in are a widow who has made herself the port's lawful labour contractor, a young rival with cheap yellow cars, and a landlord who wants the yard. Something happened on the warehouse row one night last November that nobody will speak of, and one of the firm's cars was out. The story asks what people in a written-off place owe each other, how much goes unpaid because nobody will ask, and what a man will put his name to.

## The lead

He is thirty-two, son of a Hungarian refugee and the uncle's sister; his past is never told, because nobody asks. He is not voiced and speaks only in words the player types or picks. He lives in his notebook, its first thirty pages cut out, in short, dry entries: "Envelope. Landing. Nobody said thank you. Nobody was meant to." He wants a place of his own and his own name said by people who mean it.

His flaw is that he asks nobody for anything and takes nothing, and it is played with the hands. People offer him a coffee, a flask, a lift, a fifty-korun note, and unless the player holds a button to take, his hand goes to his pocket and he pays. A page headed "Owed and owing" keeps the account, and from the second week a page headed "Asked" lets the player put one question, what do you want, to anyone, once each. His suitcase stays packed under the bed until the player unpacks it.

## The people who matter

The doorman, fifty-eight, calls the lead "boss". From the first Thursday he rides in the passenger seat, naming each building by what it was. On Sunday mornings the lead teaches him to drive car two, whose seat is stuck so far forward his knees reach his ears; his terms are "I go, you stop," and the player has the handbrake.

The bookkeeper, fifty-three, calls him "New management" and has never been heard to say a thing that was not so. At the nightly count she makes him write one line in the fare book and rules through anything untrue. Offered a lift, she says "I've legs." Once, with the firm a car short, she says "I don't drive."

The widow who runs the dock gate picks twenty of sixty men each morning. She breaks no law, puts each offer once, keeps every deal, answers a straight question truly and never speaks to the police; her method is to ask his people what they want and offer it. The rival does one thing in person each week that the lead must meet in person: "I'm just what happens when nobody's on a door." The landlord needs the yard before his loan falls due at the year's end.

Round them: a talkative docker of the doorman's old gang; a frail man in a chair at a window, in grey cotton gloves; the old woman at her window, whose late husband founded the club; the uncle's daughter, a night nurse; a detective sergeant who still wants to know where an hour went on the night of last year's warehouse fire; a gossip who sells what he hears; and, late on, the inspector, a mild Tariff Board officer with a flask, a bad knee and a propelling pencil.

## The opening and the scene players retell

The first week, about two hours, is the most scripted. The bookkeeper shows him car two, the uncle's own, whose seat sticks because he was a small man.

At about minute nine comes the carry players retell. The doorman gives him an envelope for the tallyman at the landing, and he picks a route, the lit quay, the dark yard and lane, or the cab with the firm's name on its roof, past a constable who can turn round. If the constable takes it, twenty people over the water go short, and the bookkeeper writes a sixth name in the debt book: his, eighty korun.

On Sunday the first lesson ends at, or a pace short of, a bollard already striped with the firm's paint, and the bookkeeper asks which it will be: wind the club down, take it over, or refuse to say. Winding down makes it a registered mutual aid society; it is one story either way.

## The middle

The second act is four weeks, about seven and a half hours, more than half the main story. Before Ember Night each week repeats: day fares with the doorman, a dusk job set off by an opponent, the nightly count, Friday wages and the round. The widow asks the lead what he wants; the day driver, caught carrying for the gate, can be sacked or asked. At the licence hearing twenty old dockers in funeral suits fill the bench, fetched by the doorman unasked; the lead is licensed or refused.

Signs gather round the doorman: a coat that came home without sleeves last November, a gear change he was never taught, a refusal to drive down the warehouse row, nights sitting in car two in the dark. In the fish queue the talkative docker says he could tell who had that cab on the row. On Ember Night the doorman stands at the gate in a foreman's coat while an effigy wearing the firm's roof sign burns on the bonfire. His going is fixed for every player; whether the player granted his one request decides whether he nods across the fire. The bookkeeper takes one lift that night, then writes herself into the book.

After it the lead drives nights alone. A tally and a burnt glove turn up in car two's seat runner. Last year's book holds "Night. Car two. Out and back. Eleven kilometres." Driven, the route is seven; out to the painted bollard and back makes eleven. The player may write two initials after that old line, or leave it. The comic job: the talkative docker and the gossip, in borrowed coats, pose as hard men outside the rival's lock-up, one forbidden to speak, the other to sell; the talkative docker shakes his head at the ferry clerk until his cap falls off, corrects a man in the fish queue, "Three," and the gossip sells a radio. Driving home, the talkative docker lets slip that the doorman took the coat to keep a name unsaid.

Two side stories: the uncle's watch is in a pawnshop, pawned by the doorman for funeral flowers rather than ask; the lead redeems it silently or leaves it. The sergeant's mother knows what her four korun is and tells her daughter it is the church's; the lead keeps her secret, tells the sergeant, or lets her know he knows, which shapes the sergeant's last note. A third grown story and about thirty short ones make some two hours of optional story.

## The turn

Sunday 18 November, a year to the day. At dawn the doorman, driving, stops at the mouth of the row: he went down it on foot and came up with a man on his back, and the car had gone; then it came back. That night the radio operator calls: the man in the chair cannot breathe. Car two is gone from the yard. The lead follows it for two kilometres: the bookkeeper is driving, her head below the top of the seat. The uncle's daughter, at the hospital: "Dad's car. I took Dad in off that kerb, a year tonight. I never knew who'd had this one." In the corridor, the gravest scene, the doorman tells the floor he has been learning to drive "so it'd not be you"; she answers there was a next time, tonight, and he was at the gate. At two in the morning she signs the night in the fare book, the hand matching last year's. Her driving that night is fixed for every player.

What was true: to save the club the uncle planned to rob the bonded store, the doorman and the man in the chair inside, the talkative docker on the corner, himself driving. His heart failed at his desk, so she drove. The watchman's heater started the fire; nothing was taken. She fled, turned at the bollard, came back for the doorman carrying his friend, and took them to a ship's surgeon, not the hospital.

## The last act

Two weeks, about four and a half hours. Tipped off by the landlord, the Tariff Board sends the inspector to read the books. On Thursday the man in the chair dies on the daughter's ward; nothing prevents it, and this is fixed. On Friday the club's tin holds eleven korun, and the doorman says where money is: the rival's lock-up. The player plans the job on the office board, or declines and goes on colder.

On Saturday the lead waits at car two's wheel. The rival is ready with four lads and pick-handles; the talkative docker bursts out shouting go, and yellow cars close from both ends. Nothing takes the wheel. He can flee, using the handbrake to turn at the bollard, sooner, or never; or go back, fight at the door and carry the doorman on his back down the row, three ribs and a wrist broken. If he never turns, the doorman is arrested at dawn, bailed, and from then on rides in the back and speaks to him only of work. On Sunday he writes the kilometres off the dial and she reads them: "Four. I did eleven"; or "You never left him. I wondered if anybody could"; or, to a line with no way back, nothing.

The inspector comes, a fixed event. On his ride-along, the act's comic scene, a crab escapes a fishwife's box and he addresses it as "sir". At the funeral the doorman, arm in a sling, asks his first favour, that the lead take his corner of the coffin. The inspector reads both nights aloud, any initials the lead wrote as an altered record: by Friday each driver must sign in his own hand and show the kilometres, or it goes to the investigators, and either way to the High Tribunal. The doorman says he will tell the sergeant it was him, both nights. In her kitchen, another grave scene, the bookkeeper wants to write her own name, "and not on my own. I'll write my name when you've written yours." Three courses: both names; the doorman's alone, by doing nothing; or the book in the fire.

On Thursday the lead walks a dock wall of sixty men, asking for their word; those on his "Owed and owing" page stand. A widow dealt with straight lets them go at a contract's cost and offers the rival's father work; otherwise she prices it: the firm. On Friday the men lift the landlord's padlocked yard gate off its hinges, or the lead signs an option. Each night is driven for the inspector by its signer. With the bookkeeper at the wheel the handbrake is under the player's hand: pulled, she says "Thank you, I can manage"; left, she stops a pace short herself, and the inspector writes that nothing left the bond and each driver went back. If only the doorman signed, he drives both nights one-handed with learner plates, and the inspector pencils his doubt. Where both names stand the doorman walks up the hill to confess anyway, and the lead races there. In time, the doorman reads the paper: "She's wrote it herself. And you. You soft fool. Six weeks." Late, the sergeant makes him unsay it and tears it up. She charges everyone the paper names, the lead included: bail, and a date at the spring sittings.

## The endings

One thing decides which of three he gets: whether he signed for his own night.

If both signed, the bookkeeper will not keep a book she is in, and the radio operator keeps it. Last scene: six in the morning, car one on the dock road, the doorman in the passenger seat naming buildings, the foreman's coat returned, the bookkeeper in the back as a paying fare by her own asking. The doorman's last line follows the dial. The notebook: "Three of us at the spring sittings. He takes his test in March. I have unpacked."

If only the doorman's name stands, he answers in spring for nights he never drove and keeps the widow's coat; the bookkeeper's keys lie on the fare book, and she walks to a job in the hospital's wages office, refusing the cab. Last scene: the gate at six from across the road, the seat beside the lead empty; the doorman lifts one hand, not high. The notebook: "He said it was him. I let him. The suitcase is still under the bed."

If the book burned, investigators ask everyone, the bookkeeper hands her keys to a young constable, and the doorman goes up the street in the back of a plain car. Last scene: the quay at night, the suitcase, the last ferry; a constable calls his surname, and the player can stand or run. The fare book stays closed; the notebook's last page is cut out.
