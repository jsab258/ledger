## What it is about

In 1950, a few years after a long overseas war against an island empire, the city of Porto Saleira sprawls along a hot, dry southern coast. It is a port, a film town and a building boom at once, swollen with returned soldiers, new money and a police force that takes from both. The lead is a former infantry lieutenant who came home with a gallantry medal and joined the city police as a patrolman. He is upright, ambitious and quick, and he believes the medal says something true about him. The story follows him up the force, one detective desk at a time, from traffic to murder to narcotics to fire, solving cases one by one while a larger scheme grows behind them. It asks whether an honest man can rise in a crooked institution, and what he did in the war that he has not told anyone.

## The lead

The lead is a married man with a family in a neat suburban house, a churchgoer who does not take money and keeps apart from the squad off duty. Colleagues resent him for it and for his speed of promotion. The press likes him. He is clever at a crime scene and stiff with people. The player sees the war in short flashbacks that open each new desk: the lead as a young officer who wants to do everything by the book, who clears caves slowly and carefully while his men grumble, and who is at odds with a harder, older sergeant in his company. The medal hangs over every scene in which someone calls him a hero; he never accepts the word.

## The people who matter

His patrol partner is a weary beat officer who shows him the city. His traffic partner is a cheerful, talkative detective. On murder his partner is an old, heavy-smoking homicide man who wants cases closed fast and dislikes the lead's righteousness. On narcotics his partner is a smooth, well-dressed sergeant with friends in high places. On fire his partner is a gruff veteran close to retirement who wants no trouble. Above them sits the homicide captain, a hard man who answers to the city's powers.

Outside the force are a property developer who runs a housing fund for returned soldiers; a society psychiatrist who treats veterans and the rich; a refugee nightclub singer with a past, who is one of the psychiatrist's patients; a city gang boss who controls narcotics; and three men from the lead's old company. One is the sergeant who disliked him, now an insurance investigator. One is the company's medic, now a struggling civilian. One is the company's flame-thrower operator, a quiet man who has not come home whole.

## The opening and the scene players retell

The story opens on patrol. The lead and his partner answer a shooting call at a corner shop, and the player walks the scene for the first time: moving the lead round the room, picking up objects, turning them in the hands to find marks, writing what matters into a notebook. A second call becomes a robbery, a car chase with the partner driving and the lead shooting from the window, and a gunfight in the street. Off a third minor call, the lead traces a gun to an older murder, and his work wins him a detective's badge. Each desk then begins with a war flashback before the cases resume.

The scene players retell is the first proper interview. The lead sits across from a witness while the player watches the face for small tells: a glance away, a tightened mouth, a swallow. After each answer the player picks one of three replies: to accept it, to press with suspicion, or to accuse the speaker of lying, which needs a piece of evidence from the notebook. A wrong accusation closes that line for good. A right one draws out the truth. The lead, calm and polite one moment, can be pushed by the player into a sudden shouted accusation the next, and the swing between the two is what players remember.

## The middle

The long middle is the traffic, homicide and narcotics desks, roughly two thirds of the whole. Every case runs on the same pattern: a call, a crime scene to search, witnesses and suspects to interview, places to visit across the city in the car (the player can drive or hand the wheel to the partner), and almost always a chase on foot or by car, a fist fight or a gunfight. Each case ends with a charge and a rating out of five at the station. The player can miss clues and fluff interviews and still finish; a worse case means a weaker charge and a lower rating, not a different story.

On traffic the cases are crashes and car crimes that turn out to be more. A man found dead beside his car has been killed over a disputed debt. A wedding ends in a fatal crash that was arranged. A foreign consul's stolen car is carrying narcotics. A car-theft ring hides a kidnapping. A young actress is found drugged in a crashed car, and the trail leads to a film producer who has been preying on hopefuls and to a powerful man who protects him.

Homicide is the heart of the middle. Women are found dead and posed in public places across the city, each body mutilated, each left with a token of the killer's: a personal item taken from the victim and arranged nearby. In each case the evidence points to a man close to the victim, usually a husband with a temper or a lover with a record, and the player chooses which of two suspects to charge. The homicide partner and the captain press for a quick charge each time; the lead charges, uneasy, and the press cheers. A secretary at a film studio is killed in the same stretch, a separate case that ends with a studio insider. Then the last murder breaks the pattern: notes in the killer's hand lead the lead across the city to old landmarks, each with a riddle drawn from a book of old verse, ending at a church. The killer is a drifter who works night jobs, and the lead chases him through the crypt and into a cemetery and kills him in a gunfight. The captain then tells him the killer was the half-brother of a powerful politician. His name will never be published, the charged men will be let out quietly through mistrials, and the lead will keep his mouth shut. He does. This is the gravest scene of the middle: the player has charged innocent men with his own hands and must watch them be released in silence.

Narcotics begins with army surplus morphine flooding the city and a shootout with the gang boss's men. A drugs supply line ends in a raid and a running gunfight. A young model's death, staged as a suicide, leads to the psychiatrist's patients. Then the morphine is traced to a ship in the harbour: men from the lead's old company robbed it, and the gang boss is having them killed one by one. The medic is among the thieves and is hiding. Through these weeks the lead meets the singer at the club where she works, protects her from a man pressuring her, and begins an affair with her.

Beside the cases there is a great deal of optional material. Over the radio come forty or so street crimes the player can answer between cases: a bank robbery that ends in a street battle, a gunman holding a hostage that the player must end with one clean shot, a man fleeing over rooftops. They have no plot thread and no choice beyond whether to take them. The second optional strand is a run of old newspapers hidden in the cases. Each one found plays a short scene the lead never sees: the developer and the psychiatrist meeting, the psychiatrist treating the flame-thrower operator, the medic in torment, the fund's plan being drawn up. The player chooses nothing in them; they reveal the conspiracy ahead of the lead. Beyond these lie hidden film reels, landmarks and cars to find.

Few scenes are played for laughs. The nearest are the traffic partner's patter during drives and the homicide partner's sour jabs at the lead's manners, such as needling him about church and his refusal to take a cigarette or a favour.

## The turn

The lead is close to the medic, who is ready to confess about the morphine. The narcotics sergeant, the lead's own partner, is on the developer's payroll and has been warned off. To bury a separate scandal on his own desk and to buy himself a place in the developer's fund, the sergeant tips off the press about the lead's affair. It breaks before the lead can get his confession. The newspapers drop him, his wife throws him out, the force strips him of his rank on narcotics and sends him to the fire desk, which the squad treats as exile. The station turns its back on him. The player sees him at the end of the desk in a small room, alone.

At the same time the flashbacks reach what he has hidden. In the war, his careful cave-clearing left his men behind, and they walked into an ambush. Later the older sergeant advised sealing a cave complex shut; the lead instead ordered the flame-thrower operator to burn it out. It was full of sheltering civilians. The lead then ordered his men to finish the burned survivors. The medic shot him in the back in protest, and the sergeant took command, ordered the men out and told them never to speak of it. The medal came from another day: after his battalion was killed, he argued to fall back, a friend beside him died in their foxhole, and he was found unhurt beside the remains, the only survivor, and decorated for it. This is the gravest part of the story.

## The last act

The fire desk's cases are houses burning in the city's new estates, and in one of them a family dies in its beds. The arson partner wants them called accidents. The lead does not. Disgraced, he cannot investigate openly, so he asks the old sergeant, now an insurance investigator, to look into the developer's fund.

From here the player often controls the insurance investigator instead of the lead, switching between them. As the investigator, the player searches offices and records, finds the fund's houses built with substandard materials and an insurer knowingly covering them, and fights his way through a gunfight at the developer's mansion to confront the developer, who tries to buy him and threatens him. As the lead, the player follows the fires and a factory explosion, set off on purpose by a refugee scientist with a wartime secret, that kills many workers. The investigator learns that the fund has been burning the houses of people who would not sell, through the psychiatrist's patient, who believes he is cleansing the land. The lead learns the real aim: buy land cheaply through the fund and sell it to the government at high prices for a planned motorway. The investigator finds the medic's body. Guilt had made him give the stolen morphine to the psychiatrist to help veterans, and the psychiatrist killed him once he became a risk. The narcotics sergeant, at the scene, speaks ill of the dead medic, and the lead defends him.

The investigator works out that the patient is the flame-thrower operator. The operator goes to the psychiatrist's clinic, kills him and takes the singer. The lead's case ratings and charges do not change what follows. The two old officers, together again, follow the operator into the city's storm channels during a downpour. Underground, with the water rising, they find the singer. The operator, broken, speaks to the lead of the cave they share. The investigator shoots him as a mercy. The flood comes. The lead gets the singer and the investigator to a grate and up into the open, then is swept away by the current and drowns.

## The endings

There is one ending. At the lead's funeral, beside a closed casket, his former colleagues stand with his estranged wife and family and the singer. The narcotics sergeant who exposed him gives the eulogy, praising his war record and his police work, calling the charges against him false, and crediting him with exposing the psychiatrist and the developer. The singer leaves in disgust, brushing off the investigator. The arson partner remarks to the investigator that he and the lead were never friends; the investigator agrees, and says he was never the lead's enemy either. The arson partner says he thinks the lead knew it. A closing flashback shows the old sergeant being told by men of the old company of their plan to steal the morphine, and refusing to have any part in it. The story ends there; no choice the player made changes this scene.
