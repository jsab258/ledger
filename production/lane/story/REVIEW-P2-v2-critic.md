# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 2: a critic's review

I expected a well-meant northern crime story with a cab in it. I found a friendship I would remember, built around a driving lesson and a handbrake, with the rest still scaffolding. At its best it matches Kingdom Come: Deliverance II. Elsewhere it is tidy, over-planned and too fond of explanation. The frame is kept: I found no drink, no gambling, no children, no mocked religion and no corrupt police as a thesis. The one place it gets close is Ellis's line about Tom's past, and it stays on the right side because she says outright that she will not ask.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7.

1. Tom: 7. The want (his own name said by people who mean it), the flaw (he never asks and never takes) and the fear (the packed suitcase) are clear and can be played, and the Straight Life line "You've just done it again" makes the flaw the verdict. But the take-or-pay press comes up so often that it risks turning into a virtue meter, and the suitcase is a neater symbol than a man.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Pauline, Terry and the Widow are people I would talk about afterwards. With twenty-one names who matter, though, Julie, Darren and Kevin end up as functions waiting for an offer.
3. The antagonists: 7. Maureen Jensen is the best thing here after Ron: she prices, never lies, and her four ways of coming at Tom are all different. Danny earns his scene at the gate. Agar is a man who writes letters, and his big move in the last act is changing a lock over £38 of rent.
4. The central mystery: 7. "He came back" and "Who is there he'd mind knowing?" are two real turns, and they change what the lessons meant. But the first turn is suspected on purpose, the decoy (Kevin) is cleared in the same scene that raises him, and the last two turns are told to Tom rather than found by him.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The ferry, the six lines from Sheila, the priest's forty-one empty envelopes and the carry at minute nine make a lean, confident first half hour. Sheila writing "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book is a lovely sting.
6. Structure and pace: 6. Act II runs nine and a half hours on eighteen scenes. The third movement puts the Bishop's telling, the Widow's answer and Ron's confession nearly back to back, and Act III's Friday is a five-stop errand run against the clock.
7. The middle: 7. The table of weeks (the fare who comes back, the dusk job, what Sheila finds) is a real answer to the slow middle that RDR2 was punished for. Pauline's Saturday and the two strangers are the right set pieces in the right place. Three of the six dusk jobs come from Danny, though, so he ends up as the weather.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. It deliberately avoids the hidden boss, the guilty uncle and the crooked landlord, and the player who supplies his own blackmail story is wrong twice. That is clever and fair. It is still a story about the old crime coming back on its anniversary, which players have seen before.
9. The endings: 8. Every ending is a seat, a line in the fare book and a line in the Ledger, and "Ron drove" and "Got it, boss" would land. The Suitcase is cruel in the right way. The Kingdom closes too tidily, with everyone's holdings read out like the minutes of a meeting.
10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and her daughter, each protecting the other from a thing both of them know, is the best writing in the file. Alison's offer from her editor turns the investigator into the one being bought. The watch repeats a motif the main line already owns, and Karen's wardrobe is a sketch.
11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline is genuinely funny, and so are Terry's fifth word and Shiner under orders not to speak. The ham, the crabs and the radios drift towards a whimsical local colour that will wear thin over sixteen hours unless the lines are sharper than these samples.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 7. The handbrake is the best idea in the document: an input that means something for the whole game, and a last press the player can decline. Against it, the truth reaches the player through speeches, and the climax is a pen at a counter.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The truth is told, not found. Where: section 7, scenes 12, 14 and 15, and section 8, Sheila's kitchen. After the count in scene 9, which is excellent work done with the hands, every remaining piece of the truth comes from someone sitting down and saying it: the Bishop, the Widow (who even adds a closing speech about what she has to carry), Ron at the wheel, then Sheila. Why a player would feel it: the bar asks for a truth uncovered by play, and this is four confessions in under three hours, just when the game should be handing him the controls. Fix: make the eleven miles a drive the player rebuilds himself. He has the odometer, the old paint on the bollard, the wing done twice and the sister on Ropery Lane, so let him drive the row, the bollard, the row again and the lane, and land on eleven before anyone says "came back". Then Ron's scene confirms what the player already worked out. Cut the Widow's last speech after "Who is there he'd mind knowing?": the line is enough.

2. The curious player gets the weaker game. Where: section 7, "If the player asked first", and the second movement. The bible rewards the player who asks Ron what he wants before the Fifth, then gives that branch a paragraph, while scenes 8, 11 and 13 and the morning gift are written for a Ron who has gone to the gate. Why a player would feel it: the player who did what the theme asks for gets a silent passenger and scenes written for someone else. The bar names this exactly: a choice that changes nothing. Fix: write the full-seat movement as properly as the empty one. The Widow books car one twice with Ron in it, and the silence between the two of them becomes the pressure. Scene 13 is played with Ron in the back next to the two widows. Tapper becomes the gate's foreman and the one opening the car door.

3. Agar, and the obstacles in the last act. Where: section 5 (Agar), section 8 (Friday). Agar's moves are letters, and his part in the climax is a lock at noon over one week's rent. Friday is five doors, each with a gatekeeper: Danny's cars across the lane, the Widow's permission, the agents, the solicitor, the constable. Why a player would feel it: it reads as a checklist with placed obstacles, the "missions on rails" complaint in a different coat, and it shrinks the man scene 18 builds. Fix: give Friday three doors, not five, each one costing something real. Tie Agar to the night itself: his agents held the row's watch contract, so the watchman's missing hour is partly his embarrassment and the call to Customs is self-protection as well as greed. That way his lock is about the thirty-first and the basin, not £38.

4. The road where Tom refuses the crime is a sketch. Where: section 11. It is six bullets: three jobs of a sentence each, and a Widow who is "the most reasonable person in the Hook" with no scene to show it. Why a player would feel it: the bar demands that this road not be thin, and a player who turns Sheila down on the first Sunday will feel he picked the cut-down version. Fix: give it its own midpoint. The night of the society's first meeting, the hall held against the agents, works as its Bonfire Night. Write one scene where the Widow, liking him, offers the thing he most wants, which is his name said by people who mean it, and he has to say out loud why a club should outlive its docks.

5. Reese's copy to the police carries too much weight. Where: section 8, Thursday. The stakes of the last act depend on a Customs officer choosing to forward a VAT statement because "the police have asked the public". Why a player would feel it: the whole moral weight of the signature rests on a link that sounds invented at the moment it is needed. Fix: set it up in scene 16. Ellis, with the tally in her envelope and new paint on car two's wing, writes to Customs and Excise asking to see the firm's 1989 records. Reese mentions her letter on the Monday. The copy then becomes something the player watched being set in motion.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review?

No, not yet. I would single out Ron Kirby, the three lessons and the last press of the handbrake by name: that thread is as strong as Henry and Hans, and its pay-off is the sum of the man, the way Arthur Morgan's is. But a review recommends the whole fifteen hours, and too many of them still show their plan: confessions where there should be discoveries, a branch for the curious player that has hardly been written, an antagonist who only writes letters, and an Act III that runs on errands. Fix those and the answer could become yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron and the handbrake. "I go, you stop. That's the arrangement", the bollard with the firm's paint already on it, "Not down there, boss", the third lesson down the row a year to the day, and a last input the player can leave alone. That is story and play in the same gesture, and I have seldom seen it done.
2. The Widow's rule. She never lies when asked straight and puts every offer once, and "Who is there he'd mind knowing?" turns the blackmail story the player built for himself into something smaller and much sadder. Do not make her crueller or give her a secret.
3. The fare book. "Every fare goes in the book", the two lines of 18 November 1989, one an alibi and the other a noose, and Sheila turning to a page he has never read: "15.10. Ferry landing. One. No charge." That line, written again at the end of the Suitcase ending, is the best image in the document.
