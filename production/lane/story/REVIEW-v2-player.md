# LEDGER story bible v2: a player's review

I read sections 1 to 15 in order, as the game would deal them to me, then section 0. I played it twice in my head: once taking the club over, once winding it down.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7

1. Tom: 7. The Ledger voice is the best idea in the bible ("There is no cat. She knows there is no cat"), and the flaw (he asks nobody for anything) has a real pay-off in the phone box. But the flaw lives in choices I control, so a player who lets Ron buy the chips in week one has quietly skipped Tom's arc.
2. The cast: 7. Ron, Sheila, Ada and Pauline would stay with me. Past them it is a phone book: by week three I would be muddling Edith, Christine, Brenda, Doreen, Dawn and Winifred, and June, Alison and Philip are each one want and one secret.
3. The antagonists: 8. Three rivals with three different ways in, and each has a scene that makes him a person: the Widow paying to the penny, Agar sitting behind the glass door hearing "the thirty-first of December", Danny standing over his father. Agar walking down Quay Street in the rain without a hat is the best antagonist moment here.
4. The central mystery: 6. The layers are well built and the Mickey layer does change what earlier scenes meant. But too much of it gets handed to me in the last week by people who walk up and confess, and a player who pokes around will have guessed by Act II that Mickey had more of a hand in it than he let on.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The priest's line about forty-one empty envelopes, the carry, and the failed carry ending with "Nowak, T. £82" in the debt book are all strong. Monday is busy, but every beat in it is something I do.
6. Structure and pace: 6. The calendar spine (the Fifth, Remembrance, the anniversary, the hearing, the letter) is smart and pulls a slow player forward. But it is a two-hour act, then eleven to fourteen hours, then a last week that has to hold six hours of payoff in two or three.
7. The middle: 5. Twenty good scenes spread over twelve hours, with "the game as designed" filling the gaps. The bible never says what I am chasing between scenes, and that is where the reviews in the bar say a game dies. On the straight road it is worse.
8. Surprise against cliché: 6. Ada as head of the club, made known by the tallyman standing in her front room, is a proper surprise. The rest is familiar ground: the kindly dead uncle with a dark secret, the developer with his marina and hotel, the honest outsider from the tax office who cracks it.
9. The endings: 8. "Nowak!" called from the wrong side of the name ladder, "Ron drove. He didn't hit anything. Told him so", and the Suitcase's "No charge" are endings I would describe to a friend. Ada's table in every ending is a fine way to close it.
10. The side stories: 7. The watch, Norman Dent and Karen's wardrobe are whole stories with turns. Philip's is a mood rather than a story, and the mooring is plot dressed up as a side story.
11. Tone and humour: 8. The radio as a chorus is a real find, the crabs in Reese's back seat are funny, and the comedy sits on people, not jokes. Pauline's hour is the set piece I would most look forward to.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 7. The lessons, the Friday round, the hearing built from the town's memory and the five calls are story done with hands and systems. But the reveals in the last week are mostly talk I sit through, and the best friendship in the game is gated behind a habit (riding with Ron) the player might never pick up.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The truth comes out as a line of confessions, not something I find. Where: section 8, Wednesday and Thursday. In two days Reese reads out the importer's account and the free Saturdays, Norman explains the lifts, the rent card turns up in the box, Agar knocks and tells his half, and Les Coultas tells the rest at the landing. Why a player feels it: the bar asks for a mystery uncovered by play, and this one is uncovered by a mild man with a pencil and three people who choose that week to talk. Section 15 says a keen player can find each piece earlier, but the bible never shows what happens when he does, so I assume the game is really built around Reese. Fix: make each piece something you have to do with your hands in Act II. You get the rent card by breaking into Iveson's yard, Norman's Saturdays by tailing him home from the row, and Les's account by following him to the last ferry in scene 19. Then have Reese read only what the player has left lying. If Tom already holds the truth, the inspection week is about whether he lets the books say it. If he does not, Reese finds it, and that should feel like losing control of your own story.

2. The last week carries too much and will feel rushed. Where: section 8 and section 9. Ada wants the book back, the Widow bids for the drivers, Agar's agents serve notice, Garbutt asks his favour, there are five inspection mornings, the two widows in the cab, Agar at the door, Ellis's trade, Les at the landing, the letter, the phone box, Ada's table and then the ending. All of that in two to three hours. Why a player feels it: this is the exact complaint the bar records about GTA V, a last act that hurries, and here the best scenes in the story are queued up on top of each other. Fix: move the two widows' cab ride to the end of Act II as the sit-down for any player who has dealt with Ada, so it lands before the inspection with room to breathe. Fold Garbutt's favour into scene 19. Keep the last week to Reese by day and one job a night, and make sure each night's job is something the player does, not something he is told.

3. The middle has scenes but no chase, and the straight road has nothing to build. Where: section 7, first half (S versions of scenes 3, 5 and 8), and the eleven-to-fourteen-hour span. On the keeper's road I at least climb rungs. On the straight road Act II is Darren carrying badly, a picket I can only push through, and a Friday when I am £20 short. June's lawful society, the one real project on that road, does not come up until scene 12 and is not named as a goal until scene 18. Why a player feels it: the bar counts the refusing player's road, and a player who said no on Sunday spends hours on a slow decline with nothing he is working towards. That is when people stop playing. Fix: let June say "It doesn't have to be shut, it has to be lawful" on the first Monday of Act II on the straight road, and make the society that road's ladder, with rungs you can see: Edith's forms, two trustees, the hall from Philip, a bank account, Ada's list. Each rival goes after a different rung, the way they go after the rackets on the other road. Then both roads have a climb, and the middle has a direction on each.

4. The big turn is a familiar one, and it is signposted. Where: the letter ("I have not always paid them the right way"), the August line read on the Fifth ("God forgive me, that's why"), scene 9 (Agar: Mickey owed him), scene 19 (a man at the counter wants what Mickey paid him twice), Garbutt's unease, and the box at Iveson's. Why a player feels it: I have played a lot of these, and "the good dead man was the real author of the crime" is the obvious next step once Agar says Mickey owed him money against the yard. By scene 19 a poking player has it, and Wednesday's reveal confirms a hunch instead of overturning anything. Fix: point the suspicion elsewhere on purpose. In Act II, give Agar a scene where he plainly seems to have ordered the fire (his surveyor measuring the burnt plot, his "understanding" with Mickey), and give Garbutt a moment that looks like guilt. Cut "God forgive me, that's why" or hold it back until Friday, so the Fifth reads Mickey as a man who broke under Ada's order. Then the truth points back at the man who sent for Tom, after the player has spent hours blaming someone else.

5. Too many people, and the "someone" is barely written. Where: section 4, the cast list, and the supper on the Fifth (section 7, midpoint 2). Alison or Philip is built from one scene (scene 4, the row or the chess) before coming up to the flat on the night the game depends on, and before the guest watches car two burn. Why a player feels it: the midpoint says "the guest's innocence" is lost, but I would not know the guest well enough to care. Meanwhile so many named townsfolk come through that the ones who matter get lost among them. Fix: give the someone three short hands-on beats before the Fifth (a fare, a job they help with, one true thing traded each way), so the supper is a payoff and not an introduction. Merge Philip's side story into the society thread so the hall carries his story and he is not a second, thinner option. Fold the minor speakers at the hearing and elsewhere into fewer, recurring faces (Grace, Brenda, Edith) so the town feels known, not listed. And let Ron ride along by default from the first Thursday, so no player misses lesson three because he never thought to give him a lift.

## 3. Would I single this story out

No. Not yet, though it is close in places. I would tell a friend about Ron learning to drive at eleven miles an hour, about the tallyman standing in Ada's front room while car two burns under her window, about "Go on, Tommy" from a man with a crushed hand, and about the constable shouting "Nowak!" in the bad ending. Those are scenes worth retelling, and the fare book as both alibi and noose is a clever spine. But I would also warn the friend that the middle is long and loose, that the straight road sags, and that the truth is mostly read out to you in the last week rather than found. A story I single out is one where the big turn lands on something I did. Here it lands on something Reese reads. Fix the last week and the middle, and this moves from a good crime game with a good friendship to one people would recommend for its story.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron and the passenger seat. The lessons (the bollard, eleven miles an hour, the third one that is not funny), number nine on the quay wall, the gate offer, and Ron driving something in every ending, with whether he looks across as the verdict. This is the Henry and Hans of the game.
2. The Fifth. Car two burning, Terry pulled out of the back seat, Ada lit by the fire and unafraid, the tallyman beside her chair, and "You'd better come in, Tom." It turns the first tea into an interview without a word of explanation, and it is the best scene in the bible.
3. The name ladder and the fare book. "Mickey's?" to "Nowak's?", "Nowak!" from the constable, "Go on, Tommy", "Night, Tom", and the plain book of fares that turns out to be the only one that never lied, all the way to "No charge" on the last page.
