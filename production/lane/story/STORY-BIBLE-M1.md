# LEDGER: the story bible, measured round 1 (M1)

The Passenger. 20 November 2026. Canon outranks this file; nothing here is built; canon.md and RULINGS.md are untouched. Spoken lines are samples of the voice.

What changed from phase 2's version 5, and why. The story was measured on the ruler (measure/MEASURE.md) beside KCD2, RDR2 and GTA V. It passed every item on the page, and a fourth reader, a games writer shown the whole bible, still said no: the turn landed on a woman the player hardly knew; asking Ron early removed the hardest moment; the false trail broke itself and the turn hung on one word; the stakes were an assessment and a bail date and the last input was to do nothing; the last act was errands; there were too many branches. This version changes the shape to answer each: Sheila is the second friendship; the turn is seen, not deduced; Ron goes to the gate on every path; the last week has a clock with a person on it; three endings by Ron's seat; four weeks in the middle, not six.

How to read it. Section 0 is for the owner and gives away every turn; read it last to meet the story as a player would. Sections 6 to 9 are the story as scenes, in the order played; section 10 is what was true all along.

## 0. Changes to canon or rulings, for his ruling

Each with its reason and the plain alternative. Items 1 to 9 stand from phase 2; 10 to 13 are new in this version.

1. The warehouse fire. The ruling of 30 September stands as the street's version (the owner burned his own warehouse and went south) and "the truth is never the street's". The truth here: on Saturday 18 November 1989 three old dockers went into the importer's warehouse, a bonded store, to lift cigarettes for the dockers' sick club; a paraffin heater went over; and the car that took them was one of Mickey's. Mickey was to drive and was on a hospital ward; the one who took the keys was Sheila Dunn. Nobody paid for a fire and nobody meant one. Because the stock was under bond, the night belongs to Customs and Excise, and the VAT inspector of the last act is an officer of the department that was robbed. Alternative: the fire as phase 1 had it.
2. The outfit. Canon: Mickey left "a half-dead criminal outfit". Here it is an old dockers' sick club that pays forty-one families £4 a week, fed for thirty years by what Ron's gang lifted at the dock gate, and since the scheme ended in July 1989 by a weekly payment from the warehouse row and by Mickey's own pocket. Nobody is its secret head.
3. Ada stays what canon says: a widow at her window, on the club's list.
4. Ron's old gang is minted: Tapper, Shiner and the Bishop. Ron was in the building that night, not at the wheel; the Bishop was burned, and Mickey paid his keep every Friday for the rest of his own life.
5. One opponent and two pressures: the Widow is the main opponent; Danny Cammack and Geoffrey Agar are smaller. Canon gives the three equal standing.
6. One love interest, and small: Alison Sedman. Philip Danby is a regular; Keith Garbutt only carries the Widow's offers.
7. Sheila's name for him has two steps ("Mr Nowak", then "Tom"). Tom's line (QUESTIONS 2) is adopted as recommended. His letter has words and his Ledger a voice.
8. Mickey's first heart turn was on the night of the fire. The ruling on his death stands.
9. Small things minted: the old fare books on the shelf; the hearing on Tuesday 30 October; the dockers' own fire at the gate; a clause in Agar's lease.
10. New: Sheila is the second friendship, and she is seen at the wheel. The frame says to keep Sheila as written, and her words, her walk-round and her withholding of his name are kept. What is new is three small scenes with her before the turn (she teaches him to write a fare line; she is offered a lift home every night and takes it once; she says "I don't drive") and the turn itself: on the anniversary of the fire she takes car two out to fetch a man who cannot breathe, and the player sees it. This is the change that matters most, and it is his to rule on: it gives Sheila one night in 1989 and one in 1990 that he has not approved. Alternative: version 5's turn, by one word from Ron, with Sheila at arm's length.
11. New: three endings and no more. The ruling allows two or three endings; the built design holds five states beneath three. Here each of the three has one close, and which one is decided by which seat Ron is in: beside Tom, at the Widow's gate, or gone. Alternative: the five states of phase 2.
12. New: what the law does is harder. Whoever is named answers at the Crown Court at Easter for a bonded store broken into and a man burned; nobody is let off by a kind officer. The rule about police stands (corruptible as individuals, never as a thesis): the sergeant and the inspector are both straight. Alternative: the magistrates' date of version 5.
13. New: the road of the player who winds the club down is the same story with different Friday rounds and dusk jobs, told in one section, not a second middle. Winding the business down still ends Mickey's arrangement (30 September). Alternative: version 5's separate six weeks.

Unchanged and kept: nobody asks about Tom's past and he never tells; he is not voiced; no alcohol or gambling shown or spoken of; no children anywhere; tobacco only; Mickey's people never go to the police about one of their own (1 October): in this story nobody does, and the one man who means to, means to accuse himself.

## 1. The spine in a paragraph

In October 1990 Tom Nowak, a stranger with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter from an uncle he never met, takes over a minicab office in a port whose docks have died. With it he gets an old docker who cannot drive and rides in his passenger seat, and a bookkeeper who will not use his name and walks home every night in any weather. For three weeks he works the Hook from one car with Ron beside him, counts the night's money with Sheila across a table, and on Sunday mornings teaches Ron to drive at eleven miles an hour. Then, on Bonfire Night, Ron is standing at the dock gate in a foreman's coat with another firm's name on the back. The middle of the game is the player finding out, with a steering wheel and a mileometer, what was done with one of Mickey's cars on the night of last year's warehouse fire, and being sure, and being wrong. On the anniversary he sees who drove, because she does it again in front of him. Then a mild man from Customs and Excise reads two lines in the fare book and asks to be shown eleven miles by Friday; and Ron, who has had three driving lessons, lets it be known that on Friday at four he will walk up the hill and tell the police it was him. The last week is a race to make the truth readable before a friend can lie for it. Which seat Ron is in when it is over is how Tom ends.

## 2. The theme

You never ask for owt. LEDGER is about what people owe each other in a place the official books have written off, and about how much of it goes unpaid because nobody will ask.

- The club's book is the old answer and the Widow's gate is the new one.
- Tom takes nothing and asks nothing. So does Ron. So does Sheila, who has walked home for thirty-one years sooner than be driven. Three people who never ask keep one office, and one night stands between them that none of them has spoken of.
- The story's one real crime was a theft that went wrong; its lasting damage was done by a year of good mornings.

The story never says which answer is right. It makes every answer cost a named person something the player can see.

## 3. Tom Nowak

Tom is 32, the son of a Polish post-war settler and Mickey's sister. He has never been to the Hook. His life before it is unsaid: nobody asks and he never tells. He is not voiced.

The letter. In his hand in the first shot and pasted inside the cover of his Ledger:

"Tom. You don't know me. I was your mother's brother. I have a cab office on Quay Street in Meridian, two cars, and a good woman who keeps the books. It is yours when I am gone, and the doctor says that is sooner than I would like. There is more to it than cabs. There is a book. People are owed. Come and look before you say yes. There is a man on the door called Kirby. Keep him if he'll stay. If you cannot stomach it, shut the door, and nobody will blame you, least of all me. Every fare goes in the book. M. Suddaby. P.S. Don't let them call you Mickey's nephew for ever."

His voice. Tom speaks aloud only in the words the player types or picks. Who he is lives in the Ledger, his notebook: a used one, hard-backed, with the first thirty pages cut out close to the spine. Every entry is short, dry and written from what the player did.

- After the first carry: "Envelope. Landing. Nobody said thank you. Nobody was meant to."
- After the hearing: "Twenty men in suits. I did not ask them. I should find out who did."
- After the Fifth: "Car one. On my own."
- After the lift: "She wrote it down. I would have let it go."
- After the 18th: "It was her. I followed her for a mile and a half and I still had the wrong man."

What he wants. A place that is his, and his own name said by people who mean it. The letter's postscript is the want.

His flaw. He lets nobody give him anything, and he asks nobody for anything: not a favour, not a reason, not what they want. He pays for tea that was on the house and carries alone what two could carry. It looks like pride and it is fear: a man who owes nothing and is owed nothing can pick up his suitcase.

The flaw is played, two ways.

- The offers. All through the game people offer Tom things for nothing, and each is one press: take it, or pay for it. Some are gifts (Grace's tea, Terry's flask, Ron's supper) and some have a hook in them (Danny's fifty-pound note, the Widow's lift) and nothing marks which is which. To take is to owe. A man who takes nothing is owed nothing and known by nobody: at the hearing on 30 October only people he has let do him a turn feel entitled to write for him, and on the last Friday only they will open a door. A man who takes from everybody is everybody's, and after the hearing teaches him to take, the hooks get better hidden: the gate's packing shift, a landlord's solicitor, a widow's offer to pay a debt for him.
- The question. From the morning the Widow asks him what he wants, his Ledger has a page headed "Asked". The player can put her question to anybody, once each, by opening the page in front of them; what they answer is written under their name, and some will not answer the first time. A question asked is an answer owed. It is a thing the player does, with a button, and the game never tells him when.
- The deed. He changes once, where everybody can see it, and it is not a line: on the last Friday there is a thing that cannot be done by one man, and he has to walk across a road and ask sixty people for their hands (section 8). Who gets up is the sum of the game.

The load the flaw cannot carry. Ron is the same man. Two men who never ask sit eighteen inches apart for three weeks, and one of them is carrying something the other could lift if either would open his mouth.

His line. He lays hands on no woman, nobody of sixty or over, and not the priest. The game never offers him the blow.

What he fears. The suitcase. It stays packed under the bed in Mickey's flat until the player unpacks it, one press. Sheila notices the day he does: "You've unpacked."

His past. Ada says it for the street on the first Tuesday: "I'll not ask where you were before. Nobody on this street will. We've all been somewhere."

## 4. The people, as the player meets them

Twenty-one who matter. Nobody on this list is secretly anybody else. What each is carrying is in section 10.

- Ron Kirby, 58. A docker for thirty years, on the door and the rank since the scheme ended. He calls Tom "boss". He cannot drive: "Never needed to. The work was at the end of the street." He wants to be of use the way he was when he had a hook in his belt and a gang.
- Sheila Dunn, 53, the bookkeeper, at Mickey's since she was 22. Exactly as written: "New management", the walk-round, the fare-book rule, the locked door, the Sunday question. She has never been heard to say a thing that was not so. She is sizing him up, as she promised Mickey. She has walked home every night for thirty-one years, forty minutes in any weather, and nobody at the firm has ever seen her in a car.
- Maureen Jensen, 50, the Widow. Broad-shouldered, dressed like a harbour master. Her husband ran the dock gate; she runs it better. Since the scheme ended she is the port's labour contractor, registered and lawful: sixty men stand at the gate at six and she picks twenty. She never threatens. She prices, she puts each offer once, and she has kept every deal she ever made.
- Tapper and Shiner, of Ron's old gang, on the Widow's gate now. Tapper says little. Shiner cannot stop.
- The Bishop, 60, of Ron's old gang, who is on nobody's gate. Ron's stories about him stop soonest.
- Pauline Kitching, 44, on the radio: the firm's running voice, heard in every cab at once. Nine years without a Saturday off.
- Terry Hodgson, 52, the night driver: four words a shift, always the right four. Pauline keeps a tally of her attempts to get a fifth out of him on air.
- Kevin Needham, days, twelve years in Mickey's car, who wants a plate of his own.

- Darren Milner, 25, who sells what he hears and wants to be somebody's man.
- Ada, 74, the widow at her window. Walter Harker's widow, on the club's list, lonely, sharp, the best witness on Quay Street. Her tea is tea.
- Also: Cyril Blenkin, the tallyman at the landing; Julie Kettlewell, laid off from the cold store, who can drive; Keith Garbutt, the Fixer, who carries the Widow's offers.
- June Suddaby, 34, Mickey's daughter, night sister at the infirmary; Father Brendan Walsh, who buried Mickey.

- Danny Cammack, 26. Hook Cars, two yellow cars, £1.50 a ride, pagers for his drivers. Loud on purpose, quicker than he lets on.
- Geoffrey Agar, 61, the landlord: silver, unhurried, apologises before every blow. His agents hold the freehold of the parade and half the warehouse row.
- DS Carol Ellis, 44, born two streets from the row. The fire was her file; her inspector closed it as undetermined; she has not stopped. All she has is an hour when nobody was on the row.
- Alison Sedman, 31, the paper's port reporter. One question: where did an hour go on the row last November?

- Mr T. A. Reese, 57, Officer of Customs and Excise, who comes at the end: a flask of tea, a bad knee, a propelling pencil. He is sorry, truly, when a book does not add up.

### Ron and Tom

The friendship is the plot, and it is written for the hands.

- The passenger seat. From the first Thursday Ron rides unless told not to. He names every building by what it was ("That was Stannard's ropewalk. That was the Seamen's Mission. That's still a wall"), and the player learns the map as a list of things that shut. Five times in the game he starts a story about Tapper, Shiner or the Bishop, and each stops one sentence later than the last. The fifth is finished.
- The lessons. On Sunday mornings on the empty dock road Tom teaches a fifty-eight-year-old docker to drive car two. Ron has the wheel, folded behind it with his knees at his ears, because car two's seat sticks; the player has his voice and the handbrake. Ron sets the terms himself, getting in: "That's yours, boss. I go, you stop. That's the arrangement." Lesson one: if the player is quick, Ron is stopped a yard short of a bollard; if not, the bollard. Lesson two: he drives the whole dock at eleven miles an hour, talking, and asks to go round again. Lesson three comes late and is not funny. He is learning for a reason, and he is really learning: what the player teaches him is used in the last act.
- Fridays. The Tangs', and the quay wall. Ron buys three of number nine and puts one inside his coat. "Feller I know." Ron pays, if Tom lets him.
- How it pays off. Which seat Ron is in when the last Friday is over is the ending: beside Tom, at the Widow's gate, or the back of a plain car.

### Sheila and Tom

The second friendship, and the one the turn lands on. It is made at a table, at night, with the hands.

- The count. Every night she reads the drivers' sheets aloud and he counts coin into bags, and she writes. It is two people's work and the one hour of the day they are alone. She does not chat. She says at each count whether the week's books would stand a stranger reading them.
- The pen. In the second week she turns the fare book round and makes him write a line himself, and from then on one line a night is his. She rules through what is not so. It is how the player learns what a line in that book is worth.
- The lift. He can offer her a lift home every night. "I've legs." She takes it once, in the rain on the night of the Fifth, in the back of car one and never car two, and the next night writes herself into the book for it.
- "I don't drive." Said once, on a Saturday when the firm is a car short and the keys are on the hook.
- The name. "New management"; then "Mr Nowak" if she trusts him; and "Tom" once, at two in the morning on the 18th, when she has nothing left to keep it with.
- How it pays off. On the last Friday he sits beside her with the handbrake, as he sat beside Ron, and the right thing to do with it is nothing.

## 5. The opponent, and two pressures

### Maureen Jensen, the Widow: his people, by asking

- Want: every job at the gate through her hands; Mickey's as the gate's cab firm; and the old club's book shut. "A pension for men who'll never work is a tax on men who do." Eleven on the list could still work, and will not sign with her while the four pounds comes.
- Why she is right: sixty men in work, a club that is dying anyway, and Ron a better foreman than doorman.
- Why she is dangerous: she breaks no law and no promise, and takes a firm apart by offering each of its people exactly what they want, once.
- How she comes, four ways. Offers to his people, carried by Keith Garbutt. The gate's custom: fifty pence on every fare through it, then the cold-store shift run, given or withheld. Her own body: when he will not pay, her men stand across the cold-store road at half past five and she stands in front of them, hands in her coat pockets, and his line means he can do nothing but wait, pay or turn round. And twice she books car one and sits in Ron's seat.
- Her rule, which the story never lets her break: she keeps every deal; she tells nothing unasked and no lie when asked straight; she holds nothing over anybody; and she has never spoken to a police officer about anyone, which every docker knows. The player can test it in week two: asked straight whether his day driver is carrying for her, she says, "Yes. You didn't ask before."

### Danny Cammack: his cover, in daylight

- Want: the Hook, and the end of the old lot. "You've got the keys. I've got the accent. One of us is in the wrong house."
- How he comes: not with a brick at first. He puts Hook Cars' number on the freephone in the ferry terminal. He rides in a Mickey's cab and pays a two-pound fare with a fifty: "Keep it. Call it rent." Taken, it is in the Tangs' queue by night that Mickey's pays Cammack rent, and it is read out at the hearing. He takes whatever the street leaves undefended, and says so: "I'm not doing owt to you, Nowak. I'm just what happens when nobody's on a door."
- He is not the fool of the piece. He is the first to work out who the two strangers in car coats were (scene 14) and that there is something about car two (the week of the letter), and he is the one opponent who hurts somebody. He has an opponent of his own: the gate has never picked a Cammack, and what he wants, when somebody finally asks, is a day's work there for his father.

### Geoffrey Agar: his clean money, by the calendar

- Want: the yard behind Mickey's, which carries the only right of way to the old basin. His loan on the basin falls due on the thirty-first of December.
- How he comes: by letters, each with a date in it. A rent review, "with regret". A referral of Tom's licence to the sub-committee. And he has held one thing back for a year: his watchman saw a cab on the row the night his building burned.
- He needs the Widow, and cannot afford her; the player drives him to the gate to hear her price (the week of the letter).
- His clock is real. If the yard is not his by the thirty-first, the bank has the basin; so he spends what he was saving, and his agents lock the yard on the one day it bites.

## 6. Act I: The Inheritance

Monday 15 to Sunday 21 October 1990. About two hours. The most scripted act. The beats of the approved first week stand; what this version adds is marked.

### Monday: the arrival and the first night

1. The ferry (0:00). The letter in his hand over grey water, the last line readable: "Every fare goes in the book." Nobody meets him. He has £23.50.
2. Quay Street (1:30). A line each, in passing: Darren, Ron, Ada at her window, June.
3. Sheila's walk-round (1:45). Her six lines as approved. "New management." The fare book: "Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule, and it's mine now." Added, on the forecourt: "Car two was Mickey's own. The seat sticks. He was a small man and had it where he wanted it. Kevin's given up." Kevin, six foot, drives it all game with his knees round the wheel, and says so on the radio.
4. The first fare (2:30). Father Walsh, from the infirmary to his house by the church, Pauline giving the way by landmarks. Sheila hands him Mickey's badge: "Wear it till the council sits." The priest, at his door: "You missed a good funeral. Forty-one envelopes on the plate, and every one of them empty. They wanted him to know they'd come."
5. The rest of the office (4:30). The locked door, the flat, the debt book: five names, £156. Friday is rent, £38, and five wage packets.
6. The first deed (5:30). Darren's £22. Somebody sees.
7. The first ask (7:00). Ron, the coat, the envelope. "Who's it for?" "Tallyman. Counted cargo on that quay forty year. That's all I'll say on a pavement, boss."
8. The carry (9:00). As written and as section 13 finishes it.
9. The landing (11:30). "Mickey's?" The thumb, the water. "Right. Wednesday, same again. And tell Kirby the round's twenty this week, not twenty-one." Ron counts £19 carrier's money into Tom's hand.

### Tuesday to Thursday

The approved beats, in order: Darren says the night to Tom's face; the first count; the other four debts; Ada's "What were you doing in my alley?" and her one invitation; Wednesday's second envelope against her tea.

Added or changed:

- The count. From Tuesday, every night: Sheila reads the drivers' sheets aloud, he counts the coin into bags, and she writes. Two people's work, done with the hands, and the one hour they are alone.
- Grace will not take money for his first tea. Take it or pay: the first offer the game counts. Terry's flask, on Tuesday night's rank, is the second.
- Ada on the first Tuesday, on her step: "I'll not ask where you were before. Nobody on this street will. We've all been somewhere."
- The tea. Ada tells him plainly what he has been carrying: the sick club her Walter started in 1953, forty-one names, four pounds, half over the water. "The tallyman keeps the far side. The gate used to fill it. I get mine like the rest." Then, of his doorman, as a recommendation: "He's a good man, Ron Kirby. He came home at two one morning last November with no sleeves to his coat, and I never asked. You'll find I don't."
- Alison in the cab on Wednesday, to the warehouse row. "It was a bonded store, did you know? The brigade were called at a quarter to twelve. The watchman swore he was on his rounds. Nobody was on that row for the best part of an hour. I'd like to know where an hour went." She leaves her umbrella on the back seat. Give it back, or keep it till she comes for it.
- "How did Mickey die?" Sheila: "At that desk, with the kettle on. His heart. He'd had one warning, a year ago, and went back to nights the week after."
- A letter from the council: he has been driving on a dead man's badge, and his own application will be heard on Tuesday 30 October.
- Thursday night, two dockers waiting by car two: the first time Ron rides. Afterwards, in the car: "You never ask for owt, do you. Mickey was the same. Dead at a desk with the kettle on."

### Friday: rent, wages and the round

- Rent to Mr Agar's agents; wages by hand. From this Friday Sheila says at every count whether the week's books would stand a stranger reading them.
- Night five, the third ask: a holdall. The Hook's half of the club goes round by hand on Fridays, twenty doors, and Ron walks it. "You'll want to see where it goes, boss. Or you won't. Mickey always walked it." A carry with faces: Winifred Duggleby; Albert Suggitt mending a net; Ada at her own door; a door on Tannery Row where Ron says "Knock soft. Her daughter's police"; and, last, across the Hook on Ropery Lane, one door where Ron goes up alone with a packet that did not come out of the holdall. "Not that one. That's Mickey's own."
- Then the Tangs', three of number nine, and the quay wall.

### Saturday and Sunday

- Saturday. Danny Cammack on Quay Street, buying chips, friendly.
- Sunday, early. The first lesson, in car two, off the road and off the clock. Ron has a provisional licence in his coat, taken out the week after the funeral ("There should be two can drive in a firm, boss. Time was there weren't"), and L-plates he has cut from a biscuit tin. "That's yours, boss. I go, you stop." The bollard, or a yard short of it. The bollard has a stripe of old paint on it already, the firm's colour.
- Sunday, ten o'clock. Sheila comes in on her day off. If she trusts him: "Mr Nowak." The first time. The real book is on the counter, shut. "That's the club's book and what Mickey ran beside it. You've had your week. So which is it going to be?" Wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say; asked twice, as built. The street learns the answer.

What the player believes at the end of week one: an old dockers' sick club pays forty-one families; something happened on the warehouse row last year that nobody will say; and he has a friend.

## 7. Act II: The Rise and the Squeeze

Monday 22 October to Sunday 18 November. About eight hours. Four weeks, not six: every week holds one thing that cannot be taken back.

Two movements with the Fifth between them: the Seat (about three and a half hours), in which there are two in the car by day; and the Empty Seat (about four), in which he drives alone and mostly at night. Fifteen scenes.

What a week is, and how its shape changes. Before the Fifth, days are fares with Ron in the front naming buildings, dusk is a job set off by whichever opponent is moving, night is the count with Sheila, and Friday is wages, rent and the round. After the Fifth the week is turned over: Kevin has the days, so Tom takes Terry's nights; the dusk job is replaced by something he goes looking for himself; the count is the place where what he has found is laid on the table; and on Friday he walks the round alone, with the third portion for a door he has now been through. The player feels the loss of the seat as a change in what his hands do all day, not as a line.

### First movement: the Seat

Scene 1. Tapper, and the freephone. Danny's number is on the freephone in the ferry terminal, and the boat's fares go to yellow cars. Ron brings Tapper, who works the ferry's ropes and knows when the ticket hall is empty. Getting Mickey's number back is a night's work with a screwdriver, a word with the clerk, or a price war. After: "Tapper could tap a tea chest so it sang the weight. One time him and the Bishop had a crate of..." Rain by four. He does not finish.

Scene 2. The book turned round. The first Monday's count. Every night since he came she has read the sheets aloud and he has counted coin into bags, and she has written. Tonight she turns the fare book round on the counter and holds out the pen. "You'll write one. Time, car, from, to, how many, what was paid. No adjectives." The player writes a real line from his own day, with the pen, in a box: the game takes what he puts. She reads it upside down. "Your sevens look like ones. Mickey's did." It is the only thing she says about it. From then on one line a night is his, and the book has two hands in it. If he ever writes a fare that did not happen, or leaves out one that did, she rules it through without a word and writes it right, and the Ledger notes that she did. This is how the player learns what a line in that book is worth, three weeks before he needs to know.

Scene 3. The gate at six. The Widow books a car herself and gets into the front. "Save you turning round." Ron has to get into the back, and does, without a word. Sixty men in the dark, twenty picked; Shiner first, talking. Her first offer, put once: fifty pence on every fare through the gate. Then, to the driver, the first person in the Hook to do it: "And what do you want, Mr Nowak? I'd guess nobody's asked." The player answers, or does not, and she remembers which; her last offer is priced on it. That morning the Ledger grows a page headed "Asked": from here the player can put her question to anyone, once each, and what they answer is written under their name. Getting out, not looking at Ron: "You're wasted on a door, Kirby. You always were." Ron moves back to the front before he speaks. "Shiner. Shiner could talk a customs man out of his own hat. He was talking the night we..." He stops. Choice: pay the fifty pence, or do not.

Scene 4. The long way round. Kevin's day fares come in short against his mileage; Sheila sees it in the book before anyone says it. Followed, he is carrying parcels for the gate in Mickey's car. Fronted on the rank, in front of the street: "Twelve years I've driven his car. She asked me what I wanted. Nobody here ever did." Choice: sack him, and the gate has a driver by Friday; or ask him now. Asked, he tells: a plate of his own. It can be met, and it costs: an owner-driver is less a man's own. Either way Kevin is on days and the player has learned what the Widow's question does to a firm.

Scene 5. The hearing, Tuesday 30 October. The council's licensing sub-committee, one room in the town hall. A member who owes Agar has referred the application over the dead man's badge. Agar himself is in the back row with his hat on his knee, and in the corridor after, whatever the result: "I do apologise. It is nothing personal. I should like your yard." Only people Tom has let do him a turn have written a reference. If he has paid his way with everybody, the clerk says "No references," and waits. Then the door at the back opens, and the public bench fills: twenty old dockers in funeral suits, caps on knees, Tapper and Shiner among them. Nobody asked them. Ron is standing by the door. "I can't write you one, boss. I can fetch them as'll sit." It is the biggest gift in the game, and he was never offered the press. Licensed with conditions, or refused, after which he owns the firm and may not drive a fare, and every one he drives is a risk. Neither ends the game.

Scene 6. The cold-store road. If the fifty pence was refused, or left three days, it is answered: the Widow's men stand across the cold-store road at half past five, and she stands in front of them, hands in her coat pockets. Tom's line leaves him four things: wait, with a packing shift going cold in the back; pay; turn round and lose the shift; or send Ron. If Ron goes, he gets out, walks to his old gang, says six words the player cannot hear, and the line opens like a gate. The Widow watches him do it. That is the moment she decides what she wants, and the player sees her decide. If the fifty pence was paid, she asks Ron to settle a row between two gangs at the gate, and he does, in six words. That night, at the count, it is raining, and the player has his first chance to offer Sheila the lift home. One press. "I've legs." She has walked it for thirty-one years. He can offer every night; she says the same three ways.

Scene 7. The second lesson. Sunday 4 November, written to be laughed at. L-plates cut from a biscuit tin, tied on with baling twine. Ron folds himself in behind the stuck seat with his knees either side of the wheel, like a man in a tin bath. "I go, you stop." He goes. He drives the whole dock road at eleven miles an hour with the commentary of a ship's pilot: "Coming up on Stannard's. That was Stannard's. Leaving Stannard's." A gull will not move off the road and he will not sound the horn at it ("It's not done owt") and the two of them sit behind a gull for as long as the player lets them; the handbrake is the only way to end it and Ron takes it as a criticism. He greets the same night-watchman twice, with his whole arm, and the car follows the arm. At the far end he says, "Round again?" and the player finds he has been steering by voice for a mile and enjoying it.

And one thing is not funny, and the player sees it with his own hands idle. Ron changes down for the bend at the cold store without being told, clean, by ear, a thing he was never shown. Asked where he learned that: "Watching. Thirty year of watching." Then the warehouse row opens ahead of them and he stops short of it. "Not down there, boss. Surface is bad." It is not. That night Darren, selling what he hears: "Your doorman sits in that car some nights. In the yard. In the dark, with the engine off. I've seen his hands going." A man who cannot drive, who changes gear like a driver, sits in car two in the dark, and will not go down the row.

Scene 8. The Tangs' queue, Friday 2 November, and what Ron asks. The player is in the queue for three of number nine when Shiner, six back, loud, says the thing that moves the story, and the player hears it with half the Hook: "A year a fortnight Sunday. I could tell you who had that cab on the row, if I'd a mind." Ron does not turn round. He buys two portions, not three, and then goes back for the third. On the Sunday, getting out of car two after the lesson, he does a thing he has never done: "I'll want tomorrow night, boss. And after. I've to go down the gate." If the player has learned to use the page and asks him what he wants, it takes him the length of the dock road to answer: "To be let alone, boss. It's not money and it's not you. There's a thing I've to stand on, and I can only stand on it from down there. I can't say what. Will you take that?" Take it, or do not: it is the one offer in the game that is a request. He goes either way. Nothing the player can do keeps Ron off the gate, and the game does not pretend otherwise: what asking changes is what the Fifth means.

What the player believes going into the Fifth: a coat with no sleeves, a third portion, a road Ron will not drive down, a gear change, a man sitting in a dark car, and somebody in a chip queue who could say who drove. He has one suspect, and it is his friend.

### The midpoint: the Fifth

Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990. The quay is full for the fireworks off the old coal jetty, and at the dock gate the dockers have their own fire, as every year: pallets and dunnage, a blaze the size of a van. It is the busiest night of the cab year, and the first Tom has driven without Ron: twenty minutes of the best fares in the game, three cars kept moving because Danny said to park them.

1. The fare. In Pauline's ordinary voice: "Car one, the dock gate, on the Widow's account, and she says bring a coat, there's a fire." At the gate the Widow is standing on a pallet with a clipboard, reading the winter's hire list by firelight. Beside her, straight-backed, in a foreman's donkey jacket with THE GATE across the shoulders, is Ron. His old gang is round him, and Shiner's name is read out for the night gate, alone, till Easter. Shiner stops talking.
2. The fire. On top of the dockers' bonfire, where the guy goes, somebody has sat a figure in a car coat with a cab's roof sign hung round its neck. The sign says MICKEY'S. It is Danny's lads' joke. Ron does not laugh. He does not take it down either. He watches it burn with his hands in the pockets of her coat. Half the Hook sees who stood by. If the player took what Ron asked on Sunday, Ron looks across the fire at the cab, once, and nods, and the player knows what he is looking at: a man standing on something. If he did not take it, or never asked, Ron does not look across, and the player sees a friend who has been bought. It is the same scene and it is two different nights.
3. The radio. On the dock road, going back, Pauline, to every cab at once, kindly, which is worse: "Car one. You on your own?"
4. The window. With nobody on the door, Danny's lads have put the office window in while the street watched fireworks. Ada is on her step in her coat. "You'd better come in, Tom." It is the first time anyone on the street has used his name. Tea, and an old woman talking to fill a bad hour: who did the window; what her Walter would have said. Then the thing she has been turning over: "He'll not have gone for money. I've known Ron Kirby forty years. He'd not cross the road for money."
5. The lift. Midnight, the office, a board over the window and rain coming through the gap. Sheila has swept the glass herself and counted the night's money by a desk lamp. The player offers the lift, as he may have every night for a week. She looks at the rain and at the board. "Car one. Not the other." She gets into the back, never the front, and has him stop a street short of her own. She pays nothing and he writes nothing. At the next night's count she finds the gap in his line before he does. "Monday. Ten past twelve. You've left one out." He can say it was not a fare. "Every fare goes in the book." She writes it herself, small and upright: "00.10. Car one. Office to Tannery Row. One. No charge." It is the first time in thirty-one years she has been a line in her own book, and the player has just watched what it costs her to be driven.

What is lost: the seat. In the morning Ron's door key is on the office counter, on top of his last wage packet, which he has not taken.

### Second movement: the Empty Seat

About four hours. Ron is never out of sight. Every morning at six he is across the dock road, picking twenty men. On the second morning he picks something else, without looking across: "Mickey's. Two cars for the packing shift. Six sharp." A gift from a foreman to his old firm. Take it or pay.

Scene 9. Who sits in front. Nights, alone. Nobody names the buildings. Other people start taking the front seat. Darren: "Can I sit up front? Only it's empty." He sells radios that pick up one station to fares from the front seat. Garbutt goes round the firm with the Widow's offers: Kevin has his plate by Friday, if Tom did not meet it, and Pauline is asked what she wants. If the player asks Pauline first, the answer is a Saturday. Darren says what he wants unasked: the door, and a coat with a firm's name on it. It is the one want it may be wrong to meet, and nothing says so.

Scene 10. Remembrance Sunday, 11 November. The dockside memorial. Father Walsh reads the names: the wars, and the men the port has taken since; this year, Michael Suddaby, who drove its men home for forty years. Everybody is in one place for the only time; Ron has the gate's wreath, and the coat. And beside the dockers' wreath, in a wheeled chair with a rug, a man of sixty whose hands are in grey cotton gloves. Two minutes of silence in which the player chooses only where Tom looks, and sees who looks back; Sheila is looking at the man in the chair, and then at nothing. Ellis is standing next to Tom, by chance or not. When it is over: "A year next Sunday. I've an hour on that row with nobody in it. I ask while a thing's answerable, Mr Nowak." Afterwards Ron puts a foil parcel in the man's lap. The third portion.

Scene 11. Car two, and last year's book. The wing wants doing again, and Shaun Hewson has the driver's seat out to get at the runner while he is there. "Second time I've done this wing. Mickey said he'd clipped the bollard on the dock road. He was a careful driver, Mickey. I thought it at the time. And here's your seat." Wedged in the runner, holding it at its furthest forward notch for a year: a docker's brass tally with a number on it. Behind it, a cotton glove burnt through at the palm. He puts them on the counter at the count. Sheila looks at them for the length of one line of writing. Then she does a thing the player has not seen her do: she gets up, lifts down the 1989 books from the shelf behind her chair, sets them on his side of the counter, and goes to put the kettle on. She does not open them for him. The find is a count, done with the hands: the night's sheets, the mileage column, the garage's bills and the fare book, set side by side. The seat's repair has been on the garage's bill twice in a year and struck off twice, in ink. For Saturday 18 November 1989 there is a driver's sheet for car one in Terry's hand and none for car two. In the fare book, two lines in the small upright hand he has been writing beside for three weeks:

"22.50. Car one. Office to Infirmary. One. No charge."

"Night. Car two. Out and back. Eleven miles. No sheet. No charge."

The night of the fire. He knows now what a line in that book is, and that she does not write what did not happen. Terry, asked, uses seven words: "I took Mickey up. Not the row." The dead uncle did not drive. That leaves, to the player's mind, one man: the one who changes gear by ear and sits in that car in the dark. The tea she puts by his elbow has gone cold when he looks up, and she has gone home, on foot.

Scene 12. Ropery Lane. The player can follow the third portion on a Friday, or go to the door Ron always took alone. A sister who answers; an old ship's surgeon's bag in the hall; a man in a chair who breathes like someone who has to think about it. Tom has the tally. The Bishop looks at it for a long time, and tells one thing: "That's mine. He came back in for me. Had me on his back the length of the row before the car come back. You'll be the nephew. Your uncle never missed a Friday. You needn't start."

Scene 13. Eleven miles. Mileage is a truthful thing, and car two has a mileometer on its dash. Nothing asks the player to do this, and the Ledger has had "Eleven miles?" on its open page since the count. Driven at night, the straight way, yard to landing to row to Ropery Lane to yard, it is seven. He can drive it twice. It is seven. Four miles of that night are somewhere else. Two miles up the dock road from the row there is a bollard with the firm's paint on it, older than the lessons; out to it and back is four. The Ledger writes one line under the route: "The car went. Then it came back." The player has found, with a steering wheel and a dial, what nobody has said: whoever was driving left the row with the fire behind them, and turned round. And the Bishop has said who was on foot. The two things do not fit, and the player who notices that is ahead of the story by four days, which is allowed.

Scene 14. The two strangers. The window and the effigy want answering, and the street is waiting to see whether a firm with nobody on its door can. It is planned like a job, on the board in the office, and it is the funniest half hour in the game. Tapper and Shiner, lent for a day by a foreman who does not ask what for, go over on the morning boat in borrowed car coats and come back on the last ferry as two strangers with a holdall. Tapper's coat is Kevin's and reaches his ankles; Shiner's is Darren's and does not reach his wrists. Car one meets them with its light off and drives them, slowly, past the Tangs' queue to Danny's lock-up on the row, where they are to stand and look at it for five minutes and be driven back. The one rule Ron sends with them: Shiner is not to speak, because half the Hook knows his voice. The player watches him not speak. He does not speak at the terminal, where the ticket clerk asks if he has anything to declare and he shakes his head so hard his cap comes off. He does not speak passing his own sister. He lasts as far as the Tangs', where a man in the queue says the far-side lot have sent two right hard cases, and Shiner, to the windscreen, with dignity: "Three." Tapper hits him with his cap. Outside the lock-up they stand for the five minutes like two men waiting for a bus that has been explained to them. Nothing is touched.

Then it comes out, on the way back, to the back of Tom's head, because Shiner has had nobody to say it to: "A month on the night gate. On my own. There's nowt down there but gulls. He had a word first, and I laughed, so he went and got a coat. All I said was I could tell them who had the cab. I never said the name." Tapper hits him again, harder, and it is not funny. The player has set up a job and got, out of it, the day and the reason Ron asked for a foreman's coat: there is a name, it is not to be said, and Ron went to the gate to see that it is not. To the player it is the last proof: a man does that for himself. Comes back: Danny empties his lock-up that night and is seen doing it. Two days later, in the back of car one: "The ticket lass says they went over on the nine o'clock. You sent two old dockers on a day out and I shifted four hundred radios in the rain. That's the best thing anybody's done to me. Your door's still a hole, Nowak. I'm just what happens."

Scene 15. Pauline's Saturday, 17 November. If the player asked Pauline what she wanted, she takes her first Saturday off in nine years and Tom has the radio; if not, she has gone to the gate's office for the day, on trial, and Tom has the radio anyway. Sheila comes in to mind the book. It is a comedy for two people and a microphone. He sends Terry to Copper Row for a fare who is standing in the office. He calls Kevin "car two" and Kevin, who is car two, does not answer for a full minute, on principle. Sheila corrects every call he makes without looking up from the book, in four words or fewer, and he says them into the microphone after her like a man repeating a dose. Terry is asked his position four times and uses a fifth word: "Tell Pauline it don't count." Mrs Rennison rings to ask if the new man is all right, because he sounds foreign, and Sheila takes the telephone and says, "He's from away. He's learning," which is the kindest thing she has said about him.

Then the evening goes wrong in the ordinary way. Kevin's shift ends and he goes. Terry is on the infirmary run. Three calls are waiting and one of them is Ada, at the chapel, in the rain, with her hip. Car two is in the yard with its keys on the hook. The player knows by now (the keys on their hook, the licence in her purse when she paid the glazier) or does not know, that there is a second person in the room who has ever held a licence. He can say it or ask it. She does not look up. "I don't drive." It is in the present tense and it is true. Ada waits forty minutes for Terry and says so to the whole street.

What the player has by the end of the fourth week, none of it told to him as a secret: somebody took car two to the fire, drove off, and came back; Ron was in the building and carried a man out of it; Ron asked for the coat to keep one name out of a chip queue. He believes what every reader of this kind of story believes: that the doorman who "cannot drive" drove, and is hiding it. He is wrong, and everything he has found is true.

### The turn: Sunday 18 November, a year to the day

It is one day in two halves, and the second half is the scene the game is built to reach.

Morning: the third lesson. A ship is in on Sunday overtime. The player goes to the gate at six and holds out the keys of car two, with a route in his notebook: he means Ron to drive the eleven miles. Ron drives, knees at his ears, at eleven miles an hour, and he is good now, as good as the player has made him: the landing, the dock road. At the mouth of the warehouse row he brakes, by himself, for the first time in three lessons, and puts the handbrake on with his own hand. "I've never been down here in a car, boss. Not once in my life. I went down it on my feet and I come up it with a man on my back, and there was no car. It had gone." A long time. "Then it come back. I'll say that. It come back." The player asks nothing, or asks; Ron has said all he will. About the gears, getting out: "I've sat in this car most nights since. Going through it. So I'd know how. So it'd not be..." He stops, as he has stopped five times; this one he does not finish either. He walks back to the gate. The man the player has been sure of for two weeks was on foot. Then who had the car? There is the length of one grey Sunday to think about a seat that has not moved in a year, and who is short.

Night: the row. At dusk there are candles on the burnt plot: Alison, Ellis off duty, a dozen of the street, and the Bishop, who has made his sister wheel him down in the cold because it is a year. Tom has brought Ada in car one and is waiting for her, on the far side of the Hook from the office. Sheila is in the office, on a Sunday, alone, doing the week's books because she could not stay at home tonight. At ten to eleven the radio opens, and it is not Pauline. It is the only time in the game her voice is not level.

"Car one. Ropery Lane's rung. He can't get his breath. Terry's at the ferry. Where are you?"

He is four minutes away at the best the car will do, with an old woman to get into it first. The player drives it as fast as he dares, the first time the game has let him hurry in car one for something that matters, and when he swings into the yard to pick up whoever is to come, car two is not there. The gate is open. The keys are off the hook. He catches it on the dock road: two tail lights going steadily, not fast, on the right line through every bend, and through the back window a small head that does not reach the top of the seat. He follows her, and the player holds the wheel behind her for a mile and a half and watches her drive. She puts car two against the kerb at Ropery Lane a hand's width off, as a woman does who passed first go, and gets out, and has not touched the seat. It is where she left it.

Then it is work, and there are two of them to do it. The Bishop is on the stairs in a kitchen chair, grey, and it wants two people to carry a chair down a turn of stair without tipping it: the player takes the low end and Sheila the top, and the game makes them do it in step. On the pavement she holds out the keys. "You drive. I'll go in the back with him." Car one has Ada in it. So Tom gets into car two, and the seat will not go back, and he drives the fastest two miles of his life with his knees either side of the wheel and the only laugh in the scene is that it is not funny. In the back, a woman's voice, counting a man's breaths aloud, in the same voice she reads sheets in.

The infirmary. The night sister on the door is June Suddaby. She gets the Bishop onto a trolley, looks at car two, at who is at its wheel and who is getting out of its back, and says to nobody: "Dad's car. I took Dad in off that kerb, a year tonight. I never knew who'd had this one." She knows now, and so does the player. On the row a year ago an infirmary was the one place this man could not be brought. Tonight she brought him.

The corridor. Ron comes up it at half past midnight, on foot from the gate, in the foreman's coat. Sheila is on a bench with the Bishop's rug folded on her knees. He stops. For a year he has said good morning to her every day and neither of them has said one word about it. He sits down at the other end of the bench. Nobody speaks. The player can sit between them, or stand.

At two in the morning, in the office, she opens the fare book and writes the night down herself, because every fare goes in the book: "22.55. Car two. Office to Ropery Lane to Infirmary. Two. No charge." And then two letters she has never put to a line in thirty-one years: "S.D." The 1989 book is still on his side of the counter. He can lay them side by side. It is the same hand, and the only difference is the initials.

"Mr Nowak," she says, not looking up. And then, because he has earned it or because she is too tired to keep it from him: "Tom. I'll walk."

What the turn is. Not a word and not a deduction: the player followed her tail lights. Everything he found was true and he fitted it to the wrong person, the way the street would have. The woman who taught him what a line is worth wrote that line about herself and has waited a year for a reader. The man in the next seat was never hiding his own night; he was learning to drive so that it would never be hers again, and he went to the gate so that her name would stay out of a chip queue. And she did tonight, in front of everybody, the one thing she has not done since.

The act ends on a letter: courteous, dated, signed T. A. Reese. It is on the mat on Monday 19 November and names Monday the 26th.

## 8. Act III: The Ledger Comes Due

Monday 19 November to Saturday 1 December. About three and a half hours. Two weeks: the week of the letter, in which every opponent moves on what Sunday night showed the street; and the week of the inspector, which has a clock with a man on it.

### Why the inspector comes

The firm is registered for VAT because of its account work, and the name on the form changed when a registered trader died, so every player gets the letter. Tom's own wash, if he has one, gives it teeth. And a gentleman has telephoned to say that one of the firm's cars was out on a certain night with nothing written.

It matters more than Agar knows, for a reason in plain sight since Alison's first fare: the warehouse was a bonded store, its cigarettes under the Crown's lock. The night the bond burned belongs to Customs and Excise, and the mild man who is coming about the takings is an officer of the department that was robbed.

### The week of the letter

Ron, nights. On Monday night Ron is on the rank when car one comes in, in his own coat. He has given the Widow his word for the winter and he keeps it: days are hers. "Nights are mine, boss. If you'll have them." He gets into the front seat. He does not name a building for two streets. Then: "That was Stannard's." The seat is filled again, after dark only, and the player has him beside him for everything the last two weeks ask. The Bishop is on a ward; June will not say more than "comfortable".

A fare for Mr Agar. Geoffrey Agar has never been in a minicab. He books one, by letter, to the dock gate, and sits in the back with his hat on his knee. Four hundred berths want building and every pair of hands on the quay is the Widow's. He asks her price standing in the mud. It is fair, and it is more than he has until the basin is his, and the basin is not his without the yard behind Mickey's. Both of them look at the driver. At Quay Street it is raining, and he gets out and stands in it, and leaves the hat on the seat. "I have never had to burn anything, Mr Nowak. Things on that quay fall down by themselves, and I buy what is left. My building fell down with one of your uncle's cabs outside it. My watchman saw it, and saw the driver, and I have not said so for a year, because I wished to buy your yard and not to ruin you. On the thirty-first the bank buys what is left of me. So I have telephoned the Crown. I do apologise." Then, in another voice: "Somebody of yours will want a solicitor. I keep three. That is a gift, and I do not expect you to believe it." Take it or pay. The hat can be returned or kept; kept, it is on the office hat-stand for the rest of the game and Sheila dusts it.

The Widow in the passenger seat. She books car one and sits in front, in his seat, by day, when it is empty. Her last offer, put once: Mickey's becomes the gate's firm; Tom keeps the name on the roof and a wage; the club's book is shut, the eleven who can work get work, and she will see the thirty onto what the state allows. "It stands until your books have been looked at. After that I'm buying from whoever has the keys." Yes, no, or a straight question. Asked what she knows of that night: "What half the Hook knows since Sunday. I'd as soon talk to the tide as to that sergeant, and so would any man of mine." And an offer nobody could call a threat: the gate will pay the Bishop's keep, as its own, for good. It is generous, and it would make the firm's oldest debt hers.

Car two is gone. Ellis was on the row on Sunday night and saw two cabs leave it. On Wednesday she comes to the yard and looks at car two's wing, and at its seat, and at the height of the bookkeeper through the office window. "Whose is this car to drive?" She asks while a thing is answerable. Danny hears only that a sergeant looked at a car. That night the yard gate is forced and car two is not there in the morning: he has it in his lock-up, and the price is the freephone and the rank. Refused, he does the thing in the game that hurts somebody: a yellow car crowds car three off the dock road into the wall, and Julie Kettlewell, three weeks in the job, has her arm in plaster and says she will be in on Monday. Fetching car two back is the last break-in of the game, at night, with three ways in (a key cut from one look at the padlock; the roof light; the door) and, for the first time since the Fifth, with Ron: he takes the door and whoever is behind it, and Tom takes the car, and coming out of the row with the lock-up's alarm going Ron is in the passenger seat of the car he will not sit behind the wheel of on that road. Danny, after, on the pavement with a split lip from his own lock-up door: "She weren't meant to hit the wall." It is not an apology. Hit or not, he says the other thing: "My dad stood at that gate nine winters and never got picked. You want me off your kerb, get him a day." It can be got. It means asking her.

### The week of the inspector

Monday 26 November. Reese at the counter with the book since October. A flask of tea, a bad knee, a propelling pencil. "A fare at no charge is either a kindness or a hole, Mr Nowak. I'm paid to ask which."

Tuesday. The ride-along. "I always ride a route or two. Mileage is a truthful thing." He gets into the front passenger seat without asking. "My father drove a cab. I always rode in front." It is the comic scene of the last act and it is written to be one. Brenda Laverack gets in at the fish dock with a box that she holds shut with both forearms. The box moves. Reese, politely, to the windscreen: "Is that declared, Mrs Laverack?" "They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in." At the first bend the box gets away from her. The player drives the next half mile with a Customs officer's feet up on the dashboard and one crab on the parcel shelf, which Reese addresses as "sir". Darren, flagged down at the Tangs', tries to sell a radio through the window to a man with a clipboard and is asked for a receipt book, and produces one, and it is the Seamen's Mission's. Reese buys the radio. "It only gets the one station." "It's a good station."

Wednesday. The old books. He reads both lines aloud. "Ten to eleven, car one, office to infirmary, one, no charge. A kindness. And the same night, car two, out and back, eleven miles, no sheet, no charge. Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak." He takes off his glasses. "I know that date. Every officer in this collection knows it. I was not sent about a bond, and I had hoped not to find one." Sheila has not moved. What he needs, by Friday at two: who drove, in the driver's own hand; and the eleven miles, shown to him on the road. "If it is one night, and I can see where it went and that nothing came away from that building, I shall write what I saw and what I recommend. If nobody will say, the Investigation Division will ask instead, and I must assess six years of this firm as though every unsheeted night were a working shift, and no firm of this size survives that. Either way my copy goes up the hill to the police. A bonded store was broken and a man was burned. That is the Crown Court, Mr Nowak, not a fine."

Wednesday night: the clock. Ron, on the quay wall, with three of number nine and nobody to take the third to. He has heard; the whole rank has. He says it once, to the water, and it is the longest speech he makes in the game. "Friday. Four o'clock. I'm going up the hill in my suit and I'm telling Carol Ellis it was me had that car. I was there. It's near enough true. And I can drive now. You've seen to that. They'll have me in it and I'll show them. She'll not be asked. She'll not stand in that court at fifty-three with the whole Hook on the benches." The player can use the page: what does he want? "For it never to have been her." It cannot be met. He cannot be talked out of it by a man who does not talk, and the game does not offer a speech. He can only be overtaken. From here the Ledger carries one line at the top of every page: "Friday, four."

Wednesday night: Sheila's kitchen. Tom has never been to her house and has to find it, a street past where she has him stop the car. The 1989 book is on the oilcloth, and beside it a driving licence first issued in 1961. She says four sentences, with her back to him. "He went down at ten to eleven and the men were at the landing. There were forty-one in that book, so I took them. I wrote it down on the Monday, because every fare goes in the book. Nobody reads a fare book." What happened on the row she does not say; she has never said it aloud. There is a pen on the table and a fire in the grate. If the player opens his Ledger at the page headed "Asked", she turns round. "To have it read. And to write my own name to it, and not have it written for me. Not by a dead man and not by Ron Kirby. I left him on that row. He doesn't get to carry me off it."

So there are two names that can go on the paper, and a fire.

- Hers. She will write it, if by Friday the truth can be shown whole: that nothing left the bond, that a heater went over, that the driver went back. Then what a court has in front of it is the night as it was. That takes two things the player does not have: the word of the men who were there, and the eleven miles driven for the inspector by the hand that drove them.
- Ron's. Do nothing, or help him: he has had three lessons. He will drive Reese the eleven miles himself on Friday at two and walk up the hill at four, and she will never be asked. It is a lie, told for her by the man she left, and it takes from her the only thing she has said she wants.
- The fire. The book goes in the grate. After that no book of this firm will hold, and both of them are asked anyway.

Thursday: two doors, and somebody in the way at each. He has one day, the phone box on the corner and five coins, and he can ring only people he once let give him something.

- The ward, for the Bishop's own account of who carried him and what was in his hands. June is in the way, and she is right to be: he is her patient and he is not fit to be questioned. What June wants, asked: "To know what my father was doing at a desk at eleven at night with his heart going, and who he was waiting on." The answer is on the counter at Quay Street, in a line she has never read. If the player brings her the 1989 book, she reads "Office to Infirmary. One. No charge." in the hand of the woman who sent him, sits down on a ward chair, and gives Tom ten minutes with the Bishop and writes down what he says herself, as a night sister, timed and signed.
- The gate, at the six o'clock pick, in front of sixty men, for Tapper and Shiner's word on paper, and for a day's work for Frank Cammack. This is the Widow's last scene and she is at her full height on her pallet. Her men's names on a Customs paper puts her gate beside a burned bond. If Tom has taken her lifts and left her fifty pences owing and answered her question with nothing, she prices it once, aloud: the firm. Mickey's for two signatures. If he has dealt straight with her all game she looks at him for some time, and then at her foreman, who is standing beside her in her coat and has not asked her for anything since the day he asked for it. "Go on, the pair of you. And tell his father Monday, six sharp. I'll not have the lad." It costs her, and the player sees it cost: by Friday the cold store has given its winter contract to a firm from over the water, because her gate's name is on a paper about a bond. She does not mention it. Danny's cars are off the kerb by night.

Friday 30 November. Three things in order, and the first of them cannot be done alone.

A quarter to two: the yard. Agar's surveyor is at the yard gate with two men and a padlock: the lease lets the freeholder close the yard for a survey, and he never has until the one day it bites. Car two is inside. Reese is on the forecourt with his flask. The lock comes off for a signature on an option to buy the yard, and then Agar has what he wanted and the firm has no back way out for ever. Or the gate comes off. It is ten feet of iron and it wants a dozen men, and the player has spent fourteen hours playing a man who has never asked anybody for anything. The dinner hooter has gone and the gate's men are sitting along the dock wall with their sandwich tins. He has to walk across the road and ask them. It is one press, and it is the hardest in the game, and what happens next was decided weeks ago: the men who get up are the ones he let do him a turn, or did one for and let them pay it back. Tapper, always. Shiner, talking. Then, if he has lived in this town and not just worked in it, the bench from the hearing, twenty of them, putting their caps in their pockets. They lift a ten-foot gate off its hinges and lean it against the wall with the padlock still locked. "We never touched his lock." If he took from nobody, nobody gets up but Darren, brave and no use, and he signs.

Two o'clock: the eleven miles. Whose hand the name is in decides who drives.

- "S. Dunn", in a small upright hand. She gets into car two and does not touch the seat. Tom is beside her, with the handbrake. In the back is an officer of Customs and Excise, writing. She drives the night: the landing, the row, the burnt plot with Sunday's candle ends on it; and then the four miles to the bollard, faster than she means to, as she did. The prompt is under the player's hand as it was on the dock road in October. If he pulls it, he has stopped her as he stopped Ron, and she says, "Thank you, I can manage," and it is the coldest line in the game. If he leaves it alone, she brakes a yard short and puts the handbrake on herself, and sits. Then she turns the car round. Ropery Lane; the infirmary kerb; the yard. "Eleven and a tenth. I'll allow the tenth." With Thursday's papers in the folder: "Nothing left the bond. I have three men, a watchman's heater, and a driver who went back. For the Crown's part I shall say so, Miss Dunn. What a court does with it is not mine to say. I am sorry." He takes, for the first time that week, the cup of tea she has made him every day. "I'm permitted on a Friday."
- "R. Kirby", in a docker's capitals. He drives the eleven miles on L-plates at eleven miles an hour, knees at his ears, with Tom beside him and Reese in the back. At the bollard he stops himself. "You were in no hurry that night, Mr Kirby." Reese looks at the seat, and at Ron's legs, and at the small woman in the office doorway. "I am paid to ask which. I am not paid to measure." He writes what he is given, and under it, in pencil, that he doubts it.

Twenty to four: the hill. During the eleven miles, at the bollard, Pauline to every cab at once: "Car two. If anybody's interested. Ron Kirby's just gone past the Tangs' in his funeral suit. Walking." He did not trust it, or would not watch it. The police station is at the top of the town and he has twenty minutes on her signature. So the game's last act of driving is the opposite of its climax: Tom, alone, in car one, with a sheet of Customs paper on the passenger seat, flat out up through a Friday afternoon, the ferry traffic, the yellow cars that are or are not out of the way, the market on Weighhouse Lane. For fourteen hours the game has taught him to drive as though people matter. A car hurts and never kills; if he hits somebody he can stop and help and be late, or not stop.

The steps. Ron is at the top of them with his cap in his hand, and Ellis is in the doorway, and the clock over the door says what it says.

In time: Tom holds the paper out. He has no words and needs none. Ron reads her name, in her hand, and under it Reese's: "the driver went back". It takes him a long time. "She's wrote it herself." He puts his cap on. "Then I've no business up here." Ellis has heard none of it and all of it. "You'll both be back up this hill, Ron Kirby. Easter. You were in that building. I'm not making it nothing." She charges everybody the paper names: Sheila Dunn, Ron Kirby, Tapper and Shiner, for a bonded store broken into. Bail from the counter. A sergeant's own note on the file that the driver went back and took a burned man out.

Late: he has said it. Ellis has written it down, and looks at the paper Tom is holding, and at the man who lied to her on her own step. "You were on foot, Ron Kirby. I've had a watchman's word for a year that it was somebody small." Now there is a false statement to answer as well, and her name is on the paper regardless. Ron comes down the steps past Tom without looking at him.

If the name on the paper was Ron's, there is no race: Tom drives him up the hill himself, or lets him walk.

Saturday 1 December is the ending, as section 9 has it.

## 9. The endings

Three, each with one close. States, never a menu: nothing is picked at the end. Which one the player gets is decided by which seat Ron is in when Friday is over, and that was decided by what the player did across six weeks: whether a line in the fare book was ever worth anything to him, whether anybody in the Hook would get up off a wall for him, whether he let Sheila write her own name, and whether he got it up the hill in time. Each has a last scene, a line in the fare book and a line in the Ledger, and each says where the three opponents stand.

### The Kingdom: Ron beside him

Reached when her own name is on the paper, the truth was made readable, and Ron came down the steps with his cap on.

She has set her own price, which nobody could talk down: "I'll not keep a book I'm in." After thirty-one years the fare book is kept by Pauline, badly, with Sheila reading it over her shoulder from the customer's side of the counter. The Bishop's keep is in it now as an account, in the open. The club pays out on Friday, by hand or, on the other road, as a lawful society with forty-one signatures, Ada's first.

Last scene: the dock road, six in the morning, car one. Ron is in the passenger seat by choice, by day, for the first time since the Fifth: he has given the Widow back her coat, folded, and she took it without a word and made Tapper foreman. He is naming buildings. In the back, a fare for the first time in thirty-one years by her own asking, is Sheila, going home from a counter that is not hers. At the gate the Widow is picking twenty, a contract poorer, with Frank Cammack in the line; she does not look at the cab, and then she does. On the parade the agents' board is down from the yard wall: the yard was never signed for, and on the thirty-first the bank will have Mr Agar's basin; his hat is still on the office stand. Danny, from the Tangs' doorway, with a split lip healing: "Morning, Tom." At her door Sheila gets out, and pays, and counts it twice. "Mind how you go, Tom." Ron, as they pull away: "Easter. I can't write her a reference, boss. I can fetch them as'll sit. I'll be sat with them, mind. Other side of the rail."

Fare book, in Pauline's round hand: "Dunn, S. Office to home. £2. Paid." Ledger: "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four."

### The Straight Life: Ron at the gate

Reached when a name that is not the driver's stands: Ron drove the eleven miles for her, or got to the top of the steps first.

He answers at Easter for the wheel as well as the building, and for the lie if Ellis caught it. He has gone back into the Widow's coat for good, because a man on a charge keeps what work he has, and she has kept every deal she ever made. Sheila's keys are on the counter on top of the fare book. She would not be written for; she said so, in her own kitchen, to the one person who asked. The firm stands, or Tom has taken the Widow's price and the roof sign reads THE GATE; either way it is a business and no more, with nobody on the door.

Last scene: the gate at six, from across the dock road. Tom is in car one with the seat beside him empty, waiting on the packing shift the foreman still sends his old firm. Sixty men, twenty picked. Ron reads the list in the coat. He does not come over. When the cab pulls off he lifts one hand, not high. Shiner is on the night gate till Easter and has stopped talking. Agar has his option on the yard, or the bank has Agar. Danny has the freephone on alternate weeks and says nothing in the queue. On Tannery Row a small woman is walking to a job in the infirmary's wages office, where June got her a start; she does not take the cab.

Fare book, in Tom's hand, the sevens like ones: "Gate. Account. Two cars. No charge." Ledger: "He drove it for her. Nobody asked her."

### Burn Both: Ron gone

Reached when the book went on the fire, or the firm's own books did not hold, or he signed the yard away and had nobody to ask.

"I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak." There is not one. The Investigation Division asks instead, and asks everybody: Sheila is asked for her keys by a constable half her age and hands them over without looking at Tom. A plain car goes up Quay Street with Ron in the back seat, looking straight ahead: he has told them it was him, and this time nobody was in time. By Saturday Agar's agents have their lock on the door and the Widow is buying from whoever has the keys.

Last scene: Saturday night is the first night again. The quay after ten, the lamp, the suitcase in his hand, the last ferry with its ramp down. A constable turns round. He does not call "You there." He calls, "Nowak!" The top of the name ladder from the wrong side. The player has the two inputs of night one, stand or run, and the close cuts on the press. Ada is at her window, as she was when he came.

Fare book: closed, unwritten. Ledger: the last page cut out, as the first thirty were.

No ending redeems what was done, and none makes it nothing. Reloading is never stopped.

## 10. What was true all along

For the builders: the truth in the order the player can find it. The street never has it. The street says the importer burned his own warehouse and went south; his insurers, finding nothing either way, paid.

1. The club. Walter Harker's sick club of 1953 grew a second purse under the dock scheme: cargo short-tallied at the gate by Ron's gang and moved in Mickey's cabs. Forty-one names at £4 is £164 a week. Since the gate stopped in July 1989 the row's tenants have paid a watch of about £90, and Mickey found the rest by drawing no wage and driving nights, which is why his heart went.
2. The night. By November 1989 the club could not last the winter. The importer's bonded store held cigarettes due to ship on the Monday. Mickey planned it: three men in (Ron, Tapper, the Bishop), Shiner on the corner, twenty minutes, the watchman on his break, himself at the wheel. He is not innocent; he is only not the driver. At ten to eleven his heart turned at his desk, and Terry took him up in car one. The men were already at the landing. There were two people left in the office who had ever held a licence, and one of them was on the floor. Sheila, who had kept the club's book for thirty years and knew what a winter without it meant, hauled car two's seat as far forward as it would go, and took them.
3. The fire. The watchman's paraffin heater went over among the cartons. Nothing was taken. Tapper and Shiner ran for the car, and Sheila drove. She was at the bollard on the dock road, two miles off, with the wing gone, before she knew who was not in it. She turned round. Ron had gone back in for the Bishop and had him on his back at the mouth of the row, with no sleeves to his coat. She drove them to the Bishop's sister's on Ropery Lane, because an infirmary asks questions and Mickey was in it; an old ship's surgeon did what could be done, and the street was told a stove went up. Eleven miles. The Bishop's tally went down the seat runner and jammed it, and the seat has been a short woman's ever since.
4. The year. On the Monday she wrote what the car did, because every fare goes in the book, and could not write who. Mickey came off the ward, paid the Bishop's keep every Friday, and told nobody. Ron said good morning to her every day and never once spoke of it; neither did she. She has never driven again, and has not been driven either: she walks. Agar's watchman, back from his hour, saw a cab leaving the row with somebody small at the wheel, and told his employer, who kept it.
5. The letter. "People are owed" meant the forty-one and one more. "A good woman who keeps the books" was all Mickey could write of her.
6. "New management." She has been waiting a year for a reader and has been afraid of one. It is why she keeps his name at arm's length, and why she taught him to write a line.
7. The lessons. Ron cannot drive, and wants to, for one reason: so that there is never again a night when the only one in the firm who can is a bookkeeper. He sits in car two in the dark going through the gears. He does not say so.
8. The coat. In October the Widow offered Ron the gate. He said no. Then Shiner said in the Tangs' queue that he could tell them who had the cab on the row, if he had a mind. Ron went down to the gate and asked for the coat, the first thing he had asked anyone for in his life, because a foreman says who works where. He left the only friend he had made in ten years to keep a name out of a queue; and it was the name of the woman who drove off and left him. The Widow asked no reason and holds nothing over anybody.
9. The second night. A year to the day the Bishop's chest went, and she was alone in the office with the keys on the hook, and this time she took him to the infirmary.

The turns, in the order they land. That somebody in the firm drove is plain from the count. That it was Ron is what three weeks are built to make the player believe, and this time with reason: he changes gear by ear, he sits in that car at night, he will not go down the row, and a man in a chip queue is being kept quiet by him. The turns are that the car went and came back (scene 13, on a dial); that Ron was on foot (the morning of the 18th, at the mouth of the row); and who was at the wheel (that night, through a back window, for a mile and a half). The last is not told to anybody. It is watched.

What the truth changes: "Every fare goes in the book", said in minute two by the one person with a line of her own in it; "New management"; the name she will not use; the lift she will not take; the pen she put in his hand; "I don't drive"; the seat every driver has complained of; Ron's knees; a year of good mornings; the bench of dockers; the effigy Ron watched burn; and "You never ask for owt", said by a man who has not asked one question in a year of the woman at the next desk.

Why it lands on somebody the player knows. He has sat across a table from her every night of the game. She taught him the one skill the last act needs. He has offered her a lift a dozen times and driven her once, and watched her write herself down for it. He was in the room when she said she did not drive.

Why it is fair. The seat is named in the walk-round and is a joke for eight hours. The repair is struck off the garage's bill twice, in ink. She walks; she will not get into car two; she says "I don't drive" and not "I can't". The Bishop says the car came back, and who was on foot. The tally is in the runner. Nobody else was in the office. A player who names her before the 18th has read it right, and still has to watch her do it.

## 11. The player who winds the club down

On the first Sunday he tells Sheila it is a cab firm and nothing more. The carry, the round and the watch stop. June, in his cab on the Monday, says what this road is built on: "It doesn't have to be shut. It has to be lawful. There are friendly societies older than the docks. You'd want a room, a bank book and the names."

It is the same story: the same four weeks, the same Fifth, the same count, dial, tail lights, inspector and hill. Three things differ, and only three.

- The Friday round is asking, not paying: twenty doors, a form, and people who will not sign for what Walter gave them. Albert Suggitt, mending a net: "I'll not sign a form for what Walter gave me." Ada's name is the one that brings the rest, and she holds it back until the Fifth.
- The dusk jobs carry the society, not the envelopes: its first money walked from the bank to the church hall in a holdall with Danny's lads behind; the hall held on Bonfire Night against Agar's agents, who own its freehold, so that nobody is on the office door and the window goes in just the same.
- The count turns up the eleven miles sooner and from the other side: the registrar wants three years of the old club's books, and it is Sheila who has to hand them to a stranger. She does.

Its last act is the same officer, the same line and the same Friday: he washed nothing, and the night can still close him. Its endings are the same three.

## 12. Side stories that are whole stories

The thirty-two drafts of 8 November stay as the short ones. Four are grown into whole stories.

1. The watch (Ron). Found: Mickey's watch in the pawnshop window the week he died. Turn: Ron pawned it himself, for the funeral flowers, because the office was frozen and he would not ask Sheila. Ends: redeemed and handed back without a word, or left in the window. Turn two, after the 18th: the wreath was paid for by the man Mickey's plan left in a fire.

2. A ride for the sergeant (Carol Ellis, Ivy Leadley). Found: rain, night, the sergeant's own car dead by the fish market, and Pauline, dry: "It's the police. She wants a car." The address is the door on Tannery Row where Ron said to knock soft. After that Ivy Leadley, seventy-one, books Mickey's every Sunday for chapel. Turn: Ivy knows exactly what her four pounds is. She has told her daughter for years that it comes from the chapel, because her daughter would stop it; and her daughter, who is nobody's fool, has chosen to believe her. Each is protecting the other from a thing both know. Ends: he keeps Ivy's confidence; tells Ellis; or lets Ivy know that he knows. It decides what the sergeant writes in her own note on the file on the last Friday.

3. One way (Karen Grummitt). Light. The ferry's ticket clerk, a wardrobe roped to a cab roof, a landlady's step it must pass twice, and a boat she cannot make herself get on. She is the clerk who is on, or not, the night two strangers come off the last ferry.

4. The umbrella (Alison Sedman). Three beats: her fare to the row and the umbrella left; an evening on the quay wall when she reads his Ledger over his shoulder and laughs out loud at the ham, the first time anyone in the Hook has found him funny; and, in the week after the anniversary, a fare to the row that she stops halfway. Her editor has offered her the news desk if she will leave the fire alone, and she asks Tom, as the one person whose answer she will take, what she should do. Turn: the person being bought in her story is her, and the man she asks knows who drove. Ends: she takes the desk; she keeps the row and loses it; or he tells her, and she holds it until he says.

## 13. The scene in the first half hour a player retells

The carry, minute nine, kept as written: three routes with honest costs (the lit quay, the dark yard and lane, the cab with the firm's name on its roof), a constable who can turn round, and next morning Ada asking what he was doing in her alley and Darren saying it to his face.

If the constable takes the envelope, he is honest, so Ellis has it by morning. Sheila, at the count: "Twenty people over the water are short this week. You don't know what that means yet." And in Mickey's debt book, under the five names, she writes a sixth in her own hand: "Nowak, T. £80."

The second is lesson one.

## 14. Humour and the mix of tones

Grounded noir carries the main line. The comedy is dry, local and attached to people; with no drink in the game, the radio is the bar.

- Pauline's commentary goes to every cab at once. "Kevin, wherever you are, you are not in Copper Row, because I can hear the gulls."
- Four set pieces are written above as scenes to be laughed at, one to each stretch, so that no stretch is without one: the second lesson, with the gull (scene 7); the two strangers, with Shiner under orders not to speak (scene 14); Pauline's Saturday, with Tom on the radio (scene 15); and the crabs, with an officer of Customs and Excise (the Tuesday of the last week). Smaller ones recur: Kevin's knees; Mr Agar's hat; Terry's four words.
- The same things turn grave without changing: the stuck seat is the joke of eight hours and then the worst two miles in the game; the eleven miles an hour of the lessons is the pace of the last Friday.

The darkest scenes are played quiet and never joked across: the Fifth, the corridor, the kitchen, the steps.

## 16. How play carries it, and the budget

Every beat is something the game's systems carry: a drive, a carry, a count, a break-in, a fight at a door, a press to take or pay, a page of questions asked once each. Talk is where what he did comes back. Nothing ends the game; Tom cannot die; a car hurts and never kills; nobody is killed by the story, and no gun is needed by it. No drink, no gambling, no children, anywhere.

What is fixed and what is not. Ron goes to the gate; Sheila drives on the 18th; the inspector comes. Those three happen to every player, and the story says so. Everything about what they mean and what they cost is the player's: whether Ron's going was a nod across a fire or a betrayal; whether the turn finds him sure of the wrong man; who gets up off the wall; whose name is on the paper; who is on the steps first.

Branches, counted. One question on the first Sunday (two roads, one story). Kevin two ways. The hearing two ways. The Fifth two ways (the same scene, read differently). Two names and a fire. Three endings, one close each. Version 5 had six closes, four signatures, two middles and a second road of six weeks.
