# LEDGER, story bible version 8: a critic's review

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8

1. Tom: 7. The flaw is the best thing about him: a man who cannot accept a gift, played as one press, take or pay, with no safe answer and real consequences at the hearing and the phone box. His want ("his own name said by people who mean it") is clear and the name ladder pays it off, but he is mostly a man things happen to, and his need is stated by the bible rather than forced on him by one choice that only he could make.
2. The cast: 9. Sheila, Ron, Pauline, Terry and his word tally, Cyril with his thumb on the envelope, Edith in fountain pen: these are people, each drawn in two lines and each given a turn. Ron and Tom is a friendship built for the hands (the passenger seat, the lessons, which seat he is in at the end) and it has a real chance of being remembered the way Henry and Hans are.
3. The antagonists: 8. Three rivals with different weapons (offers, the calendar, daylight competition) and peaks that come in turn; the Widow and Danny each get a scene that changes them. Agar is the thinnest: letters, hired jackets and a marina loan, rescued late by one good scene in the rain.
4. The central mystery: 8. It has a real truth (one lie by Mickey, written down by Sheila against his word) that changes the tea, the letter, the envelopes and the first night. But too much of it reaches the player as confession, and by scene 21 the climax has already been mostly given away.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The first night, the carry with three honest routes, the failed carry that writes "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book, and the tea that reads differently on a second run are excellent. The first Sunday ends in a long speech from Ada that explains the club, the fire and her method in one go, which is a lot of plot for minute 110.
6. Structure and pace: 7. Calendar days that pull a slow player forward, rival peaks in sequence and a midpoint that costs something give Act II a shape. Act III is overloaded: rival nights, Reese's five days, the kitchen, Ellis's trade and a Friday run with seven or eight errands in two to three hours.
7. The middle: 7. The rungs, the changing door on the Friday round and the town's dates are a serious answer to the "same loop" complaint. But it is twenty-one scenes, two roads, four grown side stories and three rivals in ten to thirteen hours of a hands-first game, so each beat gets minutes, and the straight road's three rungs are thinner than the keeper's five.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. The fare book as the only honest book, a VAT officer as the climax, Danny taking credit for a fire he did not light, and Frank's page are fresh. The frame around them is familiar: the sweet old widow who runs the rackets, the detective who cannot be bought, the reporter whose editor sits on the board, the developer whose loan is called on 31 December.
9. The endings: 9. Every ending has a last scene and a last image in two lines, one in Sheila's hand and one in Tom's, and Ada's table runs through all of them. "Nowak!" from the constable on the quay, the empty Ledger page and "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four" are each earned and each the sum of the man.
10. The side stories: 8. Ivy Leadley and her daughter each protecting the other from a thing both know is the best writing in the document, and the watch and the lilies tie back to the main line cleanly. Only four are grown, and the turns in two (the wardrobe, the lilies) are gentle rather than surprising.
11. Tone and humour: 8. Pauline's radio does the job a pub would do, the Ledger is funny on paper ("The ham had a seat"), and the crabs on the ride-along are a real set piece. The dark scenes are played quiet and the light ones sit before the Fifth, so the fall is felt.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The offers feed the hearing, the hooks and the phone box; the lock-up is planned like a job; the carry, the tail and the run are all things the systems can carry. Against that, the key turns of the truth are mostly people telling Tom things, and the climax is a man reading a book at a kitchen table.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The truth is told, not found, and the climax is spent before it arrives.**
What is wrong: almost every turn of the dig is a confession. Norman tells, Terry tells, Darren tells "all of it on the pavement in one breath", Ron tells, the importer tells, Agar tells, Sheila tells at the sink, Ada told on the first Sunday. Worse, by scene 21 Agar has said "I paid your uncle four thousand pounds to see that a plot came free", the importer has shown his PAID rent card, and the minute has named Agar's agents. The player walks into Sheila's kitchen already knowing Mickey took the money and lied. The only new thing there is that Sheila wrote the line.
Where: section 7, scenes 10, 12, 13, 17 and 21; section 8, Thursday night.
Why a player would feel it: BAR asks for a truth "uncovered by play" that pays off at the climax. A reviewer will say the game's investigation is a string of people unburdening themselves, and that Act III confirms rather than reveals.
Fix: Agar in the rain admits only that he paid for a plot to come free, and not to whom: "Ask your uncle's book. He wrote everything down." Ron's "He paid. I put it on Mickey's desk" stays, but the suspects (Cyril, Mickey, Ron) stay open until the kitchen. Then give two of the confessions to objects the player handles: Norman's lifts already sit in the fare book, so let the player find them there before Norman speaks; let Darren's coat be found in the spare car with paraffin on the cuff before the tape is shown.

**2. Too much story for a game that is not story-first, and a town where everyone is tied to the fire.**
What is wrong: some thirty-five named people, four books, two roads, three rivals, Kevin's copied sheets (raised three times), the Coultas brothers, Garbutt, Hal, Ellis's mother on the list, Danny's father on the list, Alison lodging above Ada, Tom's landlord being the man who bought the fire. Each link is clever. Together the Hook becomes a small world where every person turns out to be a strand in one knot.
Where: sections 4, 5 and 7 throughout; section 0, items 3, 12 and 13.
Why a player would feel it: in a game where most minutes are fares and carries, a dense web of relations is held in a player's head across hours of driving. Reviewers punish "too much talk"; they also notice when coincidence does the plotting.
Fix: cut Kevin's sheets as a separate thread (the Widow's telephone call to Customs can rest on what Kevin says, not on a night raid on the gate hut); let Les Coultas appear once and resolve offstage; drop Ellis's mother from the list and keep Ivy as the Tannery Row door without making her Ellis's mother, or keep that and drop Alison lodging above Ada's. Each cut buys minutes for the scenes that matter.

**3. The midpoint twist is visible from the first Sunday.**
What is wrong: on the first Sunday Ada says, "Mickey found the men. I found the reason." On the Fifth she is at her window, lit by the burning car, with the tallyman behind her chair, and invites Tom in to order the retaliation she wants. Danny boasts before he knows a man was in the car. An attentive player will suspect Ada that night, and the tape will confirm rather than shock.
Where: section 6, Sunday; section 7, the midpoint, beats 3 and 4; scene 13.
Why a player would feel it: BAR punishes "a plot the player has seen before". The kindly widow who is the real power is already a familiar shape, and telling the player her method a week before she uses it makes the shape easy to see.
Fix: on the first Sunday she says only, "Last year a man on the row stopped paying, and forty-one would have gone without. Mickey saw to it." Hold "I found the reason" for the lock-up confrontation. On the Fifth, Ada comes out of her front door from the back of the house, not already at the glass; let Hal's tape be the first time the player sees she was watching before the fire.

**4. Act III is a checklist.**
What is wrong: in two to three hours the last act carries the inspector's letter, the rival's three nights, Reese's five days, Alison's proof, the kitchen, Ellis's trade, June's page, the phone box, every hook called in, Cyril at the landing, Ada's table and the rival in person. Every one of these is good. All of them on one Friday afternoon becomes a list of stops.
Where: section 8, "The week" and Friday.
Why a player would feel it: the commonest complaint about GTA V's story is a last act that hurries; a run of eight errands against a clock can feel like a fetch quest wearing the finale's clothes.
Fix: move June's page to Thursday morning (she comes to the office when the paper lands) and Cyril's landing to Thursday night on the way back from Sheila's. Keep Friday to four beats: the phone box and its calls, Ada's door, the rival on the pavement, Reese closing his notebook. Cut the rival's three nights to one.

**5. The rivals' frames are stock, and Agar most of all.**
What is wrong: a silver landlord with a marina, a hotel, a fifteen per cent loan called on New Year's Eve and an editor in his pocket is the docklands villain of a hundred dramas. The incorruptible sergeant with a file her inspector closed and the reporter told twice to drop it are close behind.
Where: section 5, Agar; section 4, Ellis and Alison.
Why a player would feel it: reviewers name familiar parts quickly, and a familiar villain makes the story feel thinner than it is.
Fix: give Agar something he is right about. The basin is dead; four hundred berths are jobs, and he has a list of the men he means to hire, with Ron's old gang on it. Let him make that case to Tom once, honestly, so that selling the yard to him is a real choice for the forty-one. Give Alison's editor a reason that is not corruption: the paper is losing money and the Board is its biggest advertiser. Let Ellis bend once, on the pavement outside Sheila's, so her trade costs her something too.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review

No, not yet. It is good and in places far better than good: Ron in the passenger seat, Sheila's fare book, Ivy and her daughter, and a Customs officer as the last opponent are things I would quote. But "a reason to play" needs a story that plays as cleanly as it reads, and this one is overplotted for the game around it. Its mystery is mostly confessed to Tom rather than dug out by him, its midpoint twist is signposted, and its last act tries to pay off everything on one Friday. Fix the first and third of those and I would change my answer.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron and Tom: the passenger seat, the three driving lessons (two funny, the third not), number nine on the quay wall, the Bishop story finished at last, the gate offered and the seat left empty, and the ending told by which vehicle Ron is in and whether he looks across.
2. The fare book as alibi and noose: Sheila's rule, the free Saturdays in her small upright hand, Reese reading one line aloud, the kitchen with the pen and the fire, and every ending closed by a line in that book above a line in Tom's Ledger.
3. Take or pay: the gift and the hook that look the same, paid off at the hearing, in the hooks called in on the last Friday and in a phone box where he can only ring people he once let give him something.
