# LEDGER: the story bible, version 7

20 November 2026. Canon outranks this file; nothing here is built; canon.md and RULINGS.md are untouched. Spoken lines and Ledger lines are samples of the voice, not a script.

How to read it. Section 0 is a list for the owner and gives away every turn of the story; a reader who wants to meet the story as a player would should skip it and come back to it last. Sections 1 to 5 set out the story and its people as the player first meets them. Sections 6 to 9 are the story as scenes, in the order it is played, saying at each stage what the player believes. Section 10 is what was true all along. Sections 11 to 15 are the side stories, the retold scene, the tone, the pay-offs and the rules of play.

## 0. Changes to canon or rulings, for his ruling

Each with its reason and the plain alternative.

1. Ada is the head of what is left of the outfit, and she is its most dangerous person. Canon has Ada as a widow at her window. Here she is Ada Jensen, widow of the man who founded the Hook's dockers' club; the envelope is made up on her table; and on Bonfire Night it is on her word, not Danny Cammack's, that the firm's own car is burned, to give Tom a reason to do what she wants done. Tom meets her as a neighbour, has her tea on the Wednesday, and learns whose table it is on the first Sunday night. It changes one ruling (1 October): she never goes to the police; stood up for her tea, she talks, and her talk brings Ellis. Alternative: Ada stays a neighbour, the tallyman is the last of the old firm, and Cammack's crew burn the car.
2. Whose outfit. Canon: Mickey left Tom "a half-dead criminal outfit". Here it is a club with three seats (the gate, the row, the office); Mickey held the office, and Tom inherits that seat, its book and its debts. Alternative: the outfit is somebody else's and Mickey was its courier.
3. Maureen Jensen is Ada's daughter-in-law; her husband Alan, Ada's son, held the gate and drowned in 1986 while the men he had robbed, and his wife, stood still. Alternative: no relation; an accident nobody speaks of.
4. The man at the landing is named in week one: Cyril Blenkin, the tallyman.
5. The warehouse fire. The ruling of 30 September stands as what the outfit believes: the importer stopped paying rent and was burned out as a lesson. Added underneath, and dug up by the player across the middle act: the importer never stopped paying. Mickey owed Geoffrey Agar £4,000 on a handshake, promised him the importer's plot, told Ada the man had refused, and wrote that lie in his own real book. Ellis's file was closed as undetermined; it is the street that blames the owner. Added too: the date (Saturday 18 November 1989); Mickey drove the two men himself; the run is in the fare book because Sheila wrote it there, and she has waited a year for somebody to read it; the importer appears once, on the anniversary. Alternative: the fire exactly as ruled, known in full at the midpoint.
6. Mickey's heart stands as ruled. Added: he wrote Tom's letter the night before; Sheila posted it, to put a reader in his chair. Her promise to size up the heir stands and gains a second reason. The stranger Ron argued with in the yard was one of the fire's two men, back for money.
7. More people speak of Mickey than the ruling of 30 September allows (regulars only, two approved lines).
8. Sheila's name for him has two steps: "Mr Nowak" when she trusts him with the real book, and "Tom" once, later, for something she finds out for herself. Alternative: one step, as built.
9. Tom's line (QUESTIONS 2): no hands on a woman, anyone of sixty or over, or the priest. Adopted as recommended. Added: other people offer to do it for him, and whether he lets them is his choice.
10. Tom's letter has words. His Ledger is an old notebook with its first pages cut out, and its entries are in his own dry voice. Canon's rule holds throughout: nobody asks about his past and he never tells.
11. One love interest, not two. Alison Sedman is the someone worth being honest for, and his partner in digging up the fire. Philip Danby, canon's second love interest, is written as a friend: the last master of the closed school, minding an empty building; nothing he says touches pupils. Alternative: Philip can stand in Alison's place.
12. DS Ellis cannot be bought; her mother's name is in the club's book; the fire is her own file, kept open after her inspector closed it.
13. June Suddaby has nursed nights at the infirmary for eleven years and has stayed on Quay Street since the funeral.
14. The inspection has causes: the registration changed hands on Mickey's death; Tom's own wash; and a rival's telephone call. Mr Reese reads 1988 to 1990 as well.
15. Danny Cammack buys the Hook's other cab firm and competes on fares; he burns nothing. The act ends at one table with all three rivals, in Keith Garbutt's shop, where the outline had a sit-down with one head.
16. Burn Both is shown as two closes. Three endings shown, five states beneath (QUESTIONS 1).
17. Small things minted: the fare books for 1988 and 1989 are kept at Sheila's house; Agar's agents hold the parade's freehold; Friday is wages as well as rent; the minimum fare is £2; Remembrance is kept at the dockside memorial; Tom's licence application is heard by the council's licensing sub-committee on Tuesday 30 October; Ron cannot drive; the names Walter and Alan Jensen, Les and Ray Coultas, Frank Cammack, Ivy Leadley, T. A. Reese.

## 1. The spine in a paragraph

In October 1990 Tom Nowak, a stranger with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter from an uncle he never met, takes over a minicab office in a port whose docks have died. The letter says: people are owed. The office turns out to be one seat in an old dockers' club that still pays forty-one families a little money every week, by means that were always crooked and ended last year in a warehouse fire nobody will explain. For two months Tom, with an old docker in his passenger seat, a bookkeeper who will not use his name and a reporter who will not let the fire go, does two things at once. He decides what a debt like that is worth: keep the forty-one paid and become the port's man, one racket at a time, or shut the book and build something lawful in its place, while a widow, a ruined gentleman and a local lad each try to take the street from him. And he digs, with his own hands, for who burned the warehouse and why. Then a mild man from Customs and Excise opens the fare book, in which Mickey wrote down every fare he ever drove, and the plainest book in the office turns out to be the only one that never lied. What the books hold, what the street believes and whether anyone still says his name with warmth decide whether Tom ends as the keeper of the Hook, a man with a name and a life, or a stranger with a suitcase again.

## 2. The theme

Every fare goes in the book. LEDGER is about what a place owes its people once the official books have written them off, and who gets to keep the account.

- The dock labour scheme ended in July 1989. Nobody in the game explains why. Everybody lives after it.
- The club's book is the old answer: forty-one names, paid weekly out of theft, rent taken by fear, and one fire.
- Each rival is another answer for the same port. The Widow: work for those who can, nothing for those who cannot. Agar: sell the quay to people with boats. Cammack: undercut it, buy it, and laugh.
- The town keeps its own account of Tom and reads it back to him as a name: the new owner, Nowak, Tom, Tommy. To be known is to be owed and to owe.
- There are four books in the story (the fare book, Mickey's real book, the debt book, Tom's own Ledger) and a man from the state who asks which of them is true.

The story never says which answer is right. It makes every answer cost a named person something the player can see.

## 3. Tom Nowak

Tom is 32, the son of a Polish post-war settler and Mickey's sister. He has never been to the Hook. His life before it is unsaid: nobody asks and he never tells. He is not voiced.

The letter. In his hand in the first shot and pasted inside the cover of his Ledger:

"Tom. You don't know me. I was your mother's brother. I have a cab office on Quay Street in Meridian, two cars, and a good woman who keeps the books. It is yours when I am gone, and the doctor says that is sooner than I would like. There is more to it than cabs. There is a book. People are owed. Come and look before you say yes. Ask Sheila for the book when she is ready to give it. If you cannot stomach it, shut the door, and nobody will blame you, least of all me. Every fare goes in the book. M. Suddaby. P.S. Don't let them call you Mickey's nephew for ever."

His voice. Tom speaks aloud only in the words the player types or picks. Who he is lives in the Ledger, his notebook: a used one, hard-backed, with the first thirty pages cut out close to the spine with a blade. Nobody remarks on it. Page one is Monday 15 October. Every entry is his, short and dry, and he is funnier on paper than he lets anyone see:

- "Paid Dawn Pybus £38. Counted it twice in front of her. Don't know why."
- "Ron says rain by four. Ron has said rain by four every day. It has rained by four every day."
- "Sheila: 'new management' x6 today. A record."
- "Grace would not take money for the tea. Left it under the saucer. She brought it back out to the pavement."
- After the Fifth: "Terry's hands. Ron's sleeve. Ada said Tom. Nothing else tonight."

Entries are written from what the player did, so the notebook is a record of choices, not a commentary laid over them.

What he wants. A place that is his, and his own name said by people who mean it. The letter's postscript is the want. Each act gives it a job. Week one: make Friday, and learn what "people are owed" means. The middle: do right by the forty-one, by paying them or by building something lawful in the club's place; and find out what was done at the warehouse in his uncle's name, because he is the one whose name is on the roof now. The end: keep the book from drowning everyone in it. One standing choice makes the want visible: Shaun Hewson is repainting the parade's boards, and wants to know what Mickey's is to say.

His flaw. He lets nobody give him anything. He pays for tea that was on the house, counts money twice in front of people, carries alone what two could carry. It looks like pride and it is fear, and it is why nobody can get near him. Ron sees it by the first Thursday: "You never ask for owt, do you. Mickey was the same. Dead at a desk with the kettle on."

The flaw is played, and it has no safe answer. All through the game people offer Tom things for nothing, and each is one press: take it, or pay for it. Some are what they look like: Grace's tea, Ron's supper on the quay wall, Terry's flask on a cold rank, Alison's umbrella, Philip's spare chess set, June's needle for a split lip, Sheila's sandwich pushed across the counter without a word. Some have a hook in them, and look the same: Danny's fifty-pound note, the Widow's lift to the gate, Agar's offer to forget a week's rent, Garbutt's free read, Ada's tea. Nothing marks which is which, and the kinds do not sort by friend and enemy: the Widow, of all people, pays Terry Hodgson's wages while he is in hospital and wants nothing for it, and the deepest hook in the game is a pot of tea. To take is to owe. A man who takes nothing is owed nothing and known by nobody: at the hearing on 30 October only people he has let do him a turn feel entitled to stand up for him, and on the last Friday only they will hear him out. A man who takes from everybody is everybody's: each hook is called in, on that same Friday, at the worst hour. Paying his way is not nothing either: Sheila respects it. What the player learns, by getting it wrong once or twice, is the difference between a gift and a reason.

What he needs. To learn that a name is a debt: to be known is to owe and be owed.

His line. He lays hands on no woman, nobody of sixty or over, and not the priest. The game never offers him the blow. It offers him other people's hands. Ron, of Agar: "He's sixty-one, boss. I'm fifty-eight. I'm allowed." Danny Cammack, of Ada, near the end: "You can't touch her. I can." Whether Tom lets another man do what he will not is his choice, and the town remembers it as his.

What he fears. The suitcase: being nobody again, moving on with what he can carry. It stays packed under the bed in Mickey's flat until the player unpacks it, one press, whenever he likes. Sheila notices the day he does ("You've unpacked"), and the flat stops being Mickey's.

How each ending changes him. Each close ends on two lines: one Sheila writes in the fare book and one Tom writes in the Ledger.

- The Kingdom: he becomes the keeper, Mickey and Ada in one coat. His last entries read like Mickey's: initials and sums.
- The Straight Life: he becomes a man with a name and no kingdom. His last entry is about somebody else.
- Burn Both: he becomes what he was on the gangway. In one close the page is torn out; in the other it is left blank.

## 4. The people, as the player meets them

Fewer people, met more often. What each seems to be and plainly wants; what each is hiding is in section 10.

- Sheila Dunn, 53, the bookkeeper, at Mickey's since she was 22. Exactly as written: "New management", the walk-round, the fare-book rule, the locked door, the Sunday question. She wants the office to outlive Mickey and the fare book kept true. She is sizing him up, as she promised.
- Ron Kirby, 58, a docker for thirty years, on the door and the rank since the scheme ended. He wants to be of use the way he was when he had a hook and a gang. He calls Tom "boss", brings the asks, collected the row's rent on Mondays for twenty years, cannot drive, and from the first Thursday rides in the passenger seat unless told not to.
- Alison Sedman, 31, the paper's port reporter, lodging above Ada's since August. She wants the warehouse fire, right, under her name. Her editor, who sits on the Harbour Board, wants it dropped or blamed on the man who left. She never burns a source: true things told to her are kept, and lies she finds out become the story. She is quick, funny, and always slightly ashamed of her notebook. Tom has one too.
- Ada, 74, the widow at her window. Exactly as written: the window, one invitation, "I don't ask twice, mind". She is good to him before she has any call to be: when Ellis is on the street on the first Thursday, Ada says from her step that the new owner was sat in her front room till half past ten on Wednesday, whether he was or not. On the Friday round Ron hands her an envelope like the others: "Walter Jensen's widow."
- Cyril Blenkin, 63, the tallyman at the landing. "Mickey's?" "Right. Wednesday, same again." He counts an envelope with his thumb, eyes on the water. He wants one thing: the tally right. He writes the far bank's figures into Mickey's real book himself, in a hand taught at the same school as Mickey's.
- June Suddaby, 34, Mickey's daughter, a night sister at the infirmary. She trained and worked inland for years and came back to Meridian four years ago without telling her father where she lived. She has taken a room at the boarding house since the funeral, "wanting nothing from the office", and has not said why she stays.
- Father Brendan Walsh, the parish priest. He buried Mickey and counted who came.
- Darren Milner, 25, who sells nothing anyone can name, sleeps in the firm's spare car, and wants more than anything to be somebody's man.
- Pauline Kitching, 44, on the radio: the firm's running voice, heard in every cab at once. Kevin Needham, days, who wants his own plate.
- Terry Hodgson, 52, the night driver: four words a shift, always the right four. He drove nights for Mickey for twenty years and saw nothing, on purpose. On Tom's first cold night on the rank he passes his flask through the window without a word. Pauline has spent nine years trying to get a fifth word out of him on air and keeps a tally.
- Philip Danby, 36, the last master of the closed school at the hill end of the Hook, kept on to mind the building and list its library before it is sold. A chessboard, a paraffin heater, a dry mouth. A friend, if Tom lets him be one.
- DS Carol Ellis, 44, born two streets from the warehouse row. The fire was her file; her inspector closed it in the spring as undetermined; she has not stopped. She asks while a thing is answerable and tells once it is not.
- Keith Garbutt, the Fixer: a moustache, a car coat and a percentage. He carries messages between the three rivals and takes no side.
- Six regulars carry the town between them and are met again and again: Grace Rinaldi at the caff, Brenda Laverack at the fish shop, Lily Tang at the takeaway, Edith Stockill at the pension counter, Norman Dent on the warehouse row, Winifred Duggleby at the chapel flowers.
- Mr T. A. Reese, 57, Officer of Customs and Excise, who comes at the end: a flask of tea, a bad knee, a propelling pencil. His father drove a cab. He is sorry, truly, when a book does not add up. He is the first person in the story who only wants to know what happened.

### Ron and Tom

The friendship of the game, written for the hands. Sheila judges Tom; Ron keeps him company.

- The passenger seat. Ron cannot drive: "Never needed to. The work was at the end of the street." So he rides. He names every building by what it was ("That was Stannard's ropewalk. That was the Seamen's Mission. That's still a wall"), and the player learns the map as a list of things that shut. He forecasts rain. He has a name for every man in his old gang (Tapper, Shiner, the Bishop) and a story for none of them that he will finish.
- The lessons. On Sunday mornings on the empty dock road Tom teaches a fifty-eight-year-old docker to drive car two. Three lessons across the game, a few minutes each: Ron at the wheel, the player beside him with his voice and the handbrake. Lesson one ends on a bollard. Lesson two, Ron drives the length of the dock at eleven miles an hour, talking the whole way, and asks to go round again. In lesson two he has a provisional licence and L-plates, and Pauline tells every cab to keep off the dock road. Lesson three comes after the midpoint and is not funny.
- Fridays. After the round, two of number nine from the Tangs', eaten on the quay wall. Ron pays. If Tom lets him, it is the first thing in the game he has let anyone give him.
- What it builds to. The Widow offers Ron the gate: a foreman again, his old gang under him. He tells Tom himself, at the wheel, and asks what he thinks. The best thing for Ron may be to let him go. Either way the seat is empty for a while, and Pauline says so on air: "Car one, you on your own?"
- How it pays off. In every ending Ron is in a vehicle. Whose it is, which seat he is in, and whether he looks across as it passes, is the verdict on the friendship.

### Alison and Tom

The someone worth being honest for, and the other half of the dig. Three scenes before the midpoint supper, each with something to do: her fare to the warehouse row; the row at low tide; a night watching the watchman. One true thing is traded each way in each. She keeps two drawers, "person I like" and "story I'm chasing", and hates it when something moves between them. Everything Tom tells her truly is safe and costs her to keep. Everything he hides and she finds goes in the other drawer. After the midpoint she has a choice of her own to make, and asks him to help her make it (scene 17). What her paper prints in the last week is the sum of it, and every ending says whether she stayed.

## 5. The rivals

Three kinds of threat, kept: the Widow goes for his people, Agar for his clean money, Cammack for his cover. Each wants something of his own, each has a page in Mickey's real book, and each comes at him a different way. Their peaks come in turn, so the middle has a shape: paper first (Agar's hearing), then the street (Cammack's yellow cars), then his people (the Widow, after the midpoint), then all three at one table; and whichever of them is left hurt and standing is in the last week with hands as well as a telephone.

### Danny Cammack: his cover, by beating him at his own trade

26, born three streets from the office. Loud on purpose; quicker than he lets on. His money comes from things other people sell for him that the game never shows.

- Want: the Hook, and the end of the old lot. "You've got the keys. I've got the accent. One of us is in the wrong house."
- Why it is personal: his father Frank, a registered docker, had his hand crushed in 1981 and was struck off the club in 1984 on Alan Jensen's say-so, as a man who had talked. Danny spent his twenties watching his father wait at the window on Fridays for an envelope that had stopped.
- How he comes: not with a brick. The first Saturday he is on Quay Street buying chips and talking to Tom like an old friend. The next week he buys Akers Cars, the Hook's other firm, two cars, and has Shaun Hewson paint them yellow with HOOK CARS down the side and a telephone number anyone can remember. He cuts the minimum fare to £1.50. He gives his drivers pagers. He takes a Mickey's cab for the pleasure of it, has Tom drive him round the estate he grew up on, and pays a two-pound fare with a fifty-pound note the float cannot change: "Keep it. Call it rent." By the end of October his number is on the freephone in the ferry terminal and Pauline's phone is ringing less. Then he starts a hardship fund of his own: five pounds a week, a pound more than the club, to any docker's family that will put its name on his list instead. He is beating Tom, and the club, fairly, in daylight, with dirty money, and the street likes him for it.
- The turn: on Bonfire Night car two is burned, and the whole Hook knows who did it. The next morning he is outside the infirmary with an envelope for Terry Hodgson that June will not take. "That wasn't me. That wasn't mine. I'd not have said Terry." Nobody believes him, because nobody would. He is telling the truth.
- His page, and what it does: "F. Cammack struck off, A.J.'s word. Wrong man. Paid him since from my own." Mickey paid Frank quietly for six years, and Frank took it and hid it, and let his son hate the office that was feeding them. The page does not heal Danny. It takes away the one true thing he had (section 7, scene 19).

### Maureen Jensen, the Widow: his people, by offers

50, broad-shouldered, dressed like a harbour master. She took the gate when her husband drowned and ran it better. Since the scheme ended she is the port's labour contractor, registered and lawful: sixty men stand at the dock gate at six each morning and she picks twenty.

- Want: every job at the gate through her hands, and the club's book closed. "A pension for men who'll never work is a tax on men who do." Eleven men on the list could still work, and will not sign with her while the four pounds comes. She wants Mickey's as the gate's cab firm.
- Why it is personal: Ada is her mother-in-law, and they have not spoken since the funeral in 1986. "I buried a husband and kept his business. His mother buried a business and kept her window."
- How she comes: she keeps every deal, so her ladder is a run of offers, each fair by her arithmetic, each worse than the last, each put once. She never threatens. She prices. A voice that is not Pauline's on the open radio: "Tell Mickey's nephew the gate sends its regards." A dawn fare to the gate: "That's the scheme now. Me." Fifty pence on every fare through the gate. Then the list: work for the eleven, the social for the rest. Then Ron. Last: Mickey's becomes the gate's firm, and he keeps the name on the roof and a wage.

### Geoffrey Agar: his clean money, by the calendar, and then by other men's hands

61, silver, unhurried, the third generation of a family that holds paper on half the port. He never raises his voice and apologises before every blow.

- Want: the yard behind Mickey's, which carries the only right of way to the old basin, where he means to put four hundred berths and a hotel. "They will call it Meridian Quays. I voted against the name."
- What ruins him is the marina: borrowed against at fifteen per cent, and called in on the thirty-first of December unless the basin is his to sell. What can finish him is in Mickey's book: he sat and voted on a sale to his own company, and took a tenant's rent every Monday knowing whose it was.
- Why it is personal: he is Tom's landlord; the £38 for the office and flat goes to his agents every Friday. And Mickey owed him: "Mickey and I had an understanding. I regret that I understood it differently. He wrote everything down, you know. It was his one vice."
- How he comes: by letters, each with a date in it, so the player always knows what is coming and when. A rent review, "with regret". An objection to Tom's licence, heard on Tuesday 30 October. A solicitor's letter to June, offering to fund a claim on her father's estate. When paper has failed he pays for hands: his surveyor, Mr Lyle, a polite young man with a clipboard, and four men in the jackets of a security firm, at night, with bolt cutters, at the yard gate and Mickey's mooring. They are under sixty and they are not women.
- His own scene: Tom takes the rent to the Harbour Board, where Agar keeps a room, and is left waiting outside a glass door. Inside, a younger man from a bank is saying the thirty-first of December in an ordinary voice, three times. Agar comes out, sees who has been sitting there, apologises for the wait, and offers to forget this week's rent. Take it, or pay.

## 6. Act I: The Inheritance

Monday 15 to Sunday 21 October 1990. About two hours. The most scripted act. The beats of the approved first week stand; what is new is marked.

### Monday: the arrival and the first night

1. The ferry (0:00). First image, new: the letter in his hand over grey water, the last line readable: "Every fare goes in the book." Then the landing. Nobody meets him. Twenty seconds, skippable. He has £23.50.
2. Quay Street (1:30). A line each, in passing, as written: Darren, friendly and owing; Ron, "boss"; Ada at her window; June. New second half for June: "I don't want anything out of that office. I'd check what you're getting, if I were you."
3. Sheila's walk-round (1:45). Her six lines as approved. "New management." The fare book: "Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule, and it's mine now."
4. The first fare (2:30). Father Walsh, from the infirmary to his house by the chapel, Pauline giving the way by landmarks. New, Sheila's first test: she takes a driver's badge from the drawer. "Mickey's. Wear it till the council sits." If the player asks whether that is allowed: "No. Mickey wouldn't have asked." The priest, in the back: "Your uncle put a roof on my chapel twice and never once sat under it." And at his door: "You missed a good funeral. Forty-one envelopes on the plate, and every one of them empty. They wanted him to know they'd come."
5. The rest of the office (4:30). The locked door ("Not today"), the flat, the debt book: five names, £156. New: the sum bites. Friday is rent, £38, and five wage packets. Mickey's bank account is frozen until probate. An honest week's takings just cover the wages. The rent is the gap, and one night's carry is exactly the gap.
6. The first deed (5:30). Darren's £22: front him up, take what he offers, strike it out. Somebody sees.
7. The first ask (7:00). Ron, the coat, the envelope, as approved. New, for the player who types the obvious: "Who's it for?" "Tallyman. Counted cargo on that quay forty year. Now he counts this, and he tells me what's what. That's all I'll say on a pavement, boss."
8. The carry (9:00). As written and as section 12 finishes it.
9. The landing (11:30). "Mickey's?" The thumb, the water. "Right. Wednesday, same again. And tell Kirby the round's twenty this week, not twenty-one." A thin packet comes back and Ron counts £15 out of it for Tom: the carrier's money. Home by the yard. The tin. Sleep. First Ledger page.

### Tuesday to Thursday

The approved beats, in order. Tuesday: Darren says the night to Tom's face in words that fit what he did; the Ledger opens; the first count with Sheila; fares with a wrinkle; Wayne Harrison's £30, paid in goods at the pawn's back room; Lee Drewery's £15 on the quay, where he stands and people stop to watch. Wednesday: Ada's one invitation ("There'll be a pot on at nine tonight, if you want it. I don't ask twice, mind"); Julie Kettlewell's £9, mercy or shame; Ron with the second envelope at eight; the tea against the landing, with her window on the street. Thursday: both nights come back; somebody says "Nowak"; Dennis Iveson's £80, three ways in; Ellis on the street if it is loud; two dockers waiting by car two.

Added or changed:

- Grace Rinaldi will not take money for his first tea. Take it or pay: the first of the offers the game counts. Terry's flask, on Tuesday night's rank, is the second.
- Ada on Tuesday morning: "What were you doing in my alley?" A neighbour's question.
- "How did Mickey die?" has an answer worth hearing. Sheila: "At that desk, at ten to six, with the kettle on and the book open. His heart. He'd been told. Ron found him at six. If you're after a story there isn't one. There's a man who didn't rest."
- Darren, the first time Tom asks anyone about it: "You're not going to go round asking who did for him, are you? Because nobody did. He just stopped. Like the docks."
- Alison in the cab on Wednesday, to the warehouse row. She does not mention a cab or an outfit. "The watchman swore he walked his rounds. The brigade's times say nobody was on that row for the best part of an hour. I'd like to know where an hour went." It is raining when she gets out and she leaves her umbrella on the back seat. Give it back, or keep it till she comes for it.
- A letter from the council on Thursday: his own application for an operator's and a driver's licence will be heard on Tuesday 30 October, and an objection has been lodged.
- Thursday night, the two dockers by car two: the first time Ron rides. Afterwards, in the car: "You never ask for owt, do you."

### Friday: rent, wages and the round (new)

- Morning. Dawn Pybus with her clipboard, apologising. If he cannot pay she says who she collects for: Mr Agar's agents.
- Noon. Wages by hand from the drawer: Pauline, Kevin, Terry, Ron. Each takes the packet with a line. Sheila will not take hers until the others are paid. If he is short he chooses who waits, and that person remembers.
- Night five, the third ask. Not an envelope: a holdall. The Hook's half of the club goes round by hand on Fridays, twenty doors, and Ron walks it. "You'll want to see where it goes, boss. Or you won't. Mickey always walked it." A carry with faces: Winifred Duggleby; Albert Suggitt, mending a net on his step; Ada, at her own front door, who takes hers like the rest ("Walter Jensen's widow," Ron says, going up the step) and asks why there are twenty and not twenty-one ("Tallyman says twenty, missus. Winifred Duggleby's to wait a week." She shuts the door); a door on Tannery Row where Ron says "Knock soft. Her girl's police"; and one door where Ron takes the packet himself: "Not that one. That's Mickey's own." The name on it is Cammack. Then two of number nine on the quay wall. Choice: walk it, send Ron alone, or refuse it. What the player now believes: the envelope is a pension for old dockers, the exact old man at the landing sees to it, and Ada is on the list.
- If he said no on Monday: Ron walks a short round alone, and can be seen on the wall afterwards with one portion.

### Saturday: the match (new)

The street fills at three and at five. Danny Cammack is on it, loud, friendly, buying chips. The boxing club behind the chandler's is open. In a drawer in the flat upstairs there is a bundle of letters in Mickey's hand, stamped, addressed to June at a hospital inland, and never posted. Give them to her unopened, read them first, or leave them where they are.

### Sunday: the first lesson, the question, the passenger

- Early. The empty dock road, car two, Ron at the wheel for the first time. The bollard.
- Ten o'clock. Sheila comes in on her day off, as written. "I don't come in Sundays." If she trusts him: "Mr Nowak." It is the first time she has used his name, and the real book is on the counter, shut. "That's Mickey's real book. Everything he ran, in his own hand. You've had your week. So which is it going to be?" If not, the day-book, and "new management" still. Wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say; asked twice, as built. The street learns the answer. She puts the book back behind the locked door either way: "When you've earned the inside of it."
- Night seven, the fourth ask: a passenger. If the arrangement stands, the tallyman is at the rank after ten and gets into Tom's cab. "Warehouse row. The far end." At the burnt plot he stands a while. "Your uncle stood here with me the morning after. He said, that's the last one, Cyril. I told him it wasn't his to say." Then: "Forty-one go out. What comes back keeps the forty-one going out. That's all it ever was. Blenkin. You may as well have the name. Now Quay Street. The terrace with the lamp out." He goes in at the back gate without knocking. On Ada's table there are forty-one small envelopes in rows, and she is filling them with pound coins from a tin. "You've had my tea. Now you know what it was for. I pay myself the same as the rest. Walter would have wanted the book straight. Last year a man on the row stopped paying, and I said the row wanted a lesson or forty-one would go without. Mickey found the men. I found the reason. He never forgave me, and he never stopped carrying."
- If Tom has ended it, the passenger is June, late for her shift. "You've stopped Winifred Duggleby's coal money. Did anybody tell you that was what you were saying no to?" A pause. "He never could. I'm not saying you were wrong."

At the end of week one every player knows what the envelope is, has stood on the doorsteps or refused to, and has answered Sheila knowing what the answer costs. What he believes: an old club, run from a front room across the street by the woman who gave him tea and an alibi, burned a warehouse last year. The tea was an interview, and he passed it or did not. He has two questions and the middle act is the chase after both: how to do right by forty-one people, and what happened on the warehouse row.

## 7. Act II: The Rise and the Squeeze

Monday 22 October to Sunday 2 December. Ten to thirteen hours. Scenes fire on states, never on sequence, with two exceptions the town itself names: the calendar's days (Bonfire Night, Remembrance Sunday, the fire's anniversary) and dates that arrive in letters. Those happen whether Tom is ready or not, so a slow player is pulled forward.

The act is a chase after two things at once, and both are always in the Ledger's Open list.

- The debt. Forty-one people are owed. On the keeper's road (K, for the player who took it over or would not say) that means finding £164 a week, and the ladder has five rungs. Each is a named person's work, each has a turn, and each is attacked by a rival:
  1. The ferry's freephone and a third car. Whichever firm's number is on the telephone in the terminal gets the boat's fares. Julie Kettlewell drives the car. Turn: she is good, the regulars ask for her, and Danny offers her a yellow car and a pager.
  2. The parade's rent. Lee Drewery collects; Lily Tang pays (scene 9). Turn: Lee likes it too much.
  3. A front to wash through: a share in the launderette. Turn: its oldest hand tells the caff the machines have never been so busy on paper, and Agar's agents, who own the building, put up its rent.
  4. The cold-store contract. Turn: the Widow's stoppage (scene 6); kept, it makes him her customer.
  5. A man of his own at the landing, so that the envelope no longer needs Tom. Turn: the only one who wants the job is Darren. On the straight road (S, for the player who wound it down) it means building a lawful society to replace the club, and the ladder is three rungs, each won with the hands and attacked by somebody. Edith Stockill does the forms; Father Walsh, June and Philip Danby are the trustees.
  1. A room: the closed school's hall. Agar is buying the building (scene 15).
  2. Money in a bank: the mooring (scene 9), with the Widow's men behind him.
  3. The names. Ada has the list, and will not give it; and Danny's fund is buying them at five pounds a head.
- The dig. What happened on the warehouse row. Three jobs, done with Alison. A player can leave it alone; then the last act digs it up for him, in front of a Customs officer.

What a week is, between the scenes. Days are fares, and every third fare is somebody from the Friday round or somebody who wants to know about them. Dusk is a job: a rung to win or to hold. Night is the count with Sheila. Friday is wages, rent and the round, and it is the week's test: either forty-one envelopes are full or the player chooses whose is not, and on Monday that person is a fare. One door on the round is different every week: somebody has died, somebody has signed with the gate, somebody's daughter answers and says he is not to have it any more. After each of the town's dates the week changes: after the hearing every stop by a constable is a different risk; after the Fifth there is a car fewer; after Remembrance the gate is hiring his men; after the anniversary Ellis is on the street every day.

Where a scene differs by road, both are written.

### First half: can he make it his?

Scene 1. Monday's wages. The office, before the first call. Pauline: "Am I still wanted, then?" Kevin and Terry are listening and Sheila is not looking up. Choice: what he tells them, and whether it is true. By noon the gate has it word for word. (S) That evening June gets into his cab for her shift and says the thing this road is built on: "It doesn't have to be shut. It has to be lawful. There are friendly societies older than the docks. You'd want a room, money in a bank and the names. You've got none of them." It goes in the Ledger as three lines.

Scene 2. The long way round. Kevin's day fares come in short against his mileage; Sheila sees it in the book before anyone says it. Followed, he is carrying parcels for the gate in Mickey's car, on a promise: the Widow will get him a plate of his own. Fronted on the rank, in front of the street: "Twelve years I've driven his car. She asked me what I wanted. Nobody here ever did." Choice: sack him there; keep him and feed the gate what Tom wants it to hear; take a cut; or pay the gap and say nothing. Comes back: the radio leak is a weapon, his or hers. Kevin has copied a year of drivers' sheets for the Widow, and getting them back out of the gate hut is a night's work on the docks, now or before the inspector asks.

Scene 3 (K). The ferry's freephone. Danny's number is on it, at £1.50 a ride. Get Mickey's number back on that wall by a word with the ticket clerk, by matching the price and bleeding, or by going in at night with a screwdriver. The turn: every fare he takes off the yellow cars is one more reason the street likes Danny.

Scene 3 (S). The names. The society needs forty-one people to say they want it, and Tom has no list, so he goes to the doors he knows from the round and asks. Albert Suggitt, seventy-eight, mending nets: "I had my bit off Walter's tin before you were thought of. I'll not sign a form to have it." Door after door says the same. The first person in the Hook to put his name down is Frank Cammack, left-handed, slowly. "Don't tell our Danny."

Scene 4. The dig, one: the row at low tide. A Sunday. Alison asks him to walk the warehouse row with her, because a man with Mickey's name on his roof can stand there without being stared at. They go over the burnt plot together. She shows him the two places it started. Norman Dent's torch comes along the row and she steps behind Tom. She quotes back, exactly, what he said in the cab in week one, then asks him one true thing about Mickey's nights, and gives one of her own. He comes away with two things in her writing. The brigade's times: an hour with nobody on the row. And a line copied from the Harbour Board's minutes, which Edith Stockill kept for thirty years: in March the Board let the importer sell the burnt plot, for a third of what the plot beside it fetched, to a company whose address is Mr Agar's agents. Her editor sits on that Board beside him and has told her twice to leave the fire alone. What the player now believes: Agar wanted the land.

Scene 5. The hearing, Tuesday 30 October. The council's licensing sub-committee, one room in the town hall, reached as a fare that leaves the Hook. Three members, a clerk, a public bench. The clerk reads "no record disclosed" and moves on. Agar's solicitor has made a written representation on fitness: the dead man's badge, every caution, every telling he has collected in a fortnight. The clerk reads it out, and then the references the applicant has handed in. Only people Tom has let do him a turn have felt able to write one: Grace, Brenda, Edith in fountain pen, Father Walsh. They are sitting behind him. Anyone he has wronged has written too. If he has paid his way with everybody, the clerk says "No references," and the silence is the scene. Tom is asked one question and answers once. Outcomes: licensed; licensed with conditions; or refused, after which every fare is a risk and every stop a search. None ends the game.

Scene 6. The gate at six, and the stoppage. The Widow books a car herself and offers to sit in front: "Save you turning round." A lift he is giving her, and she will call it one she gave him. The engine running, sixty men, twenty picked. Her first offer, put once. Refused, or left three days, it is answered for him: her men stand across the cold-store road at half past five, and she stands in front of them herself, hands in her coat pockets, a yard from his bonnet. A woman, in front of his car. He can edge the packing shift forward until she steps aside or does not; send Ron to talk to his old gang; pay the fifty pence; or lose the shift.

Scene 7. The second lesson, and a hook. Sunday, the dock road, L-plates: Ron drives its whole length at eleven miles an hour, talking all the way, and asks to go round again. That night one hook is called in early, so the player learns what a hook is: Danny, who is owed for a fifty-pound note, would like a parcel run across town, no questions.

Scene 8. A man at the counter. One evening Pauline looks up at a stranger in a wet car coat. "I'm after Mickey." "He's dead, love. There's a nephew." "Then the nephew owes me. Coultas. He'll know the name soon enough." Sheila, seeing him, puts her pen down: it is the man Ron put out of the yard two nights before Mickey died. Tom can pay him, front him, or follow him. Followed, he walks to the last ferry and stands a while at the far end of the warehouse row on his way, looking at the burnt plot the way a man looks at his own work. The ticket clerk knows him by sight: he comes over every few months, and Mickey used to meet him off the boat. What the player now believes: the club hired a man from over the water, and Mickey was the one who paid him.

Scene 9 (K). The rent comes back. The far bank pays less every fortnight. Cyril, at the landing: "It doesn't add. It hasn't added since the gate stopped. The parade paid until last year. It can pay again." Tom, or Lee Drewery sent in his place, collects from the launderette, the chandler and the takeaway. Lily Tang pays without a word, and stops saying "Same as last week? I remember." That is the scene: a woman who liked him becoming polite.

Scene 9 (S). The mooring, and the money. Honest takings will never find £164 a week; the society needs capital. The yard is Mickey's own freehold, and with it goes a mooring that has never had a boat on it. Edith explains why: it carries the one right of way to the old basin. Agar's surveyor wants it. So does the Widow. Sold to either, on a contract that completes when probate is through, the deposit alone is a year of envelopes. Tom takes their bids in his own cab, an hour apart, sells to one, and then has to get the deposit from the yard to the bank, in daylight, on foot or by car, with the loser's men behind him: a carry with a tail. On Friday the takings are still £20 short of wages and rent, and before he has chosen who waits, Sheila has put two ten-pound notes from her own purse in the drawer and written "Loan, S. Dunn, £20." Let it stand, or pay her back.

Scene 10. The dig, two: watching the watchman. Saturday 3 November, the warehouse row, the cab parked with its light off, Alison in the passenger seat with a flask, Ron sent home. At half past eleven Norman Dent leaves his row, and is gone an hour. He does it every Saturday. Met at his gate, kindly, he tells the first half of it: his wife is ill and frightened at night, he goes home on his break, and he has let a detective think for a year that he was paid to be away, because the truth costs him the job and the cottage. Only to a man who has walked his round with him on an earlier night, and brought the flask, does he tell the rest: that autumn he did not walk. "One of Mickey's cabs used to run me, Saturdays. There and back, never a penny. That night and all. It was late fetching me back." Whose kindness it was he never asked. In his head: somebody in the club wanted the watchman off the row, and the club's watch is kept from a window on Quay Street.

Scene 11. The inside of it. Sunday 4 November. Sheila unlocks Mickey's office. (K) "You've earned the inside of it." (S) "You said a cab firm and nothing more. You'd better know what the nothing more was." He reads forty-one names in Mickey's hand, with the far bank's figures entered every fortnight in a second hand very like it. He reads, for the fourteenth of October last year, "Importer refused. Third week," and for the week of the fire, "The warehouse is settled. The fire settled what the rent couldn't." He reads a line from August: "The sister's lad. Nothing keeps him where he is. He'll come." The last written page has been cut out with a blade, close to the spine.

### The midpoint: the Fifth

Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990. A display on the quay for the town's grown people: a brazier the size of a van, fireworks off the old coal jetty, the one night the quay is full after dark.

1. The supper. Alison comes up to the flat for the first time. If the suitcase is still packed under the bed, she sees it. The windows flash. He tells her what was in the book, or does not. The day life is going well.
2. The door. Ron on the stairs, blood on his sleeve that is not his. "Boss. They've done car two." Under the fireworks it has been burned by the kerb under Ada's window. A week ago two of Danny's lads put the office window in, and Danny did tell them to do that; by morning one of them is telling the Tangs' queue what happens to firms that will not move over. Terry Hodgson was asleep across the back seat between fares. Ron pulled him out. Terry's hands are burned and he will not drive again. (S) Darren is behind Ron, marked about the face: they took the far bank's envelope off him in the crowd first, he says. Alison is standing behind Tom and sees and hears all of it.
3. The window. In the street the car is still alight and half the Hook has turned from the fireworks to watch it. Ada is at her window, lit by it, with the tallyman standing behind her chair. She is the only person on Quay Street who does not look surprised. Her door opens. "You'd better come in, Tom."
4. The order. Her front room, the pot, the envelopes. She does not raise her voice. "Danny Cammack is paying five pounds to my people to sign his list. Now he's burned a man of yours. He has a lock-up on the row. I want it answered before Sunday's names are read. Mickey wouldn't, after last year. You'll want to decide what you are." (S) "You said no to Mickey's arrangement and I let that stand. Now a man's hands are gone and a lad's been robbed carrying what you wouldn't. It's in that lock-up. Fetch it back before Sunday."

What is lost: Terry, for good. Car two. Whatever Alison thought he was.

What the player now believes: Danny Cammack has burned a car with a man in it, and the club that feeds forty-one people wants him to answer in kind. One of those is true.

Next morning, outside the infirmary, Danny with an envelope June will not take: "That wasn't me. That wasn't mine." From tonight Ada says "Tom", before Sheila ever has.

### Second half: whose book is it?

Scene 12. The ward. Wednesday, visiting hour. Ron drives Tom to the infirmary and back, L-plates on, without being told a thing. Ron's own hands are bandaged. Terry, in bed, hands bound, uses five words for the first time in the game: "Tell Pauline it don't count." June is the night sister. At her desk at the end of the ward, under the lamp, she is putting pound coins into three small envelopes of a kind the player knows, and does not hide them. Since the summer she has been making up three of the forty-one out of her wage. It is why she has not gone home. Agar's solicitor's letter, offering to fund her claim on her father's estate, is under the envelopes, unanswered. "Don't tell me what that makes me." On the dock road going back Ron stops the car properly, handbrake on, and finishes a story for the first time. It is the one about the Bishop, and it turns out to be about the night Alan Jensen went into the dock. "Seven of us stood there. I was nearest the ring. I've stood there every night since." Then: "She's offered me the gate, boss. His gate."

Scene 13. The lock-up, and the tape. The one job in the game that is planned like a job, with five days to plan it. A brick lock-up at the dead end of the row. Casing it from the parked cab gives the hours. Then the board in the office, and whom he takes: a key cut from one look at the padlock by Wayne Harrison at the pawn's back room; Norman's hour off the row on Saturday night; Lee Drewery for the door; a night's paint to make car three not a cab; Darren, who offers, too eagerly, to make a noise at the other end. In by the key, quietly; in by the roof light from the next building; or in by the door with Lee and Ron. Inside: crates of car radios, (S) the envelope on a shelf by the door where anybody would see it, and a can of paraffin, new, the only clean thing in the place.

In the same five days Hal Metcalfe, who keeps the coin shop that sells no coins and owns the one camcorder on Quay Street, asks Tom round to watch his film of the fireworks. Tea, biscuits arranged in advance. The tape shows the jetty, then swings across Quay Street: Ada and the tallyman already at her window, looking down at a car that is not yet on fire. Then somebody's back at car two, in a coat too big for him. Then four minutes of Hal's own feet.

It was Darren, and the coat is the one he carries the envelope in. Shown the tape, he tells all of it on the pavement in one breath. He was told a car, an empty car, by the kerb, and that after it he would have the landing for good. He did not look in the back. He has not slept since. (S) Nobody took the envelope off him; he was told to put it in the lock-up and say so. Ada, faced with it, does not lie: "A car. I said a car. Nobody said a man. You'd not have gone after Cammack for my sake. You'd go for a burned car. Everybody needs a reason."

So the lock-up is three different jobs. The player who goes on Saturday without having seen Hal burns an innocent rival's stock for a woman who maimed his driver, and learns it afterwards. The player who has seen the tape can go in for the envelope and leave the can; or not go at all; or carry the paraffin back across the Hook and set it on Ada's table. What he does about Darren, whether he tells Ron who lit the car Ron pulled Terry out of, and whether he tells Terry, are his to carry. Told, Terry asks to be driven to Quay Street, gets out by himself, and stands on the pavement in front of Ada's window with his hands in their bandages, saying nothing, for as long as she can bear it. She has no reason to give him. It is the only time in the game she leaves the glass. If the lock-up burns, the dial moves, and the first gun in the Hook is shown within the week.

What the player now knows: the head of his own club finds reasons for other people's hands. He goes back to last year's fire with that in mind.

Scene 14. Remembrance Sunday, 11 November. The dockside memorial. By the Hook's custom Father Walsh reads the names: the wars, and the men the docks have taken since. Alan Jensen. This year, Michael Suddaby. Everybody is in one place for the only time in the game: Ada, out of her house; Maureen across the square; Agar with the Harbour Board's wreath; Frank Cammack and his ruined hand, Danny at the back; Ellis beside her mother; Sheila; June; Alison with her notebook shut; Darren, grey. Two minutes of silence in which the player chooses only where Tom looks, and sees who looks back. At Alan Jensen's name six men of Ron's age look at the ground, and Ron is one of them. Afterwards, at his kitchen table, Father Walsh says the one thing he may: "Your uncle asked me, sat there, whether a man can repent of a thing and go on paying for it out of the profits. I told him no. He kept paying. I've not decided which of us was right."

Scene 15 (K). The lesson. The chandler stops paying the parade's rent and says why, in his own doorway, to Lee, with customers in the shop. Ada: "The row wants a lesson." The player has heard that sentence before and now knows how it works. Its size is his to decide: a window put in by hand; a beating sent and not watched; a fire; or the chandler's share paid quietly out of his own tin, so that nobody learns a man refused and nothing happened. Each is remembered. The last is found by Sheila in the count.

Scene 15 (S). The hall. The night before the society's first meeting, men in the jackets of a security firm come to the closed school with a van, to clear "the vendor's property" a week early. Tom and Philip hold a building: doors, a corridor, sixty stacked chairs, a history master who has never hit anybody and is surprisingly willing. In the morning the hall has its chairs out and one cracked window. Thirty come. Then Ada comes, on Cyril's arm, and speaks in public for the only time: "A club is a thing you're in. A charity is a thing done to you. I'll not be means-tested by Mickey's nephew." Half the room walks out behind her. Four rungs are standing and the list has just left the building.

Scene 16. The gate, Ron, and two widows. The Widow's second and third offers. The choice from scene 12 comes due, and it is put kindly: tell Ron to take the gate (he goes as a friend); ask him to stay (he stays, and Tom has to make that worth a man's last working years); or tell him he owes it to Mickey (he stays, and something between them is over). When Tom goes down to the gate to say it, one of her men is being unpleasant to an old docker who is not Ron. Ron, quietly: "Say the word, boss."

The Widow's last offer needs an answer from the club, and the club is not Tom. That night Ada asks to be driven. She has not been in a car since 1986. "The dock gate." Ron is in the front, because he is always in the front. Maureen gets in at the gate. Two widows in the back of a minicab on the dock road in the dark, the meter running, Tom's eyes in the mirror. They talk about the list. Then Maureen asks what she has had four years to ask. "Cyril gave you his tally on the Monday. Alan went in on the Saturday." Ada: "I didn't put him in. I showed the men what he'd had off them. Then the water did what the water does." Maureen: "Ask the man in your front seat what the water did." Ron does not turn round. "We stood there, missus. You and all." Maureen: "It's the only thing his mother and I ever did together." Tom may speak once. What he says, and what he has done with the list, decides whether they make a peace, a war or a sale. Maureen pays what is on the meter, to the penny. Ron gets out with her, or does not.

Scene 17. The dig, three: the anniversary. Sunday 18 November, a year to the day. At the burnt plot, in the afternoon, a man in a good coat is standing where his office was. He has come over for the day. He does not give his name to strangers, and the Ledger does not write it down. Spoken to honestly, he takes a card from his wallet: his rent card, stamped PAID every Monday to the thirteenth of November 1989. No initials. "I paid. Every Monday, to the big fellow with the docker's hook in his belt. I told the old tallyman so afterwards, on that landing, and he looked at me as if I was begging. So I took what the insurers gave me and I went." The big fellow is Ron. In the real book: "Importer refused." Ron, asked, goes very still, and then says the thing he has not said for a year. "He paid. I put it on Mickey's desk every Monday. Then the word come round that he'd refused, and the row was to have a lesson, and I thought, that's not right. And I never said owt. Twenty year you don't ask Mickey." Somebody between that desk and that page kept the money and wrote a lie: the tallyman, who writes in that book and heard the man out and sent him away; Mickey; or Ron, who is the only one saying so. The player has to decide whether to believe his friend before he can prove it.

That evening Alison's own choice comes due. Her editor wants "One year on: the owner's fire" for Thursday or her desk cleared. She can file it, file a holding piece and keep the real story for when it can be stood up, or walk out. She has the brigade's times, the minute, and whatever Tom has told her. She asks him, as the only person in Meridian whose answer she will take, what she should print about his uncle. Whatever he says, she remembers that he was asked.

Scene 18. The night of the book, and the search. Alison's piece, whichever she filed, has put the fire back on every counter, and Ellis has used it. Pauline, on air, to every cab: "Car three, the sergeant's been at the magistrates' all afternoon and come out with a piece of paper, so I'd tidy your glovebox." The real book cannot be in the office tomorrow. A carry across the Hook with the heaviest thing he has had under the coat, and where he takes it is whom he trusts: Ada's table; the school library, among ten thousand others (Philip will not open it); the room above Ada's (Alison will read every page and print none of it without him); or the counter, where Ellis will find it. Monday evening: Ellis and two constables in the office, Sheila standing, the drivers on the pavement, the street watching through the glass.

Scene 19. Danny. If Tom burned the lock-up, Danny is now owed, and knows it, and so does the street once the tape is seen. However it stands between them, it ends on the page. Shown Mickey's line about his father, Danny reads it twice and goes to the door on Tannery Row, and through the window the player sees a young man standing over an old one who does not deny anything. After that Danny has no grudge and no father. If Tom has dealt straight with him, he comes to the office and offers what he has: "You can't touch her. I can. She sat at that window six years and watched my dad wait for nothing." Said yes to, he puts her window in at noon and stands in the road telling the street why, and she never sits at it again; and the Hook counts it as Tom's doing. If not, he calls Tom out: Saturday 1 December, the old hall behind the chandler's, gloves on, nothing staked, the Hook on benches. In the third round one voice calls for the stranger. "Go on, Tommy." It is Frank Cammack. It is the first time anyone in the game has used that name, and Danny hears who said it.

Scene 20. Garbutt's table. The act ends in the back of Hal's coin shop, lent for an evening, because it is the one room in the Hook nobody owns. Keith Garbutt has brought all three: Agar, who has never sat at a table with either of the others; the Widow; Danny. Tom has what each wants about the other two. The Widow wants the minute and Agar's pages, to make the basin's labour hers. Agar wants Cyril's tally of what Alan Jensen took, to move the Widow's men off the basin. Danny wants one page, and to see the old lot finished. Each also wants something of Tom's: the yard, the drivers, the street. The pages are on the table as paper: he can tear one out and slide it across, sell one, show one and put it back, or leave with all three in his coat. Whoever leaves that room hurt and standing picks up a telephone the next morning. (S) The same table, with cleaner things to trade: the right of way, the cold-store contract, a society that can take eleven men off the Widow's hands lawfully.

Scene 21. Agar in the rain. Later that night Geoffrey Agar walks down Quay Street for the first time in his life, without a hat, and knocks at the office. He does not threaten. He asks for his pages. Pushed with what the dig has found, he says his part, and it is short: "Your uncle came to me last September and said that plot could come free, and would that settle it. I said it would. When it came free I found I could not afford to mean it. I have a wife who believes we are selling the house to be nearer her sister." He is the one living man who can be faced for the warehouse. Give him his pages; keep them and have his vote on the Harbour Board while he has one; take them to Ellis; or let Ron see him to his car.

## 8. Act III: The Ledger Comes Due

Monday 3 to Saturday 15 December. Two to three hours. Reese by day; the last truth found on the Thursday night, with a pen in reach; and a Friday that is run, not watched, and ends face to face.

### Why the inspector comes

1. The name on the form changed. When a registered trader dies, Customs and Excise look at the business before they register the next owner. Every player gets the letter, whatever he built.
2. Tom's own wash: real money from an unreal source, a reason to set fuel and mileage against the book and, if they do not fit, to pass a copy along the corridor to people who are not polite.
3. A telephone call from whichever rival left Garbutt's table hurt and standing: Agar about this year's takings and the dead man's badge, the Widow about the drivers' sheets Kevin copied, Cammack about a man seen carrying cash to the landing. Reese's first questions show which. That rival is in the week with hands as well.

The letter comes on Monday 3 December and names Monday the 10th. It is courteous. It is signed T. A. Reese. By Tuesday it is street talk. The fare books for 1988 and 1989 have always been at Sheila's house; "the cabinet's damp". Asked for them now, she says: "He'll want those. I'll bring them in myself, the day he asks." It is the only time in the game she keeps a book out of Tom's hands.

### The week

Monday. Reese at the counter with the fare book since October and the drivers' sheets: Tom's own wash, read aloud by a mild man with a pencil. Whatever cannot be put in front of him he asks for "in writing, by five on Friday". The firm is above the VAT line only because of its account work, and he says, mildly, what he is trained to look for: "A fare at no charge is either a kindness or a hole, Mr Nowak. I'm paid to ask which." The street watches through the glass. The nights of this week are the town's: fares, the count, whoever comes to the door.

Tuesday. Fuel and mileage. "I always ride a route or two. It's not in the manual. Mileage is a truthful thing." Reese rides in car one all morning with his flask, and Tom drives (section 13).

Wednesday. The account customers, 1988 to 1990. Sheila has brought the old books from her own house in a shopping bag, and stays standing while Reese reads. He goes quiet twice, makes two small marks, and says only: "I'll take the quarter to December 1989 away with me tomorrow, if you'd be so kind." Evening: Alison, with the proof of tomorrow's page, against every rule she has. It is what she chose on the anniversary, tested against everything since.

Thursday. The paper is on every counter by eight. Reese turns to Saturday 18 November 1989 and reads one line aloud: twenty to midnight, car two, landing to warehouse row and return, two fares, wait, no charge. "Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak." Sheila is looking at the counter. He puts a slip of paper in at the page. "I'll want it open at that place at nine tomorrow." The book goes home with her in the shopping bag, as it always has.

Thursday night: Sheila's kitchen. Tom has never been to her house and has to find out where it is, and get there with Ellis's people watching the office. She lets him in without surprise. The 1989 book is on the kitchen table with the slip in it, and for the first time it is in the player's own hands, to turn page by page:

- Every Monday of that autumn to the thirteenth of November, in Mickey's hand: "Importer a/c. Paid." The man never refused.
- Every Monday from October, in the same hand: "Office to Harbour Board and return. No charge." The importer's rent, going to Agar.
- Every Saturday night from the second of September, eleven weeks before the fire: "Row to Dent's cottage and return. No charge. Per M." The watchman's lifts, in Sheila's small upright hand, as she was told.
- And Saturday 18 November: the line Reese read. It is not in Mickey's hand. It is small and upright, entered on the Monday after, squeezed in under the day's last fare.

Mickey planned the fire in September, moved the watchman with a kindness, took a paying man's rent to the week he burned him, and told Ada the man had refused so that she would find the reason. He did to Ada what Ada did to Tom. And he never wrote his own run down. Sheila, at the sink, with her back to him: car two had forty miles on it that nobody had written. She asked him, and he told her. He would not write it, so she did, because every fare goes in the book and that was his rule. "I booked those Saturdays myself and never asked. And if I'd gone to the police there'd have been no envelopes by Christmas. So I wrote it down and I waited for somebody to read it. Nobody reads a fare book." There is a pen on the table and a fire in the grate. The player decides what happens to the line: leave it; alter it, for a man who notices ink; or put the book on the fire, after which the books cannot hold. She does not ask him to do any of them, and does not turn round.

On the pavement outside, Ellis, who followed him. Her trade is honest: a name and the proof, and at nine she takes the 1989 book as evidence in her own file, so that it leaves Reese's hands lawfully and his visit is about this year alone; the price is that the street learns who talks. He can refuse, and still reach the best ending through what the town believes. He can put her mother's name to her; she answers by offering to hand the file up to people who know nobody in the Hook.

Friday. Before Reese arrives Sheila puts a folded page on the counter. Mickey cut it from his own book the night he died and left it beside the letter. She has never read it. She posted the letter. "I wanted somebody in that chair who'd look." The page is not a confession. It is an account, in Mickey's hand: the importer's name, the town inland where he went, and a sum. Stock. Fittings. Eleven weeks' rent taken before. £11,400. "Owed. Not paid." The forty-second name, and the first.

Then the run. Reese has the office from nine, and the telephone in it is the one everybody hears. He wants three things in writing by five: a mileage record for the cars (Ron has kept one in a pocket book since his first lesson, "to have something to do with my hands"); the books of wherever the money went, which Edith keeps; and an answer for the eighteenth of November 1989. Tom has the phone box on the corner, five coins and the afternoon.

- He can ring only people he once let give him something. Each call that is answered is an errand across the Hook, driven or run. Terry, who cannot hold a telephone, answers his with his elbow, and comes.
- Every hook he took is called in now: whoever he owes is standing by the box, or rings it, with one thing they want done before five. So is Les Coultas, if he was never settled.
- The answer for 1989 is his alone. Mickey's page, on Reese's counter, is the truth: it clears Tom, names what the estate owes, and takes Mickey's name, Agar, and perhaps Ada with it. Sheila will say what she wrote and why, if he lets her. Or he says he does not know who drove.
- The last errand is Ada's door. Edith's books do not balance without the tin, and the tin is on her table. This is where he tells her what Mickey did to her, or does not (section 9).
- The rival who telephoned Customs is the last thing between him and the office door, in person. Agar, on the pavement with his surveyor and a padlock for the rent, asking once more. The Widow, in the back of a car that is not his, with Kevin driving, naming her price for the last time. Danny, across the end of Quay Street with a yellow car and nothing left to lose. It is settled there, with what Tom knows or with his hands or by walking round, and then he goes in.

Reese is not moved by who likes Tom. He reads what is in front of him. Then he closes his notebook. "That all seems to be in order, Mr Nowak." Or: "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak." Neither comes from nowhere: all week the Ledger's reading has said, in Tom's words, what is at risk and why, never what will happen.

For the straight player the week has its own stakes. He washed nothing, and Mickey's three years can still sink him: the mooring's sale cannot complete while the estate is in question, and a firm Customs will not register cannot trade. His clean books for 1990 are the proof that he is not his uncle, and for him the page on the counter is the only honest way through.

## 9. The endings

States, never a menu. Three shown, five beneath, in the built order. Each has Ada's last scene, a last scene of its own, a line that says who he became, the fate of everyone who worked for him, where the truth has come to rest, and a last image: a line written in the fare book, and under it a line in Tom's Ledger.

### Ada's table, in every ending

The last errand of the Friday run: her front room, the tin on the table. He can tell her what Mickey did, or keep it. Told, she sits a long time. "Thirty-seven years I've known when a man was lying to me from that window. He sat in that chair." Then: "He found me a reason." If Terry has stood at her window, the chair is already turned from the glass. By what the player is to her, she gives him the tin; or she puts it away; or she asks him to leave, and stands at the glass until he has gone. Not told, she will die believing last year's fire was hers. Either is a thing he chose. So is what he tells June about her father, and whether the importer is ever paid.

### The Kingdom

Reached when the business stands and the books hold. Terry is on the radio beside Pauline, wearing a headset he cannot put on himself, using more words than he ever has.

The last scene. Saturday 15 December, after ten, the landing under the customs lamp. A carrier in a dark coat that does not fit him walks the envelope down the lit quay past the constable, who looks at the coat and looks away. It is Darren, if Tom kept him; a stranger, if he did not. The tallyman steps up and asks, for the first time: "Nowak's?" Across Quay Street Ada's window is dark and her chair is gone from it: she gave him the tin, and the watch with it. The camera climbs to the flat above the office. A figure at the glass. Tom has the watch.

Below, a cab comes in off the rank, and Ron gets out of the passenger side, as he always has, and stands by the door. Sheila locks the office; or, if he scratched or burned the line she wrote, she is not there, and her keys are on the counter on top of the fare book. On the plain turn she says, "Goodnight, Mr Nowak," and has given her notice for the end of the quarter; June has gone back across town; Alison's desk at the paper is empty, or she is still at it and no longer rings; the forty-one are paid by rounds and fronts with nothing of a club left in them; Agar votes as he is told for as long as he has a vote; Les is across the water on a wage. Ada, earlier, from her back room: "Mickey kept the book. My Walter kept the street. You'll do for both." He has learned to find people reasons. Mickey's page is in his coat; whether the forty-second name is ever paid is up to him, out of the tin.

On the better turn (the built Both: one friend counts him, the street does not hold his rackets as fact, Ellis's case is answerable, the books hold and make sense), Sheila stops at the door. "Night, Tom." A careful first player can reach it. It is hard, not hidden.

Last image. The fare book: "22.10. Landing. Account." The Ledger, in a hand that has started to look like Mickey's: "41 out. T.N." If the importer was paid, a second line: "42."

### The Straight Life

Reached when no business of his stands at the close and one friendship holds.

The better turn (the built Quiet: handed to someone who can hold it, never while hunted for a killing). Friday, the school hall. The Quay Street Friendly Society pays out for the first time, in the open. Edith has the pen. "Sign there, dear. And there." Forty-one signatures in a book anybody may read. Whether Ada's is the first of them, or missing, is what happened at her table. Agar is in Ellis's hands or gone inland; the Widow has her eleven and has shaken on the rest; Les has given his statement or gone; the paper has printed what happened on the warehouse row, under Alison's name, and she is on the back row of the hall with her notebook shut. Then the three days of watching, as built: Julie Kettlewell has the firm, Terry has the night desk and the radio, and the board and the rank hold without Tom or do not. On the last evening he gets into the back of car one. Julie is driving. Ron is in the front passenger seat, by habit. "He's coming for the ride," she says. "Where to?" "Just round." Pauline, on air: "Car one's got the old gaffer and Ron. Be nice." At the office Sheila writes the fare herself. "Mind how you go, Tom."

Last image. The fare book: "Nowak, T. Round the Hook. £2. Paid." The Ledger: "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four."

The plain turn (never built, or sold up). The board is painted out. Sheila clears a desk of thirty-one years and gives him the last fare book: "It's a full one. I'd not throw it out." The forty-one are the parish's, or the Widow's eleven. He stands at the landing in daylight with the suitcase and whoever held (Alison, with her own case, if it was her; Ron, if it was Ron), and the ferry goes out without him. Somebody passing says, "Morning, Tom." The fare book's last line: "Closed." The Ledger: "Stayed."

### Burn Both

Reached when nothing else fits. Two closes.

The Reckoning, for the man who went for both and lost them. Friday: "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak." Ellis has stopped asking. Sheila is asked for her keys by a constable half her age and hands them over without looking at Tom. Ron is on the pavement with his coat on and nowhere to be. The paper's front page has his name in it, under hers. By Saturday Agar's agents have a padlock on the door for the rent. Saturday night is the first night again: the quay after ten, the lamp, no envelope. The constable turns round. He does not call "You there." He calls, "Nowak!" The top of the name ladder from the wrong side: everybody knows who he is. The player has the two inputs of night one, stand or run, and the close cuts on the press. After the credits the town is the Hook on the morning he is bailed: the padlock, the forty-one unpaid, Ada's window lit and nobody at it. The fare book: Sheila closes it unwritten and puts it in the drawer with Mickey's. The Ledger: the last page cut out, as the first thirty were.

The Suitcase, for the man who let it drift. No judgement, because nobody thinks enough of him to hold one. A grey afternoon. He walks down the gangway he walked up. Darren asks him for a pound. The gate's van passes with Ron in the passenger seat, and Ron does not look across. Ada watches from her window the whole way, as she did when he came. The fare book: "Ferry landing. One. No charge." The same charge as the line for the night of the fire. The Ledger: a date, and nothing under it.

### What every ending settles

Where the line Sheila wrote is, and whether she is still at the counter beside it; who holds the forty-one, and whether the forty-second was paid; who still has work (Sheila, Ron, Pauline, the drivers, Terry); what became of Darren; where each rival and Les Coultas stand; whether Alison stayed, and what she printed; who knows what Mickey did, and what Ada did; and what Ada is doing at ten at night. No ending redeems what was done. Reloading is never stopped.

## 10. What was true all along

For the builders: the truth in the order the player can find it. The street never has it. The street says the importer burned his own warehouse and went south, and that Danny Cammack burned a cab.

1. Week one: the club. Walter Jensen's sick club of 1953 grew a second purse under the dock scheme: cargo short-tallied at the gate, held on the row, moved in cabs; a weekly "rent" from the row and the quay. Three seats: the gate, the row and the watch, the office and the book. When the scheme ended the gate stopped paying in and forty-one names were left, at £4 a week.
2. The first Sunday: the table is Ada's, and has been since 1979. She sold the house on the hill long ago to keep the club paying, and rents the terrace from Agar's agents.
3. Scene 13: how she works. She never does a thing; she finds somebody a reason. She had the firm's own car burned by a boy who wanted the landing. She did not know Terry was in it.
4. Scenes 12 and 16: Alan. Cyril gave her his tally of what her son had taken; she showed it to his gang; on the Saturday six men and his wife stood still.
5. Sheila's kitchen: Mickey. For a year he had kept the forty-one paid with £4,000 borrowed from Agar on a handshake. In September 1989 he went over the water himself and hired the Coultas brothers, then told Agar the importer's plot could come free, and Agar said that would settle it. He had Sheila send a cab for the watchman every Saturday; went on taking the importer's rent from Ron and paid it to Agar as interest; wrote in the real book that it had stopped; told Ada the man had refused; drove the two men himself, and wrote none of it down. He needed her word because a fire that was the club's would be covered by the club's silence, and a fire that was his own would not. Terry drove the watchman home on those Saturdays and was given the night of the eighteenth off. The tallyman heard the importer say he had paid, sent him away, and told nobody: he took Mickey's word. Agar kept the debt. Mickey told the priest, told Ada it was the last, paid the two men twice more, reckoned what he owed the importer, and sent for a stranger.
6. Thursday night and Friday: Sheila. Mickey never wrote his own run down. She found the mileage, asked, was told, and wrote it in the fare book herself against his word, because every fare goes in the book; then waited a year for a reader, and when he died with a sealed letter by his hand she posted it to get one. "New management" was never only a promise to Mickey. Why the nephew: June would have shut the book the day she got it, and Mickey could neither shut it nor hand it to her. Whether he meant the letter sent, nobody can know.

## 11. Side stories that are whole stories

The thirty-two drafts of 8 November stay as the short ones, found on foot. Five are grown here into whole stories: a person with a want, a turn the player does not see coming, and an end the town keeps. Each runs across acts, brings its people back, and touches the main story without being needed by it.

1. The watch (Ron). Found: Mickey's watch in Rita's window the week he died. Rita will not say who pawned it until it is redeemed; Hal knows; there is a florist's bill. Turn: Ron pawned it himself, for the funeral flowers, because the office was frozen and he would not ask Sheila. He wanted Mickey to have had a proper send-off from somebody. Ends: redeemed and handed back without a word; asked about; or left in the window. The town keeps: what kind of boss Tom is to a proud man.

2. A ride for the sergeant (Carol Ellis, Ivy Leadley). Found: rain, night, the sergeant's own car dead by the fish market, and Pauline, dry: "It's the police. She wants a car." She gives an address on Tannery Row: the door Ron said to knock soft at. After that Ivy Leadley, seventy-one, books Mickey's every Sunday for chapel and asks for "the new one". Turn: Ivy knows exactly what her four pounds is. She has told her daughter for years that it comes from the chapel, because her daughter would stop it; and her daughter, who is nobody's fool, has chosen to believe her. Each is protecting the other from a thing both know. Ends: Tom keeps Ivy's confidence and drives her on Sundays; tells Ellis; or lets Ivy know that he knows, and is asked in. The town keeps: nothing, for once. It is the one story in the game that stays between three people.

3. One way (Karen Grummitt). Light. The ferry's ticket clerk wants a car at five on Thursday, with her case, and not a word to Mrs Rennison, whom she owes a fortnight. The case is a case, a wardrobe and a budgerigar. A cab with a wardrobe roped to the roof must pass Sylvia Rennison's front step, where Sylvia sits in her slippers from four. Turn: Karen does not get on the boat. She stands at the rail she sells tickets for and cannot do it. Ends: he drives it all back, past the step again, and Sylvia says only "Friday means Friday"; or pays the fortnight and Karen goes; or tells Sylvia. The town keeps: the wardrobe, twice, and a postcard that comes to the ticket window or does not.

4. Two of number nine (the Tangs). Lily remembers Mickey's Friday order. Peter says the shouting at closing time is nothing, as he always does. Steps: be at the counter at closing, three nights; the young men are Danny's hangers-on. Turn: Danny stops it himself, in a sentence, the night he sees Tom standing there, then tells the street he did. "My dad ate here before you could find it on a map." Ends: let him have the credit; say who stood there first; or it was Ron. On the keeper's road it crosses scene 9: the man standing at her counter at closing is also the man whose collector calls on Monday, and Lily knows it. The town keeps: who the Hook thinks protects it. Racism here is a fact about the men, never voiced, never rewarded.

5. Lilies (Winifred Duggleby). The chapel flowers; her husband's anniversary; "nobody gives lilies any more". Turn: in a short week she tells him, not knowing who he is in it, that "the bit from the club" did not come, that she has gone without before and will not make a song of it. Ends: he makes it up from his own tin and does not write it down (Sheila finds it in the count; this is one of the acts that earns her "Tom"); he does not; or he tells her whose envelope it is. The town keeps: a kindness told after the service, or a woman who now knows what her coal money is made of.

## 12. The scene in the first half hour a player retells

The carry, minute nine, kept as written: three routes with honest costs (the lit quay, the dark yard and lane, the cab with the firm's name on its roof), a constable who can turn round, and next morning Ada asking what he was doing in her alley and Darren saying it to his face.

Finished here: if the constable takes the envelope. He calls out; Tom stands; the constable opens it. The player sees his face, then a glimpse: small packets, a name pencilled on each. He is honest, so he takes it to the station, and Ellis has it by morning. Nobody comes for Tom with a hammer. Sheila, at the count: "Twenty people over the water are short this week. You don't know what that means yet." And in Mickey's debt book, under the five names, she writes a sixth in her own hand: "Nowak, T. £80." The new owner's first week ends with his own name among the debtors. A failed carry is the fastest way in the game to learn what the envelope is.

The second retold scene is the tea, minute thirty-nine, kept exactly. On a second playthrough it is a different scene.

## 13. Humour and the mix of tones

Grounded noir carries the main line. The comedy is dry, local and attached to people, with seaside-postcard cheek used sparingly. With no drink in the game, the pub's jobs are shared out.

- The radio is the bar. Pauline's commentary goes to every cab at once and is the game's running joke and its chorus. "Car one, the chapel, and the lady says not the way you went last time." "Kevin, wherever you are, you are not in Copper Row, because I can hear the gulls." Her nine-year campaign to get a fifth word out of Terry is kept as a tally on the office wall.
- The caff is the snug: Grace bars, forgives and feeds, and Brenda does the postcard lines. "Thirty years with my hands in cold fish. My Derek says it shows."
- Tom's Ledger is the straight man. "Mrs Rennison booked a car to go forty yards with a ham. Asked no questions. The ham had a seat." Alison reads over his shoulder once and laughs out loud, and it is the first time anyone in the Hook has found him funny.
- Ron's driving lessons are the game's physical comedy, and they are allowed to stop being funny.
- Darren is the clown with a cost. Forty transistor radios off the back of a lorry, every one of which picks up only the pirate station, which is playing one record all week. By Thursday the whole parade can hum it.
- Three set pieces built for laughing. Karen's wardrobe (side story 3). Pauline's hour: she takes her first lunch out in nine years and Tom has the radio; the drivers test him at once, and Sheila corrects every call he makes without looking up from the book. And Mr Reese's ride-along: Brenda gets in with a box of live crabs that does not stay shut ("Are those declared, Mrs Laverack?" "They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in"), Darren tries to sell a Customs officer a radio, and everything that happens is perfectly legal.

The darkest scenes (Ada's order, Hal's tape, the two widows, Agar in the rain, Sheila's kitchen) are played quiet and never joked across. The lightest sit in daylight, in the weeks before the Fifth, so the game has somewhere to fall from.

## 15. How play carries it

- Hands first. Every scene is a fare, a carry, a round, a tail, a break-in, a fight, a drive, a lesson, a search or a count, with talk where the consequence lands. The last week is two nights of jobs and a Friday run behind five mornings of paper.
- Nothing is on rails. Scenes fire on states; the calendar's days and the letters' dates belong to the town. Every turn of the truth can be found early: Ron at dusk on an ask night comes out of one of six back gates with the coat on his arm; Hal's tape can be watched the morning after the Fifth; Ron will finish the story of 1986 for a man who has ridden with him; this year's fare book is on the counter from the first day, and a player who reads Mickey's last months in it finds the Monday runs to the Harbour Board still being written in September.
- No game over. A lost envelope is a debt. A lost fight is Grace's kitchen. A refused licence is a harder game. Tom cannot die; a car hurts and never kills; nobody is killed by the story.
- Guns are rare and late and come only through people: the dial first moves if the lock-up burns.
- No drink, no gambling, no children, anywhere; nothing is staked on the bout; nobody speaks of real people or real news.
- What is asked of the systems is plain. The hearing: one written line per speaker, chosen by what the town's memory holds. The offers: one prompt with two answers, and a list of who gave. Two passengers: a written scene with one live choice. The ride-along: a morning of fares with a fixed passenger. The Friday run: written calls that open ordinary fetch-and-carry jobs on a clock. The lessons: the car's own handling with the roles swapped.
