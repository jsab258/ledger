# LEDGER: the story bible, phase 2, version 2

The Passenger. 20 November 2026. Canon outranks this file; nothing here is built; canon.md and RULINGS.md are untouched. Spoken lines are samples of the voice.

How to read it. Section 0 is a list for the owner and gives away every turn; a reader who wants to meet the story as a player would should read it last. Sections 1 to 5 are the story and its people as first met. Sections 6 to 9 are the story as scenes, in the order played. Section 10 is what was true all along. Sections 11 to 16 are the wind-down player's story, the side stories, the retold scene, the tone, the pay-offs and the rules of play with the budget.

## 0. Changes to canon or rulings, for his ruling

Each with its reason and the plain alternative.

1. The warehouse fire. The ruling of 30 September stands as the street's version (the owner burned his own warehouse and went south) and "the truth is never the street's". The truth here is new: on Saturday 18 November 1989 three old dockers went into the importer's warehouse to lift a consignment of cigarettes for the dockers' club, a lamp went over, and the car that took them was one of Mickey's, driven by Ron Kirby, who has no licence. Nobody paid for a fire and nobody meant one. Alternative: the fire as phase 1 had it.
2. The outfit. Canon: Mickey left "a half-dead criminal outfit". Here it is an old dockers' sick club that pays forty-one families £4 a week, fed for thirty years by what Ron's gang lifted at the dock gate and moved in Mickey's cabs, and since the dock scheme ended in July 1989 by a weekly payment from the warehouse row (about £90) and by Mickey, who drew no wage and drove nights to find the rest. Nobody is its secret head.
3. Ada stays what canon says: a widow at her window. Minted: she is the widow of Walter Harker, who started the club in 1953, and she is on its list. This undoes phase 1's first proposed change.
4. Ron's old gang is minted: Tapper, Shiner and the Bishop. The Bishop was burned in the fire and Mickey paid his keep every Friday until he died.
5. One opponent and two pressures. Maureen Jensen, the Widow, is the story's main opponent; Danny Cammack and Geoffrey Agar are smaller, each with his own way of coming at Tom and one scene that is his. Canon gives the three equal standing.
6. One love interest, and small. Alison Sedman is the someone worth being honest for. Philip Danby, canon's second, is a regular. Keith Garbutt, the Fixer, only carries the Widow's offers.
7. Sheila's name for him has two steps: "Mr Nowak" when she trusts him with the real book, and "Tom" once, later, for something she finds out for herself.
8. Tom's line (QUESTIONS 2): no hands on a woman, anyone of sixty or over, or the priest. Adopted as recommended.
9. Tom's letter has words, and his Ledger is in his own dry voice.
10. The rule about his past is used once, and not as a trick. In the last act Tom can refuse to name the driver of a car on a night a year ago and answer for it himself as the owner. He tells no lie and nobody asks him anything. DS Ellis remarks that he is the one man on the street she can say nothing about. Alternative: she says nothing.
11. Mickey's first heart turn was on the night of the fire; he was on June's ward when his car went out, and a warning from the doctor is spoken of in week one. The ruling on his death stands unchanged.
12. The inspection has causes: the change of owner, Tom's own wash, and a telephone call from Agar.
13. Three endings shown, five states beneath (QUESTIONS 1); Burn Both has two closes.
14. Small things minted: the old fare books on the shelf; the hearing on Tuesday 30 October; the dockers' own fire at the gate on Bonfire Night; Agar's lease clause.

## 1. The spine in a paragraph

In October 1990 Tom Nowak, a stranger with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter from an uncle he never met, takes over a minicab office in a port whose docks have died. With it he gets an old docker who cannot drive and rides in his passenger seat. For three weeks the two of them work the Hook from one car, and on Sunday mornings Ron learns to drive at eleven miles an hour. Then, on Bonfire Night, he is standing at the dock gate in a foreman's coat with another firm's name on the back. The port's labour contractor, a widow who never threatens and prices everything, has taken him the way she takes everyone: she asked him what he wanted. The rest of the game is the player finding out, with his hands, what was done with one of Mickey's cars on the night of last year's warehouse fire, why a man who would not cross the road for money sold himself, and who he was afraid would find out. Then a mild man from Customs and Excise reads two lines in the fare book and wants a driver's name. What the books hold, what is written on the paper and who is in which seat decide how Tom ends.

## 2. The theme

You never ask for owt. LEDGER is about what people owe each other in a place the official books have written off, and about how much of it goes unpaid because nobody will ask.

- The club's book is the old answer: forty-one names, paid weekly out of thirty years of theft.
- The Widow is the new answer: work for those who can, what the state allows for those who cannot.
- Tom takes nothing and asks nothing. So does Ron. The story's one real crime was a theft that went wrong; its lasting damage was done by a year of good mornings.

The story never says which answer is right. It makes every answer cost a named person something the player can see.

## 3. Tom Nowak

Tom is 32, the son of a Polish post-war settler and Mickey's sister. He has never been to the Hook. His life before it is unsaid: nobody asks and he never tells. He is not voiced.

The letter. In his hand in the first shot and pasted inside the cover of his Ledger:

"Tom. You don't know me. I was your mother's brother. I have a cab office on Quay Street in Meridian, two cars, and a good woman who keeps the books. It is yours when I am gone, and the doctor says that is sooner than I would like. There is more to it than cabs. There is a book. People are owed. Come and look before you say yes. There is a man on the door called Kirby. Keep him if he'll stay. If you cannot stomach it, shut the door, and nobody will blame you, least of all me. Every fare goes in the book. M. Suddaby. P.S. Don't let them call you Mickey's nephew for ever."

His voice. Tom speaks aloud only in the words the player types or picks. Who he is lives in the Ledger, his notebook: a used one, hard-backed, with the first thirty pages cut out close to the spine. Every entry is short, dry and written from what the player did.

- "Mrs Rennison booked a car to go forty yards with a ham. Asked no questions. The ham had a seat."
- After the Fifth: "Car one. On my own."

What he wants. A place that is his, and his own name said by people who mean it. The letter's postscript is the want.

His flaw. He lets nobody give him anything, and he asks nobody for anything: not a favour, not a reason, not what they want. He pays for tea that was on the house and carries alone what two could carry. It looks like pride and it is fear: a man who owes nothing and is owed nothing can pick up his suitcase.

The flaw is played, two ways.

- The offers. All through the game people offer Tom things for nothing, and each is one press: take it, or pay for it. Some are gifts (Grace's tea, Terry's flask, Ron's supper) and some have a hook in them (Danny's fifty-pound note, the Widow's lift) and nothing marks which is which. To take is to owe. A man who takes nothing is owed nothing and known by nobody: at the hearing on 30 October only people he has let do him a turn feel entitled to write for him, and on the last Friday only they will open a door. A man who takes from everybody is everybody's.
- The question. From the second week the player can ask a person, in his own words, what they want. It is never a prompt and never free: a question asked is an answer owed, and some answers cannot be met. The story does not say this is the lesson. It lets the Widow do it first.

The load the flaw cannot carry. Ron is the same man. Two men who never ask sit eighteen inches apart for three weeks, and one of them is carrying something the other could lift if either would open his mouth.

His line. He lays hands on no woman, nobody of sixty or over, and not the priest. The game never offers him the blow.

What he fears. The suitcase. It stays packed under the bed in Mickey's flat until the player unpacks it, one press. Sheila notices the day he does: "You've unpacked."

His past. The game keeps canon's rule so strictly that the player stops noticing it. Ada says it for the street on the first Tuesday: "I'll not ask where you were before. Nobody on this street will. We've all been somewhere." In the last act it is the reason he can stand in a gap nobody else in the Hook could stand in: he is the one man there about whom nothing is known, so nothing can be said.

## 4. The people, as the player meets them

Twenty-one who matter. Nobody on this list is secretly anybody else. What each is carrying is in section 10.

- Ron Kirby, 58. A docker for thirty years, on the door and the rank since the scheme ended. He calls Tom "boss". He cannot drive: "Never needed to. The work was at the end of the street." He wants to be of use the way he was when he had a hook in his belt and a gang.
- Sheila Dunn, 53, the bookkeeper, at Mickey's since she was 22. Exactly as written: "New management", the walk-round, the fare-book rule, the locked door, the Sunday question. She is sizing him up, as she promised Mickey.
- Maureen Jensen, 50, the Widow. Broad-shouldered, dressed like a harbour master. Her husband ran the dock gate; she runs it better. Since the scheme ended she is the port's labour contractor, registered and lawful: sixty men stand at the gate at six and she picks twenty. She never threatens. She prices, she puts each offer once, and she has kept every deal she ever made.
- Tapper and Shiner, of Ron's old gang, on the Widow's gate now. Tapper says little. Shiner cannot stop.
- The Bishop, 60, of Ron's old gang, who is on nobody's gate. Ron's stories about him stop soonest.
- Pauline Kitching, 44, on the radio: the firm's running voice, heard in every cab at once. Nine years without a Saturday off.
- Terry Hodgson, 52, the night driver: four words a shift, always the right four. Pauline keeps a tally of her attempts to get a fifth out of him on air.
- Kevin Needham, days, twelve years in Mickey's car, who wants a plate of his own.
- Julie Kettlewell, laid off from the cold store, who can drive.
- Darren Milner, 25, who sells what he hears and wants to be somebody's man.
- Ada, 74, the widow at her window. Walter Harker's widow, on the club's list, lonely, sharp, the best witness on Quay Street. Her tea is tea.
- Cyril Blenkin, 63, the tallyman at the landing, who takes the far bank's half of the club over the water.
- June Suddaby, 34, Mickey's daughter, night sister at the infirmary. "I don't want anything out of that office. I'd check what you're getting, if I were you." And, of her father: "He had his warning a year ago. He took no notice of it. He was never a man you could tell."
- Father Brendan Walsh, who buried Mickey and counted who came.
- Danny Cammack, 26. Hook Cars, two yellow cars, £1.50 a ride, pagers for his drivers. Loud on purpose, quicker than he lets on.
- Geoffrey Agar, 61, the landlord: silver, unhurried, apologises before every blow. His agents hold the freehold of the parade and half the warehouse row.
- DS Carol Ellis, 44, born two streets from the row. The fire was her file; her inspector closed it as undetermined; she has not stopped. In the file is a docker's brass tally found in the ashes; the register that matched numbers to men went into a skip with the scheme.
- Alison Sedman, 31, the paper's port reporter. One question: where did an hour go on the row last November?
- Keith Garbutt, the Fixer, who carries the Widow's offers.
- Mr T. A. Reese, 57, Officer of Customs and Excise, who comes at the end: a flask of tea, a bad knee, a propelling pencil. He is sorry, truly, when a book does not add up.

### Ron and Tom

The friendship is the plot, and it is written for the hands.

- The passenger seat. From the first Thursday Ron rides unless told not to. He names every building by what it was ("That was Stannard's ropewalk. That was the Seamen's Mission. That's still a wall"), and the player learns the map as a list of things that shut. Five times in the game he starts a story about Tapper, Shiner or the Bishop, and each stops one sentence later than the last. The fifth is finished.
- The lessons. On Sunday mornings on the empty dock road Tom teaches a fifty-eight-year-old docker to drive car two. Ron has the wheel; the player has his voice and the handbrake. Ron sets the terms himself, getting in: "That's yours, boss. I go, you stop. That's the arrangement." Lesson one: if the player is quick, Ron is stopped a yard short of a bollard; if not, the bollard. Lesson two: he drives the whole dock at eleven miles an hour, talking, and asks to go round again. Lesson three comes late and is not funny.
- Fridays. After the round, the Tangs', and the quay wall. Ron buys three of number nine and puts one inside his coat. "Feller I know." Ron pays. If Tom lets him, it is the first thing in the game he has let anyone give him.
- How it pays off. In every ending Ron is in a vehicle. Whose it is, which seat he is in, and whether he looks across, is the verdict.

## 5. The opponent, and two pressures

### Maureen Jensen, the Widow: his people, by asking

- Want: every job at the gate through her hands; Mickey's as the gate's cab firm; and the old club's book shut. "A pension for men who'll never work is a tax on men who do." Eleven men on the club's list could still work, and will not sign with her while the four pounds comes.
- Why she is right: she has sixty men in work that did not exist two years ago, the club is dying anyway, and Ron is a better foreman than he ever was a doorman.
- Why she is dangerous: she breaks no law and no promise. She takes a firm apart by finding out what each of its people wants and offering exactly that, once.
- How she comes, four ways. Offers to his people, carried by Keith Garbutt. The gate's custom: fifty pence on every fare through it, then the cold-store shift run, given or withheld. Her own body: when he will not pay, her men stand across the cold-store road at half past five and she stands in front of them, hands in her coat pockets, and his line means he can do nothing but wait, pay or turn round. And the front seat: twice in the game she books car one and sits in Ron's place.
- Her rule, which the story never lets her break: she keeps every deal; she tells nothing unasked and no lie when asked straight; and she has never in her life spoken to a police officer about anyone, which every docker knows. The player can test it from the first morning at the gate: asked straight whether his day driver is carrying for her, she says, "Yes. You didn't ask before."

### Danny Cammack: his cover, in daylight

- Want: the Hook, and the end of the old lot. "You've got the keys. I've got the accent. One of us is in the wrong house."
- How he comes: not with a brick at first. He puts Hook Cars' number on the freephone in the ferry terminal. He rides in a Mickey's cab and pays a two-pound fare with a fifty: "Keep it. Call it rent." He takes whatever the street leaves undefended, and says so: "I'm not doing owt to you, Nowak. I'm just what happens when nobody's on a door."
- He is not the fool of the piece. He is the first to work out who the two strangers in car coats were (scene 10), and the first to work out that there is something about car two (scene 16). And he has an opponent of his own: the gate will not hire a Cammack, and the player sees him told so.

### Geoffrey Agar: his clean money, by the calendar

- Want: the yard behind Mickey's, which carries the only right of way to the old basin. His loan on the basin falls due on the thirty-first of December.
- How he comes: by letters, each with a date in it. A rent review, "with regret". A referral of Tom's licence to the sub-committee. Last, a telephone call to Customs and Excise.
- He needs the Widow, and cannot afford her: four hundred berths want building, and every pair of hands on the quay is hers. The player drives him to the gate to hear her price (scene 17).
- His clock is real. If the yard is not his by the thirty-first, the bank has the basin; so in the last act his agents use the one tool the lease gives them, on the one day it bites.

## 6. Act I: The Inheritance

Monday 15 to Sunday 21 October 1990. About two hours. The most scripted act. The beats of the approved first week stand; what this version adds is marked.

### Monday: the arrival and the first night

1. The ferry (0:00). The letter in his hand over grey water, the last line readable: "Every fare goes in the book." Nobody meets him. He has £23.50.
2. Quay Street (1:30). A line each, in passing: Darren, Ron, Ada at her window, June.
3. Sheila's walk-round (1:45). Her six lines as approved. "New management." The fare book: "Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule, and it's mine now." Added, on the forecourt: she stops at car two and looks at its nearside wing a moment longer than at anything else in the yard, and says nothing.
4. The first fare (2:30). Father Walsh, from the infirmary to his house by the chapel, Pauline giving the way by landmarks. Sheila hands him Mickey's badge: "Wear it till the council sits." The priest, at his door: "You missed a good funeral. Forty-one envelopes on the plate, and every one of them empty. They wanted him to know they'd come."
5. The rest of the office (4:30). The locked door, the flat, the debt book: five names, £156. Friday is rent, £38, and five wage packets.
6. The first deed (5:30). Darren's £22: front him up, take what he offers, strike it out. Somebody sees.
7. The first ask (7:00). Ron, the coat, the envelope. "Who's it for?" "Tallyman. Counted cargo on that quay forty year. That's all I'll say on a pavement, boss."
8. The carry (9:00). As written and as section 13 finishes it.
9. The landing (11:30). "Mickey's?" The thumb, the water. "Right. Wednesday, same again. And tell Kirby the round's twenty this week, not twenty-one." Ron counts £19 carrier's money into Tom's hand.

### Tuesday to Thursday

The approved beats, in order: Darren says the night to Tom's face; the first count with Sheila; the other four debts; Ada's "What were you doing in my alley?" and her one invitation; Wednesday's second envelope against Ada's tea; Ellis on the street if it is loud.

Added or changed:

- Grace will not take money for his first tea. Take it or pay: the first offer the game counts. Terry's flask, on Tuesday night's rank, is the second.
- Ada on the first Tuesday, on her step: "I'll not ask where you were before. Nobody on this street will. We've all been somewhere."
- The tea. Ada tells him plainly what he has been carrying: the sick club her Walter started in 1953, forty-one names, four pounds, half over the water. "The tallyman keeps the far side. The gate used to fill it. I get mine like the rest." Then, of his doorman, as a recommendation: "He's a good man, Ron Kirby. He came home at two one morning last November with no sleeves to his coat, and I never asked. You'll find I don't."
- Alison in the cab on Wednesday, to the warehouse row. "The brigade were called at a quarter to twelve. The watchman swore he was on his rounds. Nobody was on that row for the best part of an hour. I'd like to know where an hour went." She leaves her umbrella on the back seat. Give it back, or keep it till she comes for it.
- "How did Mickey die?" Sheila: "At that desk, at ten to six, with the kettle on and the book open. His heart. He'd had one warning, a year ago, and went back to nights the week after."
- A letter from the council on Thursday: he has been driving on a dead man's badge, a member has referred it, and his own application will be heard on Tuesday 30 October.
- Thursday night, two dockers waiting by car two: the first time Ron rides. Afterwards, in the car: "You never ask for owt, do you. Mickey was the same. Dead at a desk with the kettle on."

### Friday: rent, wages and the round

- Rent to Mr Agar's agents; wages by hand, and if he is short he chooses who waits.
- Night five, the third ask: a holdall. The Hook's half of the club goes round by hand on Fridays, twenty doors, and Ron walks it. "You'll want to see where it goes, boss. Or you won't. Mickey always walked it." A carry with faces: Winifred Duggleby; Albert Suggitt mending a net; Ada at her own door; a door on Tannery Row where Ron says "Knock soft. Her daughter's police"; and, last, across the Hook on Ropery Lane, one door where Ron goes up alone with a packet that did not come out of the holdall. "Not that one. That's Mickey's own."
- Then the Tangs', three of number nine, and the quay wall.

### Saturday and Sunday

- Saturday. Danny Cammack on Quay Street, buying chips, friendly.
- Sunday, early. The first lesson. "That's yours, boss. I go, you stop." The bollard, or a yard short of it. The bollard has a stripe of old paint on it already, the firm's colour.
- Sunday, ten o'clock. Sheila comes in on her day off. If she trusts him: "Mr Nowak." The first time. The real book is on the counter, shut. "That's the club's book and what Mickey ran beside it. You've had your week. So which is it going to be?" Wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say; asked twice, as built. The street learns the answer.

What the player believes at the end of week one: an old dockers' club, kept alive by his uncle, pays forty-one families; something happened on the warehouse row last year that nobody will say; his uncle's letter says people are owed; and he has a friend. The friend is the only one of those he would miss.

## 7. Act II: The Rise and the Squeeze

Monday 22 October to Sunday 2 December. About nine and a half hours. Scenes fire on states, never on sequence, except the calendar's days and dates that arrive in letters.

Three movements: the Seat (about four and a half hours), the Empty Seat (about three), and Two in the Car (about two). Eighteen scenes.

What a week is, between the scenes. Days are fares, dusk is a job, night is the count, and Friday is wages, rent and a round that is never the same twice: twenty doors, then nineteen, then eighteen, as people die, sign with the gate or send the packet back. No week repeats, because each has its own returning fare, its own dusk job set off by whichever opponent is moving, and one thing Sheila finds in the count.

| Week | The fare who comes back | The dusk job, and who set it off | What Sheila finds |
|---|---|---|---|
| 22 Oct | Ivy Leadley, for chapel | The freephone (Danny) | Kevin's miles are short |
| 29 Oct | Julie Kettlewell, to the labour exchange and back, three times | The cold-store road at half past five (the Widow) | The gate's fifty pences, paid or owed |
| 5 Nov | Mrs Rennison and the ham | Keeping three cars moving on Bonfire Night (Danny) | A window to pay for |
| 12 Nov | Darren, in the front, with radios | The two strangers off the last ferry (Danny); the tail to Ropery Lane | A wage packet nobody has collected: Ron's |
| 19 Nov | Alison, to the row, and then not to the row | Fetching car two back (Danny); the eleven doors (the Widow) | Eleven miles in last year's book |
| 26 Nov | Mr Agar, once, to the dock gate | The yard's lock (Agar) | What the firm is worth, to the pound, in the Widow's last offer |

The firm rises as the design has it (a third car, the freephone, the shift run, the row's watch), and each rung is something an opponent can price.

### First movement: the Seat

Scene 1. Tapper, and the freephone. Danny's number is on the freephone in the ferry terminal, and the boat's fares go to yellow cars. Ron brings Tapper, who works the ferry's ropes and knows when the ticket hall is empty. Getting Mickey's number back on that wall is a night's work with a screwdriver, a word with the clerk, or a price war. In the car, after: "Tapper could tap a tea chest so it sang the weight. One time him and the Bishop had a crate of..." Rain by four. He does not finish.

Scene 2. The gate at six. The Widow books a car herself and gets into the front. "Save you turning round." Ron has to get into the back, and does, without a word. Sixty men in the dark, twenty picked; Shiner first, talking. Her first offer, put once: fifty pence on every fare through the gate. Getting out, not looking at Ron: "You're wasted on a door, Kirby. You always were." Ron moves back to the front before he speaks. "Shiner. Shiner could talk a customs man out of his own hat. He was talking the night we..." He stops. Choice: pay the fifty pence, or do not.

Scene 3. The long way round. Kevin's day fares come in short against his mileage; Sheila sees it in the book before anyone says it. Followed, he is carrying parcels for the gate in Mickey's car. The player can ask the Widow straight, and gets a straight answer ("Yes. You didn't ask before"), or front Kevin on the rank, in front of the street: "Twelve years I've driven his car. She asked me what I wanted. Nobody here ever did." Choice: sack him; keep him and feed the gate what Tom wants it to hear; or ask him the question now. Asked, he tells: a plate of his own. It can be met, and it costs: an owner-driver is less a man's own. Comes back: Kevin is the firm's leak, his or hers, and from here the player thinks of Kevin whenever a car has been somewhere it should not.

Scene 4. The hearing, Tuesday 30 October. The council's licensing sub-committee, one room in the town hall. A member who owes Agar has referred the application over the dead man's badge. Only people Tom has let do him a turn have written a reference. If he has paid his way with everybody, the clerk says "No references," and waits. Then the door at the back opens, and the public bench fills: twenty old dockers in funeral suits, caps on knees, Tapper and Shiner among them. Nobody asked them. Ron is standing by the door. "I can't write you one, boss. I can fetch them as'll sit." It is the biggest gift in the game, and the player was never offered the press. Licensed, licensed with conditions, or refused; none ends the game.

Scene 5. The cold-store road. If the fifty pence was refused, or left three days, it is answered: the Widow's men stand across the cold-store road at half past five, and she stands in front of them. Tom's line leaves him four things: wait, with a packing shift going cold in the back; pay; turn round and lose the shift; or send Ron. If Ron goes, he gets out, walks to his old gang, says six words the player cannot hear, and the line opens like a gate. The Widow watches him do it. That is the moment she decides what she wants, and the player sees her decide. If the fifty pence was paid, she asks Ron, in front of Tom, to settle a row between two gangs at the gate, and he does, in six words.

Scene 6. Danny at the gate. A fare: Danny Cammack, to the dock gate, in a good mood. He wants the gate's custom for his yellow cars and has come to offer the Widow sixty pence where Mickey's pays fifty. She does not get down off her pallet. "I'll not hire a Cammack and I'll not ride with one. Your father knows why. Ask him." It is the only time in the game Danny is quiet in public. In the cab going back: "You saw nowt." Tom saw, and Danny knows it, and from here the two of them are something other than a joke. That week Keith Garbutt is seen twice at the rank after dark, talking to Ron, who listens with his arms folded. Asked: "Fixer. Fixing." Ron is quiet for two days, and on Friday buys two of number nine, not three, and then goes back for the third.

Scene 7. The second lesson. Sunday 4 November. L-plates. Pauline tells every cab to keep off the dock road. Ron drives its whole length at eleven miles an hour, talking the whole way, and asks to go round again. At the far end the warehouse row opens ahead of them. He stops short of it. "Not down there, boss. Surface is bad." It is not. Asked about it: "That's all I'll say in a car with a radio in it." Then, getting out, a thing he has never done: "I'll want tomorrow night, boss. Summat I've to see to." He has never asked for anything.

What the player believes going into the Fifth: Kevin is the man he might lose, and Danny is the man to watch on Bonfire Night ("I'd park them cars indoors Monday, if I was you"). A watchful player has more: a coat with no sleeves, a third portion, a road Ron will not drive down, and a Fixer on the rank. Nothing stops him asking Ron, on the quay wall on Friday, the question he has by now asked Kevin. If he does, the midpoint is a different scene.

### The midpoint: the Fifth

Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990. The quay is full of the town's grown people for the fireworks off the old coal jetty, and at the dock gate the dockers have their own fire, as every year: pallets and dunnage, a blaze the size of a van. It is the busiest night of the cab year, and the first Tom has driven without Ron: twenty minutes of the best fares in the game, the cars kept moving because Danny said to park them.

1. The fare. In Pauline's ordinary voice: "Car one, the dock gate, on the Widow's account, and she says bring a coat, there's a fire." At the gate the Widow is standing on a pallet with a clipboard, reading the winter's hire list by firelight. Beside her, straight-backed, in a foreman's donkey jacket with THE GATE across the shoulders, is Ron. His old gang is round him. He sees the cab. He does not come over.
2. The fire. On top of the dockers' bonfire, where the guy goes, somebody has sat a figure in a car coat with a cab's roof sign hung round its neck. The sign says MICKEY'S. It is Danny's lads' joke. Ron does not laugh. He does not take it down either. He watches it burn with his hands in the pockets of her coat. It is the thing burning that should not be, and half the Hook sees who stood by.
3. The radio. On the dock road, going back, Pauline, to every cab at once, kindly, which is worse: "Car one. You on your own?"
4. The window. With nobody on the door, Danny's lads have put the office window in while the street watched fireworks. Ada is on her step in her coat. "You'd better come in, Tom." It is the first time anyone on the street has used his name. Tea. She tells him who did the window. Then the thing she has been turning over: "He'll not have gone for money. I've known Ron Kirby forty years. He'd not cross the road for money."

What is lost: the seat. In the morning the docker's hook he wore in his belt for thirty years is on the office counter, and his door key is on top of it.

What the player believes: his friend has been bought, like Kevin, in the same way and by the same woman, and he watched it coming and did not see it. Ada's sentence is the first crack.

If the player asked first. Ron has never been asked what he wants, and it takes him until Sunday to answer: "To be let alone with it, boss. That's all. She's offered me the gate, and there's a thing she knows." He does not say what. Asked, he turns her down. Then the Fifth is not a loss of the seat. Ron is on the office door, so the window is whole; the Widow, who has lost the man she wanted, makes Tapper her foreman that night and takes it out of the firm instead: Kevin and the cold-store run are gone by morning. The effigy still burns, and the two of them watch it from car one. And what Ron is carrying is still not said. The second movement is then played with a man in the passenger seat who has stopped telling stories at all, and its ache is that the seat is full.

### Second movement: the Empty Seat

About three hours. It holds the two funniest set pieces in the game, and Ron is never out of sight. Every morning at six he is across the dock road, picking twenty men. On the second morning he picks something else, without looking across: "Mickey's. Two cars for the packing shift. Six sharp." A gift from a foreman to his old firm. Take it or pay.

Scene 8. Who sits in front. Fares alone. Nobody names the buildings. Other people start taking the front seat. Darren: "Can I sit up front? Only it's empty." Let him, or not. He has forty radios that pick up only the pirate station, and sells them to fares from the front seat. Garbutt goes round the firm with the Widow's offers: Kevin has his plate by Friday, if Tom did not meet it; Julie is offered a van; and Pauline is asked what she wants. If the player asks Pauline first, the answer is a Saturday, and it is the game's best set piece of plain comedy. Pauline's hour: she takes her first Saturday in nine years and Tom has the radio. The drivers test him at once. Kevin reports himself in Copper Row with gulls behind him. Sheila corrects every call Tom makes without looking up from the book. Darren says what he wants unasked: the door, and a coat with a firm's name on it. It is the one want it may be wrong to meet, and nothing says so.

Scene 9. Car two. The wing wants doing again, and Shaun Hewson has the back seat out to get at it. "Second time I've done this one. Same place. Mickey said he'd clipped the bollard on the dock road. He was a careful driver, Mickey. I thought it at the time." Under the seat, where a thing would roll: a cotton glove, burnt through at the palm, and a docker's brass tally with a number on it. Sheila looks at them on the counter and goes on writing. The 1989 books are on the shelf behind her chair, where they have stood since the walk-round. The find is a count, done with the hands: the night's sheets, the mileage column and the fare book, set side by side. For Saturday 18 November 1989 there is a driver's sheet for car one in Terry's hand and none for car two. Kevin's sheets are short all that month, as they are now. In the fare book, two lines in the small upright hand:

"22.50. Car one. Office to Infirmary. One. No charge."

"Night. Car two. Out and back. Eleven miles. No sheet. No charge."

The night of the fire. Terry, asked, uses seven words: "I took Mickey up. Not the row." Mickey Suddaby was on a ward with his heart on the night his cab went out, and his daughter was the sister on it. The dead uncle did not do it. His own fare book says so. That leaves the other man with keys. Kevin, fronted, is frightened and then angry: he was at his mother's on the far bank, he can prove it, and he says the thing that turns the player round: "I've fiddled miles, I've never fiddled eleven in a night. Whoever had that car out couldn't find his way home."

Scene 10. The answer. The window and the effigy want answering, and the street is waiting to see whether a firm with nobody on its door can. It is the one job in the game planned like a job, on the board in the office, with a week to plan it. Three keys.

- Stage it. Tapper and Shiner, lent for a day by a foreman who does not ask what for, go over on the morning boat in borrowed car coats and come back on the last ferry as two strangers with a holdall. Car one meets them with its light off and drives them, slowly, down Quay Street, past the Tangs' queue, to Danny's lock-up at the dead end of the row, where they stand and look at it for five minutes and are driven back to the landing. Nothing is touched. The planning is who sees what, and the one rule Ron sends with them: Shiner is not to speak, because half the Hook knows his voice. Shiner lasts as far as the Tangs'. In the car he cannot stop, and what comes out, to fill the silence, is the thing he has been not saying for a month: "I only told her who was driving. That's all. I thought she'd laugh. She didn't laugh. She went quiet and did sums." Tapper hits him with his cap.
- Answer it by hand, at the lock-up, at night. Or do not answer it, and the yellow cars are along the kerb by the weekend.

Comes back: Danny. Done well, he empties his lock-up that night and is seen doing it. Two days later he gets into car one, alone, in the back. "Them two. The ticket lass says they went over on the nine o'clock. You sent two old dockers on a day out and I shifted four hundred radios in the rain." A long look. "That's the best thing anybody's done to me." Then, serious: "Your doorman's on her gate and your door's a hole. I'm not doing owt to you, Nowak. I'm just what happens." Done badly, he knew on the night and says so in the Tangs' queue.

What the player now believes: Ron drove car two on the night of the fire; Shiner told the Widow; and Ron is at her gate because she is holding the police over him. It is the shape every story of this kind has. Two parts of it are wrong.

Scene 11. Remembrance Sunday, 11 November. The dockside memorial. By the Hook's custom Father Walsh reads the names: the wars, and the men the docks have taken since. This year, Michael Suddaby. Everybody is in one place for the only time; Ron has the gate's wreath, and the coat. And beside the dockers' wreath, in a wheeled chair with a rug, a man of sixty whose hands are in grey cotton gloves. Two minutes of silence in which the player chooses only where Tom looks, and sees who looks back. Afterwards Ron puts a foil parcel in the man's lap. The third portion.

Scene 12. Ropery Lane. The player can follow the third portion on a Friday, or go to the door Ron always took alone. A sister who answers; an old ship's surgeon's bag in the hall; a man in a chair who breathes like someone who has to think about it. Tom has the tally. The Bishop looks at it for a long time, and tells one thing, the only telling in the movement: "That's mine. He came back for me. Carried me out on his back, and then he drove that car like a man killing it. You'll be the nephew. Your uncle never missed a Friday. You needn't start." He says "came back". The player may hear it or not.

Scene 13. Two widows. Ada asks to be driven. She has not been in a car in four years. "The dock gate." The Widow has signed three of the club's eleven that week. At the gate the foreman opens the cab's back door for his employer, and it is Ron, a yard from Tom with a car door between them. Then two widows in the back of a minicab on the dock road, the meter running, Tom's eyes in the mirror. Ada: "A club is a thing you're in. What you're offering is a thing done to them." Maureen: "It's a wage." Then Ada, because she misses nothing: "You've Ron Kirby in a coat with your name on. What did that cost you?" Maureen: "Nothing. That's what I can't get past." Both of them look at the back of Tom's head. Maureen pays what is on the meter, to the penny.

### Third movement: Two in the Car

About two hours.

Scene 14. The Widow in the passenger seat. She books car one and sits in front, in his seat. Her last offer, put once: Mickey's becomes the gate's firm; Tom keeps the name on the roof and a wage; the club's book is shut, the eleven get work, and she will see the thirty onto what the state allows. "It stands until your books have been looked at. After that I'm buying from whoever has the keys." The player can say yes, say no, or ask her a straight question. She has never lied to one. Asked what she holds over Ron: "Nothing. Shiner told me who drove your uncle's car, and I told Ron I knew. That's the whole of it. I told him what a foreman earns in the same breath and I let him do the sum." And the police? She almost laughs. "He knows me better than that. Every man on that gate does." Then who did he think she would tell? She looks at the road for some time. "Who is there he'd mind knowing?" She gets out. "I'd no call to tell you anything. I'd not have. But I let him think I might, and I got a foreman by it. That's mine to carry, and I'll say so to him if you want it said." Ron did not sell himself to keep out of a cell. He sold himself so that the man who had been so pleased with him on the dock road would never hear what he did on it.

Scene 15. The third lesson. Sunday 18 November, a year to the day. The player goes to the gate at six. Ron is picking twenty men, and he is good at it, and they like him. Tom holds out the keys. "I go, you stop." Ron drives, properly, which is the thing to notice, out along the dock road and, this time, down the warehouse row. Past the burnt plot he does not slow. He speeds up, the way he did that night, back towards the dock road and the bollard, and the player has the handbrake. Stopped, a yard short or with the wing gone a third time, he sits with his hands on the wheel and finishes a story for the first time in the game. Mickey went down at his desk at ten to eleven, and the watchman's hour was the hour, and the stock was shipping on the Monday; so Ron took the keys. Three of them went in. The lamp went over. "I can drive, boss. Near enough. I'd sat next to drivers thirty year. I got Tapper and Shiner in and I went. I was at this bollard before I counted heads. Then I went back." Eleven miles: the row, the bollard, the row again, Ropery Lane, the yard. "He's never once said it. Came back, he says, like it was a kindness. Then you come, and you were that pleased when I got the length of the dock. And she says she knows who drove. I'd have swept her yard for nowt." Every laugh the lessons had in them is still there, and is now something else.

Then the question the player was never given the chance to answer on the quay wall: "She's had me a month. What do you think, boss?" The choice is the act's, and it is put kindly both ways. Come back: he comes, and the seat is filled, and the gate has lost the best foreman it had, and he knows what he gave up. Stay: he stays at the gate as a free man, which is the best thing for Ron, and the seat is empty for good. Or tell him it is his to decide, and ask him what he wants. He does not know. That answer comes in Act III.

Scene 16. Car two is gone. The anniversary has put the fire back on every counter. Ellis walks the row again with the watchman, then comes to the yard and looks at car two's wing, wet with new paint. She has had a brass tally in an envelope for a year. "Whose is this car to drive?" She asks while a thing is answerable. Shiner talks, and not only to widows: that night the yard gate is forced and car two is not there in the morning. Danny has it in his lock-up, and Garbutt brings the price: the freephone and the rank. He does not know what the car is evidence of. He knows a sergeant looked at it and an old docker went white. Fetching it back is the game's one break-in planned like a job, with three keys: a key cut from one look at the padlock; the roof light from the next building; or the door, with whoever will stand beside him. Who that is, is the sum of the movement: Ron, if he came back; Darren in a coat too big for him, brave and no use; Terry, who gets out of car one by himself; nobody. Or he pays, or leaves it, and Danny has something to sell a sergeant. Danny, after, win or lose: "Fair enough. I'd have wanted it back and all."

Scene 17. A fare for Mr Agar. Geoffrey Agar has never been in a minicab. He books one, by letter, to the dock gate, and sits in the back with his hat on his knee. Four hundred berths want building and every pair of hands on the quay is the Widow's. He asks her price standing in the mud. She gives it, once; it is fair, and it is more than he has until the basin is his, and the basin is not his without a right of way through the yard behind Mickey's. Both of them look at the driver. Going back, Agar says nothing for the length of the dock road, and then: "I have been outbid by a lady in gumboots for the labour to build on land I cannot reach. I do apologise. You will be hearing from my agents." The three opponents are one knot, and Tom's yard is the middle of it.

Scene 18. Agar in the rain. Geoffrey Agar walks down Quay Street for the first time in his life, without a hat, and knocks at the office. He has come to offer for the yard. Refused, he says the truest thing any rival says: "I have never had to burn anything, Mr Nowak. Things on that quay fall down by themselves, and I buy what is left. On the thirty-first I shall buy what is left of you, or the bank will buy what is left of me." Then, at the door, in another voice: "I am told a man of yours may want a solicitor before the year is out. I keep three. It is a gift. I do not expect you to believe that." Take it or pay. It is the heaviest press in the game, and nothing marks which it is. Either way he has already made a telephone call.

The act ends on a letter: courteous, dated, signed T. A. Reese.

## 8. Act III: The Ledger Comes Due

Monday 3 to Saturday 15 December. About two and a half hours. Seven beats, and an opponent moving in every one.

### Why the inspector comes

The name on the form changed when a registered trader died, so every player gets the letter. Tom's own wash, if he has one, gives it teeth. And a gentleman has telephoned to say the firm's cars are out at night with nothing written; Agar does not know how right he is.

The letter comes on Monday 3 December and names Monday the 10th. In the week between, each opponent moves once. The Widow's offer stands, and Garbutt says so at the counter. Agar's agents write, with regret: under the lease, a week's rent unpaid by noon on a Friday lets them change the lock; they have never used the clause; they give notice that they will. Danny, if he still has car two, lets it be known what he will take for it.

### The week

Monday 10 December. Reese at the counter with the book since October. "A fare at no charge is either a kindness or a hole, Mr Nowak. I'm paid to ask which."

Tuesday. The ride-along. "I always ride a route or two. Mileage is a truthful thing." He gets into the front passenger seat without asking. "My father drove a cab. I always rode in front." He sits where Ron sat, with his flask. If Ron is back, he gets into the back for the second time in the game, and the two of them discuss the weather with great seriousness. Brenda gets in with a box of live crabs that does not stay shut. "Are those declared, Mrs Laverack?" "They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in." Darren tries to sell a Customs officer a radio. Everything that happens is perfectly legal.

Wednesday. The old books, six years of them. He goes quiet twice and makes two small marks.

Thursday. He reads both lines aloud. "Ten to eleven, car one, office to infirmary, one, no charge. A kindness; I see whose name is against it. And the same night, car two, out and back, eleven miles, no sheet, no charge. Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak." Then, mildly, the reason it is his business: "A car out with no sheet is a shift I cannot see. If the driver will sign that he took no fare, it is one night and an oversight. If nobody will, I am obliged to suppose it was a working shift, and that there were others, and to assess six years on that footing. It would come to a little under four thousand pounds, and I should not like to do it." He lays a short printed statement on the counter: a line for a name, a line for a signature. "By five tomorrow. I ought to say one thing more. That night and that row are a matter the police have asked the public about. Whatever is signed, I must send them a copy. I am sorry."

Thursday night: Sheila's kitchen. Tom has never been to her house and has to find it. The 1989 book is on the oilcloth with the slip in it. "I wrote what the car did. I never wrote who. I came in on the Monday and it had eleven miles on it and a wing gone and the back seat smelt like a grate. I've known a year, and so has he, and we've said good morning every day of it. I'd not write a name I didn't see." Then she turns to the first page under the new owner and shows him a line he has never read: "15.10. Ferry landing. One. No charge." She entered him the day he came. "You were the last thing Mickey sent for. It wanted writing down that somebody came. Nobody reads a fare book." There is a pen on the table and a fire in the grate. He can leave both lines of 1989. He can strike the first, the true one, Mickey's alibi, so that a dead man drove. He can put the book on the fire, after which the books cannot hold. She does not ask him to do any of them. On the pavement outside is Ron. Kevin talks. "I'll go in tomorrow and sign it, boss. Don't tell me not to."

Friday: the round, backwards, and everybody in the way. The phone box on the corner, five coins, and until five. He can ring only people he once let give him something, and every call that is answered is a door he must drive to and knock on. What is signed goes to the police, so each paper he comes back with changes what a signature will cost the man who gives it.

- The Bishop, on Ropery Lane, to put in writing whose lamp it was, that nothing left the building, and who carried him out. Danny's yellow cars are parked nose to tail across the end of the lane: he has heard that Mickey's nephew needs to be somewhere by five. Round, through, or a word with Danny, who can be asked what he wants.
- June, at the infirmary, for where her father was at eleven that night. It is the first thing anyone has asked her for since the funeral.
- The gate, for Tapper and Shiner, who cannot leave it without the Widow's word. He has to ask her. If he has dealt straight with her all game: "Go on, the pair of you." If not, she prices it: one thing of his, named, once.
- Noon, the office. Agar's agents' men are at the door with a new lock, because Friday is rent. Thirty-eight pounds, in his hand, in time, or the afternoon is spent on the pavement.
- Agar's solicitor, if the gift was taken, to say what the papers add up to.

The constable of the first night stops him on the lit quay with a folder under his coat. "What's that, Nowak?" This time he shows it.

Five o'clock. The counter, Reese's pencil, one line. Three things can be written on it.

- "M. Suddaby." Reese: "The dead sign nothing. I can take the proprietor's word that the late proprietor drove, if the book does not say he was elsewhere." It needs the true line gone and June's silence. Nobody is charged, because nobody is left to charge. It is the thing the letter asked him not to be.
- "R. Kirby." Ron signs it himself. Reese: "Did you take a fare, Mr Kirby?" "No." "Then I have one night and an oversight, and I am glad of it." The copy goes up the hill. What Ellis has then is a man who drove three others to a warehouse they meant to rob. What it costs him is what Friday's folder made it. With the Bishop's paper, June's and the gate's, a detective who has wanted the truth for a year gets all of it at once: three old men, a lamp, nothing taken, an owner long since paid by his insurers, and the one man hurt asking that nobody answer for it. "I shall send it where I have to send it, Mr Kirby, with what I think on top. I don't think it will come back." With an empty folder it is a burned warehouse, a maimed man and a name.
- Nothing, and his own signature under it. "The proprietor declines to name the driver and answers for the night himself. T. Nowak." It is not a lie and it uses nothing but the truth: the firm is his, so the hole is his. Reese: "Then I must assess, Mr Nowak. I am sorry." A little under four thousand pounds, which the firm does not have; it is paid by selling, or it is not paid. If Ron is in the room: "Give me the pen, boss." The last word is the player's. The copy that goes up the hill has no driver on it. Ellis, later, at the office door: "A car, a night, and an owner who wasn't here then and won't say who was. You're the one man on this street I can't put in that car, and the one I know nothing else about. I'm not going to ask. I think you knew I wouldn't."

Then Reese closes his notebook. "That all seems to be in order, Mr Nowak." Or, if the firm's own books for this year do not hold: "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak."

Saturday 15 December: the last drive. Ron comes to the rank and holds out his hand for the keys. "I go, you stop." Tom gets in on the passenger side: his voice, and the handbrake. Where Ron drives depends on what was written and on what he was asked on the row. Up the hill to the police station, because a copy has gone there and he would sooner walk in than be fetched. To the ferry ramp, with a bag on the back seat, to be gone before anybody comes. To the dock gate, in the foreman's coat. Or just round. The Widow is on the pavement at the gate as they pass, hands in her pockets, and she nods once to the man who asked her a straight question, if he did.

The handbrake has meant one thing all game: Ron goes and Tom stops him, because twice in his life Ron has not stopped himself. At the end of the last drive there is a kerb, or a ramp, or a bollard, and the prompt is under the player's hand as it was on the dock road. Pulled at the ferry ramp, it keeps Ron in the Hook, and he has been made to stay. Pulled on the hill, it keeps him from the steps, and Tom has decided for him again. And if things between them are right, and the player leaves it alone, Ron brakes, and stops, and puts it on himself. "Got it, boss." The last input in the game is one the player can decline, and the game has spent three lessons teaching him what declining it means.

## 9. The endings

States, never a menu. Three shown, five beneath, in the built order. Each has a last scene, a line Sheila writes in the fare book and a line Tom writes in the Ledger. In each the verdict is which seat Ron is in, and each says where the three opponents stand.

### The Kingdom

Reached when the business stands and the books hold.

The plain turn. A dead man's name is on the paper. He has three cars, the shift run and the row's watch. Last scene: the landing after ten. A carrier in a dark coat that does not fit him walks the envelope down the lit quay; it is Darren, if he was given the door. The tallyman asks, for the first time, "Nowak's?" On the rank a cab comes in and Ron gets out of the passenger side, as he always has, and stands by the door, and does not look up at the flat window. He is back because a line was struck for him that he never asked to have struck, and he knows whose alibi it was. Sheila's keys are on the counter on top of the fare book. Agar has the yard, at Tom's price; Danny has the freephone and nothing else. Fare book, in a hand that is not hers: "Landing. Account. No charge." Ledger, in a hand that has started to look like Mickey's: "41 out. T.N."

The better turn (the built Both: one friend counts him, the street does not hold his rackets as fact, Ellis's case is answered, the books hold). Ron's name is on the paper because Ron was asked what he wanted and said it, and Tom stood beside him at the counter with a folder the Hook had filled. The file went where it had to go and did not come back. The Bishop's Fridays are in the fare book now as an account, in the open. Last scene: six in the morning, the dock road, car one. Ron is in the passenger seat by choice, naming buildings. "That was Stannard's ropewalk." At the gate the Widow is picking twenty, with Tapper as her foreman; she looks at the cab and nods. Agar's basin went to the bank on the thirty-first; Danny's cars have the freephone and Mickey's the gate. Sheila, as he goes out: "Night, Tom." Fare book: "Ropery Lane. Account. Fridays." Ledger: "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four."

### The Straight Life

Reached when no business of his stands at the close and one friendship holds.

The better turn (the built Quiet). His own signature is on the paper and no driver's name. The assessment has been paid by handing the firm to somebody who can hold it: Julie Kettlewell has the cars, on a loan from the chapel's fund that Father Walsh signed for, with Terry on the night desk and the radio; Tom drives for her. Last scene: Saturday, the rank. Car two is at the kerb with L-plates on and Ron at the wheel, on the right side, and Ron looks across. He drives him round the Hook at eleven miles an hour, and what he says on the way is the truest line in the ending: "You never ask for owt. You've just done it again. I'd have signed it. You never asked." It is a gift, and it is the flaw, and it is both. Sheila has found out for herself what the firm cost him and why; it is the thing she says "Tom" for. The Widow has the eleven; Agar has the yard, bought from Julie at a fair price; Danny says "Morning, Tom" in the Tangs' queue, and means it. Fare book, her hand: "Nowak, T. Round the Hook. No charge." Ledger: "Ron drove."

The plain turn. He sold to the Widow, on her terms, kindly. The roof sign reads THE GATE. The club's book is shut; Ada has not taken what the state allows. Sheila clears a desk of thirty-one years and gives him the last fare book: "It's a full one. I'd not throw it out." He stands at the landing in daylight with the suitcase, and the ferry goes out without him. The gate's van passes with Ron in the passenger seat, and Ron lifts one hand. Somebody passing says, "Morning, Tom." Fare book: "Closed." Ledger: "Stayed."

### Burn Both

Reached when nothing else fits. Two closes.

The Reckoning, for the man whose own books did not hold whatever he wrote. "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak." Sheila is asked for her keys by a constable half her age and hands them over without looking at Tom. By Saturday Agar's agents have their lock on the door and the Widow is buying from whoever has the keys. Saturday night is the first night again: the quay after ten, the lamp, no envelope. The constable turns round. He does not call "You there." He calls, "Nowak!" The top of the name ladder from the wrong side. The player has the two inputs of night one, stand or run, and the close cuts on the press. Fare book: closed unwritten. Ledger: the last page cut out, as the first thirty were.

The Suitcase, for the man who never asked anybody anything. No judgement, because nobody thinks enough of him to hold one. A grey afternoon. He walks down the gangway he walked up. Darren asks him for a pound. The gate's van passes with Ron in the passenger seat, and Ron does not look across. Ada watches from her window the whole way, as she did when he came. The fare book's last line, in the small upright hand, is the line she wrote the day he came, written again: "Ferry landing. One. No charge." Ledger: a date, and nothing under it.

### What every ending settles

What is written on the paper; where Ron is and in which seat; what Sheila calls him; who pays the Bishop's Fridays; who has the firm, the yard, the gate and the freephone; what became of Darren and Kevin. No ending redeems what was done. Reloading is never stopped.

## 10. What was true all along

For the builders: the truth in the order the player can find it. The street never has it. The street says the importer burned his own warehouse and went south.

1. The club. Walter Harker's sick club of 1953 grew a second purse under the dock scheme: cargo short-tallied at the gate by Ron's gang, held on the row, moved in Mickey's cabs. It paid forty-one names £4 a week, half of them over the water: £164. When the scheme ended in July 1989 the gate stopped; the Widow went lawful and paid nothing in. Since then the row's tenants have paid a weekly watch of about £90, and Mickey found the rest by drawing no wage and driving nights himself, which is why there was never a third car and why his heart went.
2. The night. By November 1989 the club could not last the winter. The importer's warehouse held a consignment of cigarettes, due to ship on the Monday. Mickey planned it: three men, twenty minutes, the watchman on his break, a cab for cover, himself at the wheel. At ten to eleven his heart turned at his desk. Sheila took him to the infirmary in car one, with Terry driving. The men were already waiting. There would not be another Saturday. Ron, who had sat beside drivers for thirty years, took car two.
3. The fire. A lamp went over on the cotton bales. Nothing was taken. Ron got Tapper and Shiner into the car and drove. He was at the bollard on the dock road, with the wing gone, before he counted heads. He went back, carried the Bishop out, and drove him to his sister's on Ropery Lane, because an infirmary asks questions and Mickey was in it; an old ship's surgeon did what could be done. Eleven miles. He walked home at two with no sleeves to his coat. The brigade was called at a quarter to twelve by a man on the far bank who saw the glow.
4. The year. Sheila found the miles, the wing and the smell on the Monday and wrote what the car did. Mickey came off the ward, paid the Bishop's keep every Friday, never forgave himself for a plan that burned a man, and told nobody who had driven. Ron swore he would not sit on that side of a car again.
5. The letter. "People are owed" meant the forty-one and one more. "Keep him if he'll stay" was the nearest Mickey could come to saying the rest.
6. The lessons. Ron could drive, near enough, the whole time. He let a stranger teach him because the stranger was pleased, and because a man who is being taught is not asked how he learned.
7. The Widow. In October Shiner told her who drove. She told Ron she knew, and what a foreman earns, in one breath, and let him do the sum. She would never have told the police, and Ron knew that as well as any man on the dock. What he could not bear was the nephew knowing: not that he drove, but that he drove off. She had no intention of telling Tom either. She let him think she might.

The three turns, in the order they land. That Ron drove is meant to be suspected: a watchful player has it before the Fifth, and Kevin is there for the player who wants someone else. The turns are the two after it. He went, and then came back (scene 15, set up by the Bishop's two words). And nobody was blackmailing anybody: the thing he sold himself to hide, he was hiding from the man in the next seat (scene 14).

What the truth changes: every lesson; the bollard; three of number nine; "Not down there, boss"; the bench of dockers at the hearing, fetched by a man paying something off; the effigy he watched burn; and "You never ask for owt", said in week one by a man hoping he never would.

Why it surprises. A reader watching for the hidden boss, the guilty dead uncle and the crooked landlord finds none of them. Then, having found a friend with a secret and an opponent who knows it, he supplies the blackmail himself, and is wrong twice: about what she would have done, and about who Ron was afraid of.

Why it is fair. The sleeves are in Ada's tea. The third portion is bought in front of him every Friday. The bollard has the firm's paint on it before Ron ever touches it. Ron will not drive onto the row. The lines are on a shelf in plain sight. Shiner talks. And the Widow answers a straight question straight from week two.

## 11. The story of the player who winds the firm down

On the first Sunday he tells Sheila it is a cab firm and nothing more. The carry and the round stop. The two roads then run the same eighteen scenes, and each reads differently. Where the keeper has the carry, the round and the watch, this road has three jobs of its own, as heavy in the hands.

- Monday. June, in his cab: "It doesn't have to be shut. It has to be lawful. There are friendly societies older than the docks. You'd want a room, a bank book and the names." Father Walsh has a room. Edith Stockill has the forms. The names are Ada's to give.
- The first Friday after. Ron: "There's one I'll still walk, boss. It's not the club's. It never was." The Bishop's door. A man who has shut the book still owes on the page that was Mickey's own.
- The Widow likes him. He has done half her work, her price for the firm is higher, and on this road she is the most reasonable person in the Hook. Refusing her means saying why a club should outlive its docks.
- Ada. "A club is a thing you're in. A charity is a thing done to you. I'll not be means-tested by Mickey's nephew." The society needs her name first on its list, and she gives it only to a man who has been in her debt and stayed there: the tea, taken, and never paid for.
- The hands. This road keeps every job the other has, because the jobs belong to the firm and the friendship, not to the club: the freephone, the cold-store road, the two strangers, car two, the count, the tail to Ropery Lane, the lessons. Its own three, in the weeks the keeper's road has the carry and the watch. The pay-out (week of 29 October): the society's first money, carried from the bank to the chapel hall in a holdall on foot, with Danny's lads behind him, a carry with a tail. The hall (week of 12 November): Agar's agents own the chapel hall's freehold too, and send four men in security jackets to clear it the night before the first meeting; Tom and Father Walsh's sidesmen hold a building, doors and a corridor and sixty stacked chairs. The eleven (week of 19 November): eleven doors, eleven men who could work and will not sign with the gate while the four pounds comes, each to be asked what he wants; the Widow has asked them already, and Albert Suggitt says so.
- The last act. He washed nothing, and the 1989 line can still close him: an assessment of six years falls on whoever owns the firm now. His clean books for 1990 are the proof that he is not his uncle, and what is written on the paper is the same choice.

## 12. Side stories that are whole stories

The thirty-two drafts of 8 November stay as the short ones. Four are grown into whole stories: a person with a want, a turn, and an end the town keeps.

1. The watch (Ron). Found: Mickey's watch in the pawnshop window the week he died. There is a florist's bill. Turn: Ron pawned it himself, for the funeral flowers, because the office was frozen and he would not ask Sheila. Ends: redeemed and handed back without a word, or left in the window. Turn two, after scene 15: the wreath at the funeral was paid for by the man who drove the car, and the watch was the only thing of Mickey's he ever took.

2. A ride for the sergeant (Carol Ellis, Ivy Leadley). Found: rain, night, the sergeant's own car dead by the fish market, and Pauline, dry: "It's the police. She wants a car." The address is the door on Tannery Row where Ron said to knock soft. After that Ivy Leadley, seventy-one, books Mickey's every Sunday for chapel. Turn: Ivy knows exactly what her four pounds is. She has told her daughter for years that it comes from the chapel, because her daughter would stop it; and her daughter, who is nobody's fool, has chosen to believe her. Each is protecting the other from a thing both know. Ends: he keeps Ivy's confidence; tells Ellis; or lets Ivy know that he knows, and is asked in. 

3. One way (Karen Grummitt). Light. The ferry's ticket clerk wants a car at five on Thursday, with her case, and not a word to Mrs Rennison, whom she owes a fortnight. The case is a case and a wardrobe. A cab with a wardrobe roped to the roof must pass Mrs Rennison's front step. Turn: Karen does not get on the boat. She stands at the rail she sells tickets for and cannot do it. Ends: he drives it all back, past the step again ("Friday means Friday"); or pays the fortnight and she goes. And Karen is the clerk who is on, or not, the night two strangers come off the last ferry.

4. The umbrella (Alison Sedman). Three beats across the acts: her fare to the row and the umbrella left; an evening on the quay wall with two of number nine, when she reads his Ledger over his shoulder and laughs out loud at the ham, the first time anyone in the Hook has found him funny; and, in the week of the anniversary, a fare to the row that she stops halfway. Her editor has offered her the news desk if she will leave the fire alone, and she says so laughing, and then asks Tom, as the one person in Meridian whose answer she will take, what she should do. Turn: the person being bought in this story is not anyone she is investigating. It is her, and the man she asks knows who drove. Ends: she takes the desk, and the paper runs the row as an anniversary and nothing more; she keeps the row and loses the desk; or he tells her, and she holds it until he says, at a cost to her. Every ending says where she is.

## 13. The scene in the first half hour a player retells

The carry, minute nine, kept as written: three routes with honest costs (the lit quay, the dark yard and lane, the cab with the firm's name on its roof), a constable who can turn round, and next morning Ada asking what he was doing in her alley and Darren saying it to his face.

If the constable takes the envelope, he is honest, so Ellis has it by morning. Sheila, at the count: "Twenty people over the water are short this week. You don't know what that means yet." And in Mickey's debt book, under the five names, she writes a sixth in her own hand: "Nowak, T. £80."

The second retold scene is lesson one: a docker of fifty-eight, a bollard, and the player's hand on the brake.

## 14. Humour and the mix of tones

Grounded noir carries the main line. The comedy is dry, local and attached to people. With no drink in the game, the pub's jobs are shared out.

- The radio is the bar. Pauline's commentary goes to every cab at once. "Car one, the chapel, and the lady says not the way you went last time." "Kevin, wherever you are, you are not in Copper Row, because I can hear the gulls." Her tally of Terry's words is on the office wall. The fifth word comes when Tom runs the radio on her Saturday, and Terry, asked for his position for the fourth time, says five: "Tell Pauline it don't count."
- Six set pieces built for laughing, spread so that no stretch is without one: lesson one and lesson two (the first movement); Pauline's hour and the two strangers in car coats, with Shiner under orders not to speak (the second, where the game most needs them); Agar in the back of a minicab at the dock gate with his hat on his knee (the third); Mr Reese's ride-along with the crabs (the last act). Karen's wardrobe floats.

The darkest scenes (the effigy, Ropery Lane, the Widow's straight answer, the third lesson, Sheila's kitchen) are played quiet and never joked across.

## 16. How play carries it, and the budget

- Hands first. Every scene is a fare, a carry, a round, a tail, a break-in, a fight, a drive, a lesson, a count or a planned job, with talk where the consequence lands.
- Nothing is on rails. Every turn of the truth can be found early: the third portion can be followed in week one; the books are on the shelf from the walk-round; the Widow answers a straight question from the gate at six onward; and Ron, asked what he wants before the Fifth, does not go.
- No game over; Tom cannot die; a car hurts and never kills; nobody is killed by the story. No gun is needed by it, and the dial moves only if the player answers Danny with more than a window. No drink, no gambling, no children, anywhere.
- What is asked of the systems is plain: written lines chosen by what the town's memory holds; one prompt with two answers; a typed question; a fixed passenger; a handbrake.

The budget, kept as it was written.
