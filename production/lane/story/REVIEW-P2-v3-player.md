# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 3: a player's review

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7 out of 10. It is good, and in two or three places it is better than good. As a whole it is too gentle and too tidy to be one I would push on people.

1. Tom: 7. Writing the flaw as something you play (take it or pay for it, the packed suitcase, asking what people want) is clever, and the Ledger lines give him a dry voice without a microphone. He is still mostly a pair of eyes in a mirror until the last act, and his change is quieter than the man sitting next to him.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Ada, Pauline, Terry and Reese are people I would remember. Darren, Julie and Kevin are thinner, and Alison as the love interest barely gets one evening on the quay wall.
3. The antagonists: 6. The Widow is a fresh idea, an opponent who never lies and never threatens, but nothing she does ever frightened me. Danny hurts one person once, and Agar is a man with a calendar and a phone call.
4. The central mystery: 7. Finding the four missing miles on the dial with your own wheel is a properly great piece of detective work done with your hands. Under it, though, the truth is small: a cigarette lift went wrong, nothing was taken and nobody died.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. Ferry, walk-round, carry at minute nine, a constable who can turn round, Ada at her window the next morning: that is a strong first night. Lesson one with the bollard is the scene I would describe to a friend.
6. Structure and pace: 7. The week table stops the loop from going stale, and the three movements of Act II have a clear shape. The first movement is four and a half hours for seven scenes and could sag, and the third movement crams the lesson, the last offer, the stolen car and Agar into two hours.
7. The middle: 7. The Empty Seat is the best part of the game: Ron picking men across the road every morning, Remembrance, the two strangers. But the clues pile up so fast in it that I would have the answer before the movement means me to.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. Setting up the blackmail I expect and then denying it is smart, and the dull taxman owning the night is a real turn. That Ron drove I would guess by Remembrance, and Kevin is cleared too fast to fool anyone.
9. The endings: 8. Every ending comes down to which seat Ron is in, and that is a verdict you can see. "I'd have signed it. You never asked" and the handbrake you choose not to pull are exactly how a game should end. The softest one, Reese recommending that nothing be done, feels like the story letting everyone off.
10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and her daughter, each protecting the other from a thing they both know, is as good as KCD2's best side stories. Karen's wardrobe is a sketch, and the watch only lands once the main story has done the work.
11. Tone and humour: 8. Pauline on the radio, Terry's fifth word, Shiner under orders not to speak, the crabs in front of a Customs officer: these are actually funny, and funny because of who is doing them. The dark scenes are kept quiet, as they should be.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The mileometer, the handbrake, the passenger seat and the fare book all carry the story, and nothing is on rails. The worry is the ask-what-they-want system: done badly it becomes a menu option I press on everyone.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The truth and the climax are too small and too kind for sixteen hours.
What is wrong: the night of the fire was three old men, a lamp and some bales. Nothing came out of the building, nobody died, the Bishop is already paid for, and the last act ends with an officer saying "I shall recommend that nothing is done."
Where: section 10, points 2 and 3; Act III, Friday, the "R. Kirby" branch.
Why I would feel it: after Red Dead I expect the ending to cost something. Here the inspector's mercy is handed out rather than earned, and I would forget the best ending by the weekend.
Fix: give the night a living victim who is still paying. The watchman, sacked for being on his break, is the obvious one: he lives on the row, the street blames him, and Agar's phone call rests on his word. Let Reese's "nothing is done" clear the three men only if the true account also clears the watchman in writing, so that Ron's own name on the paper is what gives a stranger his life back. That way the good ending has a price and a reason.

2. Ron's reason for the coat does not hold up when you think about it.
What is wrong: Shiner has already said it once in the Tangs' queue, which is where the whole street hears everything and where Darren sells what he hears. Moving Shiner to the night gate afterwards shuts the door after the horse has gone, and Ron walks out on Tom and watches the effigy burn to protect a secret the queue already has.
Where: section 10, point 7; scene 13 (Shiner's outburst); scene 14.
Why I would feel it: the coat is the midpoint and the emotional hit of the whole game. If the reason behind it falls apart when I think it over, the hit goes soft on the drive home.
Fix: make the slip specific and dangerous. Shiner said it in the queue with Ivy Leadley, the sergeant's mother, standing two places behind him. The Widow's gate is the one place in the Hook where police never get an answer, so Ron goes under her rule and takes Shiner with him. That ties in side story 2, and it makes the coat a shield rather than a gag.

3. The middle gives the answer away too early.
What is wrong: the sleeves, the burnt man in gloves at Remembrance, the third portion, "second time" on the wing, the glove and the tally under the seat, Terry's "Not the row" and the fare book lines all land within about an hour of the Empty Seat. Kevin, the only other suspect, is cleared in the same scene that raises him.
Where: Act II, second movement, scenes 9 and 10.
Why I would feel it: BAR is right that a guessed plot sinks a game. I would know Ron drove halfway through the second movement and spend the rest waiting for the story to catch up with me.
Fix: take the glove and tally out of scene 10 and put them in Ron's coat pocket, found later. Then let car two's eleven miles fall on a night when Kevin's sheet is also missing, so that the count points at Kevin. Keep Kevin as a real suspect until the dial in scene 12 rules him out, because a man who fiddles miles would not need four extra ones.

4. Nobody frightens me.
What is wrong: the Widow keeps every rule, and her worst act is to stand in a road. Danny breaks one arm. Agar sends letters. The world feels safe all the way through.
Where: section 5; scenes 5, 17 and 18.
Why I would feel it: GTA's villains were thin but they were frightening. Here I would admire the Widow and never once dread her booking a cab.
Fix: give the Widow one lawful offer that chills me. She offers to pay the Bishop's Fridays from the gate. It is generous, it is legal, and it puts Ron in her debt for good, at the very moment he is trying to owe nobody. And give Danny a second move before car two, so that his hurting Julie is the end of a climb rather than a jump.

5. Take-or-pay and "what do you want" risk becoming a routine.
What is wrong: tea, flask, the fifty-pound note, the gate's cars, the solicitor, the umbrella, the hat, the coins in the phone box. That is a lot of single presses on the same theme, and nothing tells me what they add up to until the hearing or the phone box. The question is meant to be asked "in his own words", which in practice will be a menu option.
Where: section 3 (the offers and the question); scene 4; Act III, Thursday.
Why I would feel it: once I spot the pattern I will play it as a mechanic and not as a choice about who Tom is.
Fix: cut the offers to about eight that matter, each with its own Ledger line in Tom's voice, so I can see the debt build up. Make the question something Tom can ask only once of each person, never of passing fares, and let a refused answer stay on the page of the Ledger until it is met.

## 3. Would I single it out?

No. Not yet. Ron and Tom are the nearest thing to Henry and Hans I have seen on paper: the lessons, the passenger seat, the four miles on the dial and the handbrake you leave alone are things I would talk about. But a review that says "play it for the story" has to promise you will not see it coming and that the end will cost you something. As written, I would see the main turn coming in the middle, and the best ending lets everyone off. Fix the coat and the cost of the climax and this goes up a full point and becomes a yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. The driving lessons and the handbrake. "I go, you stop," the bollard with the old paint on it, eleven miles an hour, and a last input in the game that the player chooses not to press. This is the spine, and it is something only a game can do.
2. The mileometer as the detective. Driving the route twice, getting seven, and working out the four by yourself is the best discovery scene here, because the player finds it with the wheel and nobody tells him.
3. Reese and the Widow as people who never lie. The mild man with the flask who turns out to own the night, and the opponent who answers a straight question straight, are what stop this from being the usual crime story. Keep both exactly as they are.
