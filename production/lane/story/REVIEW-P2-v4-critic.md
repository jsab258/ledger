# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 4: a critic's review

This is a story about two men who will not ask each other anything, and a small woman who has told nothing but the truth for a year and still kept the one thing that matters. At its best it does things with a steering wheel and a mileometer I have not seen a game story do. It is also leakier and softer than its best scenes.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8 out of 10.

1. Tom: 7. The want (his own name said by people who mean it) and the fear (the packed suitcase, one press to unpack) are clean and playable. But his change is left to a binary press the player can learn to game, and the best endings show the flaw intact rather than overcome, so Tom is a good frame more than a man who turns.

2. The cast: 9. Ron, Sheila, Ada, Pauline and Terry are the real thing: each has one voice, one want and one habit you would recognise from the back of a cab. Ron's stories that stop one sentence later each time is the best character device in the document.

3. The antagonists: 6. The Widow is a fine idea, a woman who wins by asking, but she never pays a price, never loses her temper and stands aside at the climax. Agar apologises, Danny hurts one person once, and the last-act officials are kind; there is texture here but almost no menace.

4. The central mystery: 8. A truth found with the hands (the tally in the runner, seven miles driven twice against eleven written) that reframes "Every fare goes in the book", "New management" and a year of good mornings. It loses a point because its own best clue gives the answer away too early.

5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The carry, the landing and Ada's "What were you doing in my alley?" are a strong first night, and the constable writing "Nowak, T. £80" into the debt book is a sting players will tell friends about. Two hours of the most scripted act, with rent, debts, a hearing letter and a walk-round, risks the slow-opening complaint.

6. Structure and pace: 7. Seat, Empty Seat, Two in the Car is a lovely shape, and putting the loss at Bonfire Night is right. Act III spends three days at a counter and a kitchen table before anyone gets in a car.

7. The middle: 7. The Empty Seat movement is the best stretch in the bible. The first movement and the weekly loop of fares, dusk jobs and counts are a six-row table, and nine and a half hours will need far more than six rows.

8. Surprise against cliché: 7. The friend who seems bought but is sacrificing himself is a known shape, and Ada undoes it the same night. The real turn, that the driver is the one person the story trains you to trust, is genuinely good, but it is a known mystery move too and it is visible from the seat.

9. The endings: 8. Each has a last scene, a fare-book line and a Ledger line, and "Ron drove" and "41 out. T.N." are endings I would remember. The conditions behind them (books holding, "Tom's own wash", five states, "the built Both") are opaque, so a player may not see his ending coming from his own play.

10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and her police daughter, each protecting the other from a thing both know, is a whole story and touches the climax. The watch and the umbrella are good; One Way is a sketch; four grown stories in two hours is thin for fifteen to twenty hours of play.

11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline on the radio, Terry's fifth word, the crabs and Agar's hat are warm and dry, and the darkest scenes are rightly played quiet. It is likeable rather than laugh-out-loud; nothing here is as funny as KCD2's set pieces at their best.

12. Story and play feeding each other: 9. The mileometer, the handbrake as "somebody else goes and Tom stops them", the seat that will not move, the hearing bench filled by people Tom let do him a turn: this is what the bar means.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The seat solves the mystery in week one. What is wrong: the bible says every player will believe Ron drove, but it tells us in the walk-round that car two's seat is stuck forward, that Ron is folded behind it with his knees at his ears, that Sheila is five foot one and fetches the steps, and in lesson one that Mickey taught Sheila on that road, "far as I know". Where: section 6, the walk-round and Sunday's first lesson; section 10's "why it is fair". Why a player feels it: any reader of detective novels sees that a short person last set that seat, so the red herring is ruled out by the joke meant to dress him, and scene 15 lands as confirmation, not shock. Fix: keep the seat as Kevin's running grumble, but make it "sticks", not "sticks forward", until Shaun pulls it in scene 10 and shows the runner was jammed by the tally at whatever notch it stood; move "Mickey taught Sheila on this road" out of lesson one and give it to Ron on the quay wall after scene 12, and drop "far as I know" until the third lesson.

2. The main opponent is absent from the climax, and the last act goes soft. What is wrong: the Widow never threatens, never breaks a deal and in Act III only says "Go on, the pair of you". The real pressure is Reese, who winks at a lie ("I am not paid to measure"), and Ellis, who gives a caution for a night that burned a bonded store and a man's hands. Where: section 5; section 8, Thursday and Friday. Why a player feels it: a story about debt with nobody collecting at the end. Fix: put the Widow's price on Friday itself. Tapper and Shiner's word is hers to give, and she names one price, once, in the open: the firm, signed over that afternoon, for the two men at the forecourt by two. She breaks no rule and holds nothing over anyone; she simply prices the truth. Let Reese's acceptance of "R. Kirby" go into his notes as a doubt, so January stays a real threat in that ending.

3. The road of the player who winds the club down is a sketch. What is wrong: section 11 is five bullets against a middle whose engine is the club's money: the round, Shiner's brag, Ron's coat, the eleven. Where: section 11, and every Act II scene that assumes the carry continues. Why a player feels it: the bar says no stretch may be thin, including this road. A player who refuses the crime in week one will find scenes that have lost their reason and a midpoint (the hall) that plays like a side job. Fix: write that road's six weeks as a table like section 7's, with its own returning fares, dusk jobs and Sheila's finds; say plainly that Ron still takes the coat for the same reason, because the fare book and Shiner exist on both roads; and give the hall on the Fifth its own loss to match the empty seat.

4. Take-or-pay can be solved, and Tom does not clearly change. What is wrong: the hearing teaches early that people who were let do Tom a turn write for him and fill the bench. From then on the sensible player takes everything, so the flaw stops being character and becomes a strategy. The best endings then praise or forgive the flaw ("You've just done it again") instead of showing Tom ask. Where: section 3, the offers and the question; scene 4; section 9. Why a player feels it: a choice with a known best answer is no choice, and an arc that ends where it started reads as no arc. Fix: after the hearing, let the hooked gifts outnumber the clean ones and come back sharper (Danny's fifty read in court is the model). Make the Kingdom's better turn need the one question Tom has never put to Sheila, asked at her kitchen table on Wednesday night, so the best ending is the one where he finally asks.

5. Act III is talk and paper, and the endings' rules are hidden. What is wrong: Monday to Wednesday is Reese at the counter, Reese reading lines aloud, and a kitchen confession the player has already guessed. Thursday is a phone box. The ending conditions rest on states the player never sees counted, including "Tom's own wash", which the bible never sets up. Where: section 8, the week; section 9's headings. Why a player feels it: the bar's punished slow scenes and too much talk arrive at the worst moment, and an ending that turns on unseen bookkeeping feels forced rather than earned. Fix: fold Reese's counter and the old books into one beat, and open the week with the ride-along and the crabs. Make Thursday's yard gate the act's big set piece. Have Sheila say one line at every Friday count about whether the year's books would hold, so the player sees his ending coming in the fiction.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review?

No. Not yet. I would single out scenes: the second lesson, the effigy with the roof sign, the eleven miles driven twice on a dial, and "Still where she left it". But the bar is the whole story, and this one gives its answer away in the walk-round, sidelines its main opponent at the climax, leaves the refuser's road unwritten and softens in its last act. Fix those and the answer could change; the bones are better than most.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron and the passenger seat: the lessons, the stories that stop a sentence later, the third portion, the bench he fetched unasked, and Ron in a vehicle in every ending. This is the game's Henry and Hans, built for the hands.

2. The eleven miles: a truth found with a wheel and a dial, driven again on Friday by whoever's hand is on the paper, with the handbrake as the last input and nothing the right thing to do with it.

3. Sheila as the one who never said a false thing: her withholding, "Mr Nowak" and the one "Tom", and the fare book as alibi and noose paid off by a mild man with a flask.
