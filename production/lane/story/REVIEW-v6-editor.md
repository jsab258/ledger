# LEDGER story bible v6: script editor's note

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7.

1. Tom: 8. The want (his name said with warmth), the flaw played as one press, the line that offers other men's hands and the packed suitcase make an unvoiced man somebody; the Ledger's dry voice does the rest.
2. The cast: 8. Sheila, Ron, June, Terry and Pauline are specific and each turns. But "fewer people" is not delivered: over forty are named, and Philip, Kevin, Julie and Lee are jobs, not people.
3. The antagonists: 8. Maureen, Agar and Danny each have a want, a page, a scene and their own way of coming at him; Ada is the most dangerous. Cyril, at the hinge of the truth, has no want or scene of his own.
4. The central mystery: 7. A real truth, dug by hand across four jobs, changes what the first week meant. But the dead man's plan does not quite hold, and nearly every clue is a document.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The carry with the constable who can turn round, and the failure branch that writes "Nowak, T. £80" into the debt book, is the best thing in the first hour.
6. Structure and pace: 6. The calendar spine (the hearing, the Fifth, Remembrance, the anniversary, the letter) is excellent and keeps a slow player moving. Act III is overloaded and will feel hurried, the exact fault the bar names.
7. The middle: 7. It turns, twice (the Fifth, then the anniversary), and the rival peaks come in turn. The doubled roads halve the depth of each; the straight road reads thinner.
8. Surprise against cliché: 6. Danny's innocence and Mickey as the author of the fire are good turns. The widow at the window who runs the outfit is the twist a seasoned viewer calls in the first hour.
9. The endings: 8. Every close has a last scene, a line in the fare book and a line in the Ledger, and Ada's table in all of them is right. Ada walks away from every ending largely untouched, which a player who saw Terry's hands will feel.
10. The side stories: 8. Ivy and her daughter, each protecting the other from a thing both know, is a whole story in four lines; the watch and the wardrobe are good. Norman's carries too much main plot to count.
11. Tone and humour: 7. Some of it is actually funny ("The ham had a seat"; the crabs that "don't fill forms in"; Ron at eleven miles an hour asking to go round again). Brenda's postcard lines are stock, and the light thins badly after the Fifth.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 7. Hands-first framing is right and the hearing and phone box are buildable. But the dig is a paper chase, the offers have no tells, and the climax's key choice does not make sense as play.

## 2. The five weakest points

### 1. The climax night does not hold together

What is wrong. On Thursday morning Reese reads the 18 November 1989 line aloud. He then locks the seized 1989 fare book in the boot of his own car outside a boarding house. That night Tom opens the boot, Sheila is waiting on the wall, and the player chooses to leave the line, alter it "for a man who notices ink", or burn the book. Altering a line the inspector has already read aloud achieves nothing. A Customs officer leaving seized records overnight in a parked car is not believable, and stealing them from his car would bring far more trouble than the line. Sheila knowing where to wait is convenient.

Where. Section 8, Thursday and Thursday night.

Why it matters. This is the fare book's climax, one of the owner's three keeps; if the hardest choice is pointless, the noose goes slack.

Fix. Reese does not read the line aloud. On Thursday he slips a paper marker into the 1989 book at November, says he will "go through that week in the morning", and the book goes home with Sheila, as all the old books always have, under his receipt, to be produced at nine. The night scene moves to Sheila's front room: she has opened it at the marker before Tom knocks. Altering it is now a real choice (Reese has not seen it), burning it has a known cost (listed records missing), and Sheila's waiting is earned. Ellis's nine o'clock trade still works.

### 2. Mickey's plan has a gap at its centre

What is wrong. Mickey hires the Coultas brothers in September 1989, arranges the watchman's Saturday lifts from 2 September, and only then tells Ada the importer has refused, "so that she would find the reason". The bible never says why he needs her reason at all, when he has already got the men, the cab and the empty row. He writes "Importer a/c. Paid" in the fare book while writing the lie in the real book: a careful liar leaving the truth in the open. Why Agar wanted the burnt plot, as opposed to the yard, is never stated. And Cyril, Ada's own man, hears the importer say he paid and tells not even Ada.

Where. Section 10, items 2 and 5; section 7, scene 16; section 8, the boot.

Why it matters. The last truth is what the whole game is built to pay off. A player who dug will ask "why did he need Ada?", and "he did to Ada what Ada did to Tom" only lands if the mechanics are sound.

Fix. Make Ada's word necessary: under the club's rules the row is under its protection, and Ron and the row men would stand between any outsider and a paying tenant unless the table declared a lesson. Mickey needs the lesson declared so that the row looks away. Say plainly that the rent went through the fare book dressed as an account customer, laundered into turnover (which is why a VAT man reads it), because nobody reads a fare book. Give Agar one line on the plot: it is the frontage of Meridian Quays. Give Cyril a reason for silence: he owed Mickey, or he did tell Ada and she chose not to hear it, which would deepen her at the table.

### 3. Too much story for fifteen to twenty hours of a hands-first game, and Act III pays for it

What is wrong. Act II runs twenty scenes on each of two roads, four dig jobs, six whole side stories and thirty-two short ones. Act III, in three to four hours, holds Reese on five days, the yard fight and Agar in the rain, Alison's proof, Ellis's trade, the boot, Sheila's confession, Mickey's page, the phone-box run, the rival on the pavement, Ada's table and the close.

Where. Sections 7 and 8; the claim in section 4 of "fewer people".

Why it matters. The bar names a hurried last act and a thin middle as what players punish most.

Fix. Fold Agar's confession into Garbutt's table, so Tuesday night of the last week is only the yard held with hands. Cut Les Coultas to one appearance (scene 8) and let Mickey's page close him. Give the straight road fewer rungs (three, not five) and spend the saved hours on two of them done properly. Keep Act III's Monday and Wednesday as short paper days, so the weight sits on Tuesday night, Thursday night and Friday.

### 4. The midpoint twist is the one everybody expects, and the man who should hide it is a cipher

What is wrong. An old widow who watches the street from her window, invites him once, likes to know "where the paper sleeps", is on the round as "Walter Jensen's widow" and is the subject of the most-praised early scene is the obvious hidden head. Cyril is the apparent boss but never acts like one beyond giving Ron a number. Any viewer of British crime drama clocks Ada by the Wednesday tea.

Where. Section 4 (Ada, Cyril), section 6 (the round, Sunday's passenger), section 7 (the window on the Fifth).

Why it matters. The bar asks for no predictable stretch, and the midpoint is where the middle must turn. If guessed, the Fifth is a confirmation, not a blow, and Mickey must carry all the surprise.

Fix. Build Cyril as a convincing boss with a want of his own: give him a week-one or week-two scene where he refuses Tom something and overrules Ron in front of the street, and a private reason (a debt to Walter, a son of his own in the gate's pay) that makes him the obvious suspect for the fire. Then give Ada one genuine kindness in the first tea (she pays Winifred's missing week from her purse) that reads as mercy and, on the replay, as a ruler keeping her people. The Fifth then reverses a belief, not a suspicion.

### 5. Set-ups left hanging

What is wrong. Several threads are planted and dropped. Who drove the watchman home every Saturday from September 1989, and fetched him late on the night of the fire? Terry "drove nights for Mickey for twenty years and saw nothing, on purpose": the set-up begs for him to be that driver, and nothing pays it. Danny's offer "You can't touch her. I can" has no written outcome if Tom says yes. Ray Coultas is a name.

Where. Section 7, scenes 10, 15, 18; section 3 (his line); section 8, the inspector's causes.

Why it matters. If the strongest offer of other men's hands (Danny on Ada) leads nowhere, the design blinks. Terry as the unknowing driver would deepen the midpoint.

Fix. Make Terry the Saturday driver, sent by Sheila "per M", who never asked; he tells Tom in his four words on the ward. Write Danny's yes as a scene that stays inside the frame: Danny takes Ada's envelope off her table in front of the street and walks the round himself, and she loses the round, not her skin.

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Pound notes. June fills envelopes with "pound notes"; the Bank of England £1 note was withdrawn in March 1988. Fix: pound coins and fivers.
2. Minicabs on ranks. Mickey's is private hire, licensed as an operator, yet his cars and Danny's wait on "the ferry rank" and Terry sits on "a cold rank". In 1990 a private hire car on a taxi rank was plying for hire, and a British viewer from the trade will notice. Fix: call it the ferry pitch on the firm's own forecourt, or give Mickey's two hackney plates (Kevin already "wants his own plate").
3. The hearing. A private hire sub-committee did not call for character references from a public bench. Fix: the chair asks whether anyone has written in support, and the people who stand are the letters read out, with those present nodding in the room.
4. The lock-up raid uses "Norman's hour off the row on Saturday", but Ada's deadline is Friday's round and the bible says the player goes on Thursday. Fix: make it Norman's Thursday tea break, or move the deadline.
5. The paraffin can and (S) envelope sit inside Danny's locked lock-up. How did Darren get in? Fix: Darren once ran radios for Danny and still has a key.
6. Ada "sold her house long ago" yet lives in it with a lodger. Fix: she sold it to Agar's agents and rents it back, which ties her to Agar.
7. Mickey's £4,000 "kept the forty-one paid for a year", but the forty-one cost £8,500 a year. Fix: "kept them paid through the winter".
8. Agar says Mickey came "last September"; spoken in December 1990, that means 1990. Fix: "September of last year".
9. A VAT man's interest in a free fare. Fix: give Reese the trade reason in one line: forty miles of fuel with no fare against it looks like takings kept off the book.
10. The offers have no tells, and every hook is called in on the last Friday. That breaks the bar's "seen coming". Fix: each hook-giver names a day or a favour in passing, and the Ledger writes it down.

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review

No, not yet, though it is closer than most. The bones beat the genre: a theme carried by four books, a lead built for an unvoiced player, a friendship in the passenger seat, a VAT inspector as the last antagonist. The two widows in a minicab, Terry at Ada's window and "Go on, Tommy" are scenes a reviewer quotes. But he would also see the midpoint coming, feel the last act hurry and stumble on the boot night. Fix those three and I would name it.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. The fare book as alibi and noose: the line Sheila wrote when Mickey would not, read by a mild man from Customs. The most original engine here.
2. Ron and Tom: the passenger seat, the lessons that stop being funny, the Bishop story, and every ending judged by which seat Ron is in.
3. Tom's line, the offers of other men's hands, and take-or-pay: character made of presses, remembered by the town.
