# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 4: a script editor's note

Where the bible points at text I was not given (the carry, the approved first week), I have judged only what is here.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8. A fair mystery built on two people in one car, with a climax that is driven, not watched. Not yet a 9: its central choice is not tied to its theme, its twist is easier to see than it thinks, and its last third is overloaded.

1. Tom: 8. The flaw is played in two inputs, take-or-pay and the question, and the suitcase is a fear you can press. But what Tom does to reach the best ending is undefined, so his change has no decisive act.
2. The cast: 8. Sheila, Ron, Pauline, Terry, Ada and Reese are heard in one line each. Twenty-seven is too many for 16.5 hours: Kevin, Julie and Darren are functions.
3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is lawful, never lies, and comes four ways, including with her own body on the cold-store road. Agar is letters until scene 18, Ellis barely has a scene, and the Widow pays nothing in any ending.
4. The central mystery: 8. A truth that changes "Every fare goes in the book", "New management" and the seat, found with a mileometer, not a confession. The joints are the coat's mechanism and how visible the answer is.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 7. The carry at minute nine and lesson one are the right two scenes. Act I is still two hours of walk-rounds, teas and twelve introductions.
6. Structure and pace: 7. Act II's shape is good and the Fifth is well placed. The third movement crams six events into two hours while the week of 3 December is empty.
7. The middle: 7. The weekly table is a real answer to the loop complaint, and the Empty Seat is the best stretch. It is still the same three-beat week six times.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. The Fifth surprises and the dial is fresh. The answer, the trusted one who never lies, is a known type, signposted hard.
9. The endings: 7. Every ending has a last image and a seat. Triggers are fuzzy, and Sheila's price rests on a power Customs did not have.
10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and her daughter, each protecting the other from a thing both know, is excellent. Alison's is good; Karen's is a sketch.
11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline, Terry's fifth word, the crabs and the lessons are funny. Listing Agar's confession as comedy shows the back half is thinner than claimed.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The dial, the seat, the handbrake left alone, references and phone calls only from people you let help you: the bible's great strength. The typed question needs a design that does not collapse into a menu.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The climax is not decided by the theme. Where: section 8, Wednesday night in Sheila's kitchen, and section 9, the Kingdom's better turn and the Straight Life's better turn. What is wrong: the best ending happens because Tom "did not write one for her and did not let Ron", but nothing says what the player did to make her sign her own name, and the Straight Life ending then scolds Tom for never asking. The game teaches one verb, the question, and the climax does not use it. Why it matters: an ending must be earned by play; this one is earned by an absence. Fix: make the question the key. In the kitchen, Tom can ask Sheila what she wants. Asked, she says she wants to sign it herself, and the Kingdom's better turn follows. Not asked, only the other names are open. The flaw gets its cure in one press.

2. The twist is fairer than it is hidden. Where: section 6, the walk-round ("Car two's seat sticks") and lesson one ("Mickey taught Sheila... far as I know"); section 4, five foot one with the steps; scene 10, both lines "in the small upright hand". What is wrong: by week one's end a genre-literate player holds a short woman, a seat stuck forward, a lesson in her past and a woman who never says a false thing; at scene 10 the line is in her hand. Why it matters: the bible claims nobody suspects her; many will by hour two, then wait eight hours for confirmation. Fix: give the seat a second owner. Make Mickey a small man, so "it's set where Mickey had it" is the street's reading until scene 10 rules him out. Move "Mickey taught Sheila on this road" out of lesson one and into Ada's mouth in the Empty Seat. Let drivers write their own lines in the fare book, so the small upright hand only matters once you know to look.

3. The wind-down road is a sketch. Where: section 11. What is wrong: five bullets cover the player who refuses the crime, and BAR names this road. It does not say how the count, Reese or the last Friday play for a man with no rackets, or which ending he can reach. Why it matters: a thin honest road is exactly what reviewers punish. Fix: write it as scenes, with its own column in the weekly table, its own dusk jobs (the pay-out, the hall, the eleven men), Reese arriving anyway on the change of owner, and its own version of the Kingdom: the society registered, the firm lawful, Sheila's line still to answer.

4. The last third is overloaded and the week before it is empty. Where: section 7, scenes 15 to 18 in two hours; section 8, Thursday's three doors and the phone box; the week of 3 December. What is wrong: the reveal, the last offer, Danny's theft, Julie's injury, the break-in and Agar's confession pile up, then the game idles a week. Why it matters: a hurried last act is GTA V's named fault; scene 15 needs room to land. Fix: move scene 18, Agar's fare, into the week of 3 December as Act III's opening, followed by Reese's letter. Move Danny's theft of car two a few days earlier, into the week of 19 November where the table already puts it. Let Thursday be two doors.

5. The opposition is uneven, and the Widow never pays. Where: section 5; scene 4; scene 17; every ending. What is wrong: Agar has no face before scene 18; Ellis is a file and a closing line; the Widow ends up with the eleven, the gate and Tapper in every ending, at no cost. Why it matters: an unbeaten opponent reads as the writer's favourite. Fix: put Agar at the hearing in scene 4, apologising across the room. Give Ellis Remembrance Sunday, standing by her mother, looking at the man in the gloves. Let her rule cost the Widow once: when Ron comes back after scene 15, she has to pick twenty without him, a fight breaks out at the gate, and she loses a contract the player can see.

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Section 9 and section 8, Monday: Sheila's line "Ferry landing. One. No charge" records a fare that never happened, since "Nobody meets him". The woman who never says a false thing has written one. Fix: Terry, unasked, picks Tom up at the landing.
2. Section 8, the green licence "issued in 1961": in 1961 licences were red council booklets renewed every few years; the green paper licence came with the national centre in the 1970s. Fix: a green licence valid to seventy, first issued 1961, which she has kept renewing, and that is itself a clue.
3. Section 6 and scene 10: a working cab's seat stuck for a year, with a six-foot driver, and nobody mends it. Fix: Sheila, who pays the garage account, has twice struck the seat off the bill.
4. Section 8, Friday: Reese says "they will not let that stand" about her keeping the books. Customs had no power to bar a private bookkeeper. Fix: she gives up the book herself, so that the firm's records are not kept by a cautioned person while it is under watch.
5. Section 10, the fire: Tapper and Shiner were in the car, so Sheila cannot have reached the bollard "before I knew who I'd left". Fix: "They were shouting. I was at the bollard before I could make myself stop."
6. Section 10: a lamp in a 1989 break-in, and cotton bales in a cigarette bond. Fix: a paraffin heater in the watchman's hut beside mixed stock, knocked over in the dark.
7. Section 0, item 1: "cigarettes for the dockers' club". In 1990 Britain a dockers' club means a licensed bar, which brushes the no-alcohol rule. Fix: say "the sick club" everywhere.
8. Section 4: Kevin is "twelve years in Mickey's car" yet drives car two. Fix: say which car was Mickey's.
9. Section 7, the table: Ivy Leadley is the returning fare in the week of 22 October, but the side story starts her Sunday bookings after the sergeant's ride. Fix: put the sergeant's ride in week one of Act II.
10. Section 7, the table: Ron's uncollected wage packet turns up in the week of 19 November, two weeks after he left. Fix: it is the packet from the week of the Fifth, still in the drawer.
11. Scene 15: the gate picking men at six on a Sunday. Fix: Sunday overtime on a ship, named.
12. Section 8: a two-car minicab firm may sit under the 1990 VAT threshold of about £25,000. Fix: one line that Mickey's has been registered since the third shift.
13. Section 10, item 8: putting Shiner on the night gate does not shut a man's mouth by day. Fix: Ron tried a word first and Shiner laughed ("You're on a door, Ron"), so only the man who picks him each morning can hold him.
14. Section 14: Agar's confession is listed as a laughing set piece. Fix: keep only the hat as comic, and name a different third-movement set piece.

## 4. Would I single it out in a review

No, not yet. I would single out two things in it: Ron and Tom in one car, and a mystery solved with a mileometer that ends on a handbrake you must leave alone. But as written the best ending is not won by the game's own verb, the answer shows early, the honest road is thin and the last third is crowded. Fix the first two points and it is a 9.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. The seat. From a walk-round joke and Kevin's knees to "Still where she left it" and Sheila driving the night again without touching it. The best clue here, because the player sits in it for nine hours.
2. The eleven miles. Seven on the dial, four to a bollard, "Eleven and a tenth. I'll allow the tenth", and the last input being nothing.
3. Ron. The lessons at eleven miles an hour, the bench of dockers he fetched to the hearing, the stories that stop one sentence later each time, and which seat he sits in at the end.
