# LEDGER story bible, version 3: a player's review

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8

- Tom: 7. The letter's postscript as his want, the cut pages and the suitcase are strong. Turning the flaw into the small-offers press is clever. The trouble is that once a player twigs it, "take everything" is simply the right answer, so the flaw stops costing anything.
- The cast: 8. Sheila, Ron, Ada, Pauline and Reese are people I would talk about afterwards. There are well over fifty names, though, and a third of them would blur into "the woman at the counter".
- The antagonists: 7. Agar in the rain without a hat, and Danny's yellow cars and his father's window, are excellent. The Widow is better in the back of a cab than she is as a ladder of offers, and Les Coultas and Garbutt are plot carriers, not people.
- The central mystery: 7. It has a real truth, found with my own hands, and it turns Mickey from victim into author. But by the anniversary card (scene 17) I would have guessed it, and Act III then tells it to me five more times.
- The opening and its retold scene: 8. The failed carry that ends with "Nowak, T. £82" in the debt book is the moment I would tell a friend about. Week one is crowded with names and asks, but it has a pull.
- Structure and pace: 7. The rivals' peaks taking turns, and calendar days that drag a slow player forward, give the game a real spine. Act III piles a confirmation on every night and goes slack just where it should tighten.
- The middle: 7. The keeper's road has a strong run of scenes: the Fifth, the lock-up, Remembrance Sunday, the two widows. Between those set pieces there are ten or more hours of fares and counts that the bible barely describes, and the straight road is thin on hands.
- Surprise against cliché: 6. "The kindly old woman is the boss" and "the dead benefactor was the guilty one" are both turns I have met before. Ada's comes with too many arrows pointing at it.
- The endings: 8. Ron's seat in every ending, "Nowak!" called from the wrong side of the name ladder, and "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four" are all earned, and each has a last image.
- The side stories: 8. Ivy and her daughter shielding each other from a thing they both know is the best thing in the bible. The watch, Karen's wardrobe and Hal's tape are whole stories. The mooring is plot information dressed as a side story.
- Tone and humour: 8. Pauline is the game's chorus and it works. Reese's ride-along with the crabs is a proper set piece. A few Ledger lines tip from dry into twee.
- Story and play feeding each other: 8. The hearing, the phone box, the lessons played with the car's own handling, and reading the two books side by side all come out of play, not cutscenes. The last Friday is the weak spot: Tom's hands go idle.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The final truth is guessable by two thirds of the way in, and then it is told over and over.
   What is wrong: by scene 10 the player "knows" Mickey moved the watchman. By the midpoint he knows Mickey found the men. At scene 17 the card proves "somebody wrote a lie in Mickey's book in Mickey's hand". Nobody else writes in Mickey's hand, so every player says "Mickey" right there. Act III then delivers the same truth through the two books (Sunday-to-Monday evening), Agar on Tuesday night, Reese on Wednesday, Les Coultas on Wednesday night ("Said there was an old girl who'd want to think it was her idea"), Reese again on Thursday, and Sheila's page on Friday. Norman's clue in scene 10 also muddles things: "It never come that night" reads as if Mickey failed to move him, not as if he did.
   Where: section 7, scenes 10 and 17; section 8, every day.
   Why a player feels it: the bible promises "one new truth" in the last act. The player already has it, so the last week becomes a run of people confirming his guess. That is the "ending guessed early" complaint the bar warns about.
   Fix: give the lie in the book a credible second author. Cyril carries the real book between the office and Ada's table and has his own tally to protect. Let a small scene show him writing an entry for Mickey once, so the card points at him as much as at Mickey. The fare book's free Saturdays then clear Cyril and convict Mickey in the same evening. In Act III, give each source exactly one new fact: Agar the debt, Les the September visit (cut his "old girl" line), Sheila the confession. Rewrite Norman's line so the kindness is plain: the cab took him home every Saturday that autumn, including that one.

2. The midpoint's human cost falls on a man the player hardly knows, and the cast is too big for its key scenes.
   What is wrong: Terry Hodgson, whose burned hands are the price of the Fifth, is introduced as "nights, four words a shift" and does nothing before he is hurt. Meanwhile the bible names Christine Pickering, Ian Megginson, Gary Mawer, Doreen Harland, Albert Suggitt, Nora Cronin, Sylvia Rennison and dozens more. Remembrance Sunday asks the player to read faces across a full chapel.
   Where: section 4; section 7, the midpoint, beat 3, and scene 13.
   Why a player feels it: I care about Ron pulling Terry out because I care about Ron. I feel nothing for Terry, and June turning away Danny's envelope at his bedside lands on a stranger.
   Fix: give Terry three short beats before the Fifth. A night shift where his four words are always the right four. A small offer of his own (his flask, or the first of his sandwiches). And Pauline's running bit with him on air. Fold the one-scene names into the regulars: let Gordon handle the mooring, Edith the register search, and Lee do Ian Megginson's key work.

3. The straight road is mostly paperwork and talk.
   What is wrong: the keeper gets the ferry rank, the rent round, the chandler's lesson and the lock-up. The player who winds it down gets five rungs (forms, trustees, a hall, a bank account, a list of names), door-knocking, Sheila's £20 and a meeting that half the room walks out of. He shares the stoppage and the lock-up, but his own scenes have almost no hands in them.
   Where: section 7, scenes 1 (S), 3 (S), 9 (S), 14 (S); section 9, The Straight Life.
   Why a player feels it: the bar names this road as the one that must not go thin. A GTA or RDR player who chose to go straight will wonder where the game went somewhere around hour six.
   Fix: give each rung a physical threat from a rival, played with hands. Agar's men break into the school to wreck the hall before the meeting, and Tom and Philip hold it at night. The Widow's men watch the first deposit go to the bank, and Tom has to drive the clean money across the Hook with a tail on him. Danny's lads snatch the society's box on the first payout and Tom chases it down Copper Row. The paperwork stays as the reward each job buys.

4. The small-offers press has a winning strategy, so the flaw never bites.
   What is wrong: every offer is from a friend and taking it is never wrong. Taking pays at the hearing and at the phone box, and paying gains nothing but a Ledger line. A player who has read one guide accepts everything.
   Where: section 3, "The flaw is played"; section 7, scene 5; section 8, Friday.
   Why a player feels it: a choice with a correct answer is not a choice. The game's best idea about Tom turns into a box to tick, and the phone box at the end becomes a reward screen.
   Fix: let some offers carry a hook. Danny's fifty-pound note ("Call it rent"), Garbutt's free read, the Widow's lift to the gate, Agar's apology and his offer to waive a week's rent. Taking those puts Tom in that rival's debt: one of the five phone-box lines goes to them, they call in the favour in Act III, and the town reads him as theirs. Paying should also earn something, such as Sheila's respect in the count, so that a proud player has his own road to "Night, Tom".

5. The climax leaves Tom's hands idle.
   What is wrong: Friday is Sheila handing over the page, five written phone exchanges, and Reese taking statements from whoever turns up before he closes his notebook. It is a careful readout of the campaign, but the player watches it. The real hands climax is Thursday night's car boot, and the game then steps back.
   Where: section 8, Friday; section 15, "Five calls".
   Why a player feels it: after twelve hours of carries, break-ins and fights, the ending arrives as a results screen. The audit is a brave and original climax, and that is why it needs one last thing for the hands.
   Fix: make each answered call set Tom a short job across the Hook before ten. Fetch Edith's folder from the pension counter. Drive Ron to the gate hut for the sheets. Get Ivy to chapel so Ellis can be found there. Hold the office door while the Widow's man waits outside. With five coins and three hours, which errands he can physically reach is the climax. Then Reese closes the notebook on what Tom actually carried in.

## 3. Would I single this story out

No, not yet, though it is close. I would tell a friend about scenes. Ron at eleven miles an hour asking to go round again. The third lesson, when he is quiet and his hands are bandaged. The burning car lighting Ada's window with the tallyman standing cap in hand behind her. Two widows in the back of a minicab with the meter running. Reese and the crabs. "Nowak!" at the end. Those are better scenes than most games have. But a friend who plays these games for the plot would guess the big turn at the anniversary and spot the old woman at the window long before the Fifth. He would then sit through a last week that keeps confirming what he guessed. Fix the guessability and the over-telling, give the straight road its own action, and make Terry somebody, and I would say yes without thinking.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron and Tom: the passenger seat, the three lessons, the empty seat on the radio ("Car one, you on your own?"), and which vehicle and which seat he is in at every ending. It is the Henry and Hans of this game, and it is built for the hands, not for cutscenes.
2. The fare book as the true book: Mickey's plainest record turning out to be the only one that never lied, read by the player page against page and then by a mild man from Customs. "Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak" is the line of the game.
3. The town as the account of Tom: Sheila's withheld name, the name ladder from "Mickey's nephew" to "Go on, Tommy" and "Night, Tom", the hearing where only the people he let help him will stand, and Ivy and her daughter quietly guarding each other.
