# NOTES-B-ORAL: British dockers, oral history and working life

Dated 21 November 2026. Reader: research subagent. All sources read via WebFetch on 21 Nov 2026 (the tool returns a summary of the page, not the raw page; so none counts as read in full by hand). Short quotes only. Pub, drink, betting and childhood material skipped by rule.

## Questions
1. The hiring: call-on, pen, tally or brass, button, blue-eyed boys, stood off, fall-back pay, foreman's power; after 1967; casual hiring after 1989.
2. The work and its words: gangs, hooks, hatch, tally clerks, checkers, lightermen, special payments, spelling, nicknames, cargoes, injuries, tools, clothing, food, superstition, names for managers.

## Findings

### Q1 Hiring
- "Blue-eyed boys" were the favoured men picked for the better-paying jobs at the call, and were widely resented. Source: Hull repository article, https://hull-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/4226937 (read 21 Nov 2026).
- Before 1939 the "tinpot system" hired men at the gangway end of the ship. Under the National Dock Labour Scheme men gathered at purpose-built halls called "controls" at each dock, held their dockers' books, and competed at the morning call. Men missed were often moved to another dock or paid a retainer. Same source, read 21 Nov 2026.
- A retainer, spelled "dint" money in the source, went to men not hired at the morning call, who were expected back for the lunchtime call. The spelling is odd and may be a mis-rendering of a dialect word; treat as UNVERIFIED. Same source.
- Waiting in "pens" for the foreman's call; when the union expelled Harry Constable he lost his registration and the men in the pens refused to go out until he was given work (period: before the 1950s). https://www.socialistparty.org.uk/?p=26268 (read 21 Nov 2026).
- Before the 1942 wartime scheme, gangs queued at the dock gates each day; the 1947 scheme made sacking much harder. Relatives had preferential entry: one man started at the Royal Group of Docks in 1960 after his father nominated him. https://isj.org.uk/memories-of-a-marxist-docker/ (read 21 Nov 2026).
- Registered dockworkers got permanent employment in 1967; afterwards employers held discipline directly and absenteeism drives brought more strife. Thesis text via https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/5634/1/403287.pdf (read 21 Nov 2026; only first 100,000 of about 830,000 characters reached).
- Shipowners kept about 2,000 paid dockers in reserve to cover holiday periods; "most of the time you didn't get to go to work" (pre-reform London). https://spitalfieldslife.com/2012/03/21/colin-ross-docker/ (read 21 Nov 2026).
- 1989: casual hiring returned once the scheme ended; the abolition bill was announced 6 April 1989, men struck in July and returned in August; 500 Liverpool dockers were sacked. https://www.adamsmith.org/blog/no-more-docker-jobs-for-life (read 21 Nov 2026). This is a free-market think tank, so its framing is hostile to the men. No docker's own words on casual hiring returning were found.

### Q2 The work and its words
- Gangs chose their own ganger; a ganger was like a foreman but accountable to the men. Gangs covered for a man needing time off or helped fix a broken-down car. "Session money" was extra pay for covering a barge. Piecework made up for day pay that was below unemployment benefit. https://isj.org.uk/memories-of-a-marxist-docker/ (read 21 Nov 2026). The same man says of the 1989 ballots, "They wore us down with the ballots."
- Bristol (Avonmouth) nicknames with reasons, from a museum oral-history page: https://bristolfloatingharbour.org.uk/oral-history/dockers-nicknames (read 21 Nov 2026). The page is paraphrased by the reader tool; reasons are as the tool reported them.
  1. Manhole: fell down a ship's hold.
  2. Mile'r: swam across Avonmouth Docks.
  3. Flags: knocked down special bunting from a ship with a crane.
  4. Dictator: from a near-miss when a man named Richard was almost caught stealing potatoes (a pilferage-linked name).
  5. Preacher: worked every Sunday.
  6. The Bat: worked nights, never days.
  7. Zoomy: always left early.
  8. Tracker: rode trains without paying.
  9. Lord Ash: always smoking.
  10. Odd ha'ppenny: always asked for a halfpenny to make up a penny.
  11. Splinter: son of a man called Plank. Pebbles: son of Stoneface.
  12. Twiggy: surname was Branch. Mouse catcher: father was a rat catcher.
  13. Two note Bill: a superintendent who paid low wages for unpleasant work.
  14. Dick Barton: a docks policeman.
  15. Steady Eddie: drove a tractor very slowly. Kipper: constantly napping.
  The page says men often knew each other only by nickname, and names could be witty or cruel.
- Hooks: "all the other dockers always had hooks", used in the holds to pull bags out. East Hull memories, https://maritimehull.co.uk/whats-happening/blogs/stories-remembering-the-docks-stories-from-east-hull (read 21 Nov 2026). Same page: cargo from West Africa c1969 included logs, cocoa beans and palm kernels; crawling between wagons with engines running; no safety helmets; ice and snow the worst conditions; during a strike a family lived on banana sandwiches, "a loaf of bread and a banana" for five. A signal "Lamp Lad", nicknamed Aladdin, climbed signals over 50 ft to trim and light lamps.
- Asbestos in hessian sacks; the union refused it in 1967 at the Royal London Docks. Red lead loaded loose and sacks of starch were reader comments, not the article. Spitalfields Life page above.
- Deal porters at Surrey Commercial Docks wore special leather headwear to protect the neck, and a gang took hourly rests. Lightermen piloted small boats from ship to shore; tally clerks recorded weights (tea, 1951 photo caption). https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/jobs-that-made-londons-docks-run/ (read 21 Nov 2026).
- Not found on pages read: hatch, checkers, welt or spelling, tea and food at work, superstitions, names for managers (bar "Two note Bill"). UNVERIFIED from memory only; not used.

### Q3 Mutual aid
- The London Distress Fund, which the subject's father helped set up, paid a widow 75 pounds when a docker died. Dockers had no sick pay or injury compensation; accident rate highest in industry; average life expectancy about 58 to 60 in the 1960s (the page's claim). Spitalfields Life, read 21 Nov 2026. How it was collected is not stated.
- Nothing found on funeral customs, the cap going round, or dividing societies. Not reached.

### Q4 Pilferage
- Described by one thesis as "the clearest violation of the criminal law", with prison and removal from the register possible; also employers' anxiety to clear ships linked to improper side-payments. LJMU thesis URL above (partial read). The "Dictator" nickname above (potatoes) is the only vernacular touch. Vocabulary (such as "totting" or "perks") not found on any page read: UNVERIFIED.

### Q5 1989 as lived
- Redundancy offered: up to 35,000 pounds (Adam Smith Institute); 36,000 pounds tax-free (ISJ docker). Hull article: industrial peace in August 1989 and hundreds took redundancy and left. Sources above.
- Officials repeated strike ballots under the new law until they got the wanted result; militants including the interviewee were sacked. ISJ page.
- A London docker who left in 1978 for a container plant called leaving "the saddest day of my life"; pay went from 30 to 350 pounds a week. This is 1978, not 1989, useful for tone only. Spitalfields Life.
- Not found: minicab or taxi work, men who never worked again, passing the gate, wives' accounts.

### Q6 Strange and specific
- Docks police, superintendents and a nickname for each: the police officer called "Dick Barton" (Bristol page).
- Pass book from the National Dock Labour Board as the object a man carried (Spitalfields Life photo).
- Union lamps and signals: Lamp Lad detail above.
