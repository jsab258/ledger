# NOTES-D-ORAL-2: dockers' oral histories, second pass

All pages read 21 Nov 2026 through the fetch tool (a small model summarises each page, so every quotation below is as that tool returned it and is not checked against the page itself). The second pass is thin on the 1989 end and on hiring slang. Most pages turned out to be abstracts, intros or other eras.

## a. Hiring in the men's own words

- Favouritism at the call. J.D., a Liverpool docker: "the blue-eyed system where they used to give you the tap on the shoulder". Source: https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/332951/1/Greig%20Taylor%27s%20thesis%20in%20one%20document.pdf (read 21 Nov 2026).
- The leftovers. J.D., same thesis: "we used to get what was left after everyone else had been hired". Men who got only that work: "We were known as the 'strags'". (Same URL, read 21 Nov 2026.)
- Allocation to employers. J.D.: "You were allocated to various employers" and "It wasn't done on a fair basis." (Same URL.)
- The morning "call" is named as such by David Walker in his Glasgow piece. https://core.ac.uk/download/9036103.pdf (read 21 Nov 2026). Walker writes that "More often fathers vouched for sons" so they were admitted to the union.
- "Book" is defined in the Liverpool thesis as a docker's registration with the National Dock Labour Board. Clerical staff gave up their books to take a staff job. (Same MMU URL.) "TUR" is the Temporary Unattached Register.
- A Liverpool interviewee, E.R., said dockers were thought to be asked in job interviews about mortgages and cars, so the firm could judge if they had "too much to lose". (Same MMU URL; second-hand report of an interview question.)
- Pen, stand, tally, brass, disc, attendance money: NOT found on any page reached.

## b. The 1989 end as lived

- Not found in the men's words on any page reached.
- Turnbull and Wass (1994), https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/095001709484001 (read 21 Nov 2026, abstract only): the scheme was abolished in July 1989. Mass redundancy of former registered dockers, "especially trade union activists", let employers restructure. They say this ended what had been called the "greatest game in the world". Title: "The Greatest Game no more: Redundant Dockers and the Demise of 'Dock Work'".
- Hull, from the port boss's side. Yorkshire Post, 13 Jun 2018, https://www.pressreader.com/uk/yorkshire-post/20180613/281651075812186 (read 21 Nov 2026). Mike Fell (ran the port to 2003): "it was over in three weeks". Norman Fowler called the scheme "a total anachronism". Fell said the old board's shop stewards "had the upper hand". Fell recalled being able to ask managers to drive a consignment of Lada cars off a Russian ship, which "would have been a criminal offence until then". Alexandra Dock reopened in 1991. These are management words, not dockers'.
- Hull thesis, https://hull-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/4223573 (read 21 Nov 2026, first 100,000 characters of 500,000 only): abolition "effectively terminated the docking occupation and removed many dock workers from the industry". The author interviewed ten ex-dockers and stewards but the intro quotes none.
- Liverpool thesis (earlier period, severance in the 1970s-80s): J.D. on voluntary severance, "It was like becoming millionaires overnight". J.D. on urging mates to refuse it: "We'd encourage lads not to take the cash because we could see the jobs disappearing". Useful tone for a man who sees jobs go. (Same MMU URL.)
- Jobs afterwards, agency hiring, the gate: NOT found.

## c. Nicknames

Inkl, https://www.inkl.com/news/reasons-behind-the-eye-wateringly-funny-nicknames-dockers-gave-each-other (read 21 Nov 2026). All Liverpool, from the Liverpool Echo as relayed. Page gives nothing for Hull, London or Glasgow. Reasons as given:
- The bird doctor: always says the job is poor.
- The London fog: never does the lifting.
- The lazy solicitor: dozes on the job.
- Cinderella: clocks off just before twelve.
- The sheriff: keeps asking where the hold-up is.
- The clergyman: never gets a Sunday off.
- Swan Vesta: always on strike.
- Lord Nelson: warns others to watch for the boss.
- Storm lamp: never switches off.
- Sell the bed: always on nights.
- The destroyer: chases extra payments.
- Blood donor: very pale.
- Diesel fitter: opens crates joking about who the contents will suit.
- Lino: often found lying on the floor.
- Mints: never in before eight.
- The broken boomerang: never returns.
- The man in black: always off to a funeral.
- The mangy cat: banned from every yard.
- The vicar: shouts down the hatch to get the crew's attention.
- The blister: turns up when the hard work is done.
- Also: "strags" (Liverpool, the men left with the leftover work, MMU thesis) and "the friendly doo" (Glasgow, recalled by Owen McIntyre, per Walker). "None-ers" is Liverpool slang for clerical staff with no union membership. Clerical jobs were seen as "sitting on radiators" (M.C.).
- Skipped: nicknames that rest on drinking or family size.

## d. Mutual aid

- Almost nothing. J.D., Liverpool: "you wouldn't scab out the fella who lives next door to you". Walker: "many of the men knew each other as workmates". L.D.: "you never starved on the docks". No sums, collections, widows or funerals found.

## e. Work words and objects

- Bull rope: a superintendent stevedore describes hauling tractors "over the bin with a bull rope" (Walker, core.ac.uk).
- Glasgow jobs named: checker, crane driver, winch operator.
- "A good stevedore was worth his weight in gold" (L.D., Liverpool). "You serve your time on the quay" and then aspire to "go down below" (J.D.), meaning working in the hold.
- Holdsman levels kept after containers made them unnecessary (T.T., Seaforth, 1972).
- Stuffing and stripping (container packing and unpacking); break-bulk (loose cargo); groupage; counter-offs, wharfingers, timekeepers, ship's planners, quay foremen (grades). The "five-mile" rule: L.D. said dock work was "anything within... a five mile radius".
- Dirty money, whistles, hooks, tea, superstition: NOT found. The LJMU thesis (https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/5634/1/403287.pdf, first 100,000 of about 830,000 characters read 21 Nov 2026) paraphrases side payments: former London managers said employers paid dockers extra to get ships away before berthing charges built up. It also says a threatened downing of tools was often enough to bring employers to heel. Both are paraphrase, not quotation.

## f. Strange and specific

- The old stereotype of dockers leaving with pockets full of cargo (Yorkshire Post).
- Lada cars driven off a Russian ship at Hull after 1989.
- A steward asking Jack Jones, "What about the boy?" (a father asking about his son's place in the trade; MMU thesis).
- A New York firm lost its licence for giving gang men a Christmas turkey (LJMU thesis, paraphrase; American, so not usable as British fact).
