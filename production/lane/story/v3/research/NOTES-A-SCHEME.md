## Q1. What the Scheme was

- Set up 1947 by the Attlee government as the Dock Workers (Regulation of Employment) Scheme; run by the National Dock Labour Board; local boards had equal numbers of worker and employer representatives; about 150 firms bound by it; Board records held at Cardiff University. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Dock_Labour_Board (read 21 Nov 2026). The Spectator adds it grew out of Ernest Bevin's 1940 scheme and reserved dock work for registered dockers with guaranteed minimum pay. https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/5th-august-1989/16/the-docks-turned-upside-down (read 21 Nov 2026)
- Laid-off registered dockers were promised other work or a £25,000 pay-off (Wikipedia page above). The Adam Smith Institute (a free-market think tank, advocacy not neutral history) says jobs could pass to sons and pay was well above non-scheme rates. https://www.adamsmith.org/blog/no-more-docker-jobs-for-life (read 21 Nov 2026)
- Devlin 1967: the Dock Workers (Regulation of Employment) (Amendment) Order 1967 (S.I. 1967 No. 1252) gave effect to the 1965 Devlin Report. Order dated 18 August 1967, laid 22 August, start date 18 September 1967 (an earlier 15 September was first announced). Aim, in MP Nicholas Scott's words: "decasualise the system of dock labour and give permanent employment to dock workers." A "no redundancy" pledge underpinned it; cuts were to come by wastage or voluntary severance. Scheme labour force about 60,000, early-1970s need about 40,000. https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1967/nov/29/dock-workers-regulation-of-employment (read 21 Nov 2026; last 23,000 characters not read)
- Government case in 1989 (Fowler, Second Reading 17 April 1989): statutory monopoly enforced by criminal penalties; restrictive practices such as paying men to stand by; outdated demarcation; cost to consumers and taxpayer (almost £1 billion combined, taxpayer and industry). Strikes 1967 to 1987: 3,569 involving registered dockers, over 4 million days lost. Days lost 1985 to 1987: 528 per 1,000 employees in scheme ports against 183 across industry. https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1989/apr/17/dock-work-bill (read 21 Nov 2026; first 100,000 of 309,000 characters only)
- Opposition case (Meacher): productivity up about 700% in 20 years (730% at Second Reading); scheme administration cost about 1.5p a tonne of a £7 to £15 handling charge; abolition risks return of casual work. https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1989/apr/06/dock-labour-scheme (read 21 Nov 2026)
- Which ports: 6 April 1989 statement says the scheme covered 40 main ports and 35 ports stood outside it; non-scheme ports had about 4,000 dockers (up about a third in 10 years); Felixstowe and Dover named as big non-scheme ports where casual working was "effectively non-existent". Same source. At Second Reading: scheme ports handled over 90% of trade 20 years before, about 70% in 1989; non-scheme about 30% by volume and half by value; Felixstowe committed £54m for 1989 and £92m over six years; Dover nearly £85m (17 April source above). Hansard 16 May 1989: non-scheme share of UK market rose from 10% (1967) to nearly 30% (1987). https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1989/may/16/dock-labour-scheme (read 21 Nov 2026)
- Why it mattered: non-scheme ports could hire and fire and take new trade; scheme ports lost share (sources above). The ASI claim that "ports lost trade because of its costs" is advocacy.
- Register size: 71,846 (1959); 45,542 (1970); 27,000 ten years before 1989; 9,530 in 1988 (Howson); 9,400 on 6 April 1989; 9,310 (16 May 1989 answer, total for all scheme ports; the same answer elsewhere says 9,319; they differ slightly). Age 1987: 693 aged 25 to 35, 3,200 aged 35 to 45, 6,408 over 45 (16 May source).

## Q2. The 1989 timeline

All lines from the Bristol Radical History Group article unless another source is shown. https://www.brh.org.uk/site/articles/abolition-of-the-national-dock-labour-scheme/ (read 21 Nov 2026)

- 6 April: Norman Fowler, Secretary of State for Employment, announces a White Paper and Bill to abolish the scheme (Commons, 3.31 pm). Compensation: "up to £35,000 until early 1991, and up to £20,000 in the following 18 months"; the top sum for 15 years' service, not a flat £35,000; cost shared equally by Government and employer (Hansard 6 April). Fowler called the scheme "a total anachronism" (Wikipedia, Q1).
- 7 April: unofficial walkout; nearly 500 Bristol dockers leave after a mass meeting. 10 April: called off after pressure from the national shop stewards committee.
- 14 April: TGWU executive backs Ron Todd's "all lawful means". 17 April: Dock Work Bill Second Reading (Hansard above). 20 April: talks with the National Association of Port Employers collapse; national ballot gives a three-to-one vote for a strike; employers argue the strike is political and so unlawful.
- High Court rules the strike legal (date not given); Court of Appeal grants employers an injunction. 8 June: 24-hour protest strikes at Bristol and several ports. 12 June: Bristol votes to continue unofficially pending a Lords appeal. 19 June: return to work pending the appeal.
- 3 July: Act passed and in force the same day. Dock Work Act 1989 (c. 13), long title "An Act to abolish the Dock Workers Employment Scheme 1967"; section 1: scheme and the 1946 Act "cease to have effect on the date of the passing of this Act"; local boards cease; section 8(3) in force on passing; section 5 compensation scheme by regulations. https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1989/13/enacted (read 21 Nov 2026). Hansard gives 3 July as abolition date: https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1989/jul/24/dock-workers (read 21 Nov 2026). Bristol Port Authority issued new contracts that day.
- Early July: the Lords appeal succeeds but 28 days had passed, so a fresh ballot was needed. 7 July: second ballot, three-to-one for strike. 10 July: strike begins.
- 21 July: 2,288 registered dockers made redundant or given notice, 25% of the register at 3 July (Hansard 24 July above).
- 24 and 31 July: Bristol Labour councillors split over backing the strike, then urge a return to work. 31 July: Hull docks, strike crumbling, dockers back despite Liverpool pickets; TGWU shifting to fight over men sacked during the strike (Brian Barker, TGWU Hull). https://learningonscreen.ac.uk/lbc/search/index.php/segment/0008700342003 (read 21 Nov 2026; audio summary only)
- 1 August: TGWU Executive calls off the strike. 7 August: Bristol dockers back. Final vote to return held at a meeting in Liverpool; Ron Todd said the union had little choice. https://learningonscreen.ac.uk/lbc/search/index.php/segment/0008900462017 (read 21 Nov 2026; summary only). Spectator 12 Aug 1989: Todd "the general (as he said) without an army"; Fowler faced down the strike "without calling in the police". https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/12th-august-1989/17/strikes-docked (read 21 Nov 2026)
- Liverpool the last port back; a Leeds thesis says "after a six-week strike" (https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/1137/1/thesis_MCL_2010.pdf, read 21 Nov 2026, parts only). That does not fit a 10 July start and early August end (about four weeks); treat "six weeks" as UNVERIFIED. ASI says about 500 Liverpool dockers were sacked (advocacy source; UNVERIFIED elsewhere). The ASI sequence (abolition after strike) is wrong against Hansard.
- Numbers: 9,221 on the register; by 13 October 1989, 3,938 made redundant; Department contributed £78,760,050; about £90 million for 1989 to 90. https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1989/oct/18/dock-workers-redundancies (read 21 Nov 2026). By 25 Jan 1990: 4,318 redundancies, £84.5 million compensation, 11 employers insolvent with 868 redundancies; over 90% of claims in first three months. https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1990/jan/25/dock-workers (read 21 Nov 2026). Total 1989 to 90 payments £150,077,500. https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1995/oct/27/dock-labour-force (read 21 Nov 2026). By 1992 about 7,200 ex-registered dockers redundant or applied (Howson, Q3); more than 80% took severance (Leeds thesis, citing Turnbull 2000).
- Years of service: only the 15-year top rate (£35,000 then £20,000) is confirmed. A full table by years of service was not found: UNVERIFIED. Howson says initial payments were £35,000, reducing in stages to June 1992.

## Q3. After, 1989 to 1992

- Casual and temporary work returned: labour-only supply firms, pools of redundant dockers on call, short-term hires; about 20% of redundant dockers back as temporary labour by 1994; in London temporary men were mostly ex-permanent staff on worse terms. Employment in British ports fell from 136,000 (1968) to 25,000 (1998). Wages before abolition: average registered docker nearly £150 a week above other manual workers (1988). Howson MPhil, Greenwich, 2004; https://core.ac.uk/download/9657624.pdf (read 21 Nov 2026; first 100,000 of 425,000 characters only)
- New agencies: Torside in Liverpool (some workers' co-operatives, some agreed by TGWU and employers). By October 1992 Associated British Ports recognised no unions except for marine pilots. Employers had pledged (93% of registered men's employers) not to return to casual work (16 May 1989 source above); that pledge sits against the 20% figure.
- Tilbury: 102 dockers dismissed in 1989; Port of London Authority later paid £1,028,000 compensation; tribunal system cost estimated £376,000 (Hansard, 2 July 1992). https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1992/jul/02/dockers-dismissal (read 21 Nov 2026). Case name Port of London Authority v Payne (1993, Court of Appeal) appeared in search results but its page was not read: UNVERIFIED. Leeds thesis: unionised dockers removed "by buying them out or by sacking them as in the Port of Tilbury".
- Port of London Authority (Thames, Hansard 13 Dec 1991): 445 registered dockers at abolition; 183 redundant 1989, 18 in 1990, 192 in 1991. Grimsby and Immingham 498 redundant 1989, 24 in 1990, 10 in 1991. Lindsey Dock Services (106 staff) and John Sutcliffe Consolidated Stevedores (226 staff) collapsed; their workers' compensation was £3,028,000 and £7,272,000. https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1991/dec/13/dock-labour-scheme (read 21 Nov 2026)
- Costs: abolition cost over £250 million all-in (Turnbull, Woolfson et al, 1992, as cited by the Leeds thesis); taxpayer £420 million severance since early 1970s plus £350 million support to scheme ports (Fowler, 6 April).
- Containerisation: Leeds thesis only notes containers cut dock employment from the 1960s and Seaforth container terminal opened 1972. Gang sizes and tonnage per man: not found, UNVERIFIED.
- Marinas, flats, retail, development corporations: not found in pages read. UNVERIFIED.
- Ex-dockers as taxi or minicab drivers: searched twice; found nothing. Honest result: no source supports it, so it must be marked invented if used.
- What dockers did with the money: no source found.
