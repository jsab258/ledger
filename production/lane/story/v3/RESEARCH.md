## Questions asked

1. What was the Scheme, and exactly how and when did it end?
2. What happened to the men and the old ports in 1989 to 1990?
3. What did dock life sound like from inside (hiring, words, names, mutual aid)?
4. What did a port town and a cab office of 1990 run on (law, money, weather, coins)?

## 1. The Scheme and its end (firm: Hansard, the Act)

- 1947: dock work reserved for registered dockers, run by a national board and local boards with men and employers in equal numbers. 18 September 1967: casual hiring ended; every registered man got a permanent employer, under a "no redundancy" pledge. A man's registration was his "book".
- 40 ports were in the Scheme and 35 outside. The ports outside (Felixstowe, Dover) took the container and ferry trade: Scheme ports fell from over 90% of trade to about 70% in twenty years.
- The register: 71,846 men in 1959; 45,542 in 1970; about 9,400 in April 1989; 6,408 of them were over 45.
- 6 April 1989: the minister announces abolition, calling the Scheme "a total anachronism". Severance "up to £35,000 until early 1991, and up to £20,000 in the following 18 months", the top sum for 15 years' service.
- 3 July 1989: the Dock Work Act passes and the Scheme ends that day. 10 July: the strike starts after two ballots and a court fight ("They wore us down with the ballots"). 1 August: called off. A Hull port manager's memory: "it was over in three weeks."
- By 21 July 1989, a quarter of the register had been made redundant or given notice. By 13 October, 3,938 men. By November 1990, port by port: Hull 572 (85%), Bristol 419 (87%), Tees 454 (93%), Goole 135 (82%), Garston 108 (100%), Manchester, Dundee and Great Yarmouth 100%. Eleven employers went insolvent, taking 868 men's jobs with them. At Tilbury 102 men were dismissed in the strike; it cost the port £1,028,000 at tribunal years later.

So in 1990 a middling old port had lost four in five of its dockers inside sixteen months, each going with a cheque, and the top rate of that cheque had a closing date: early 1991.

## 2. After (fair: two theses, Hansard)

- Casual work came back at once, against the employers' pledge: labour supply firms, pools of redundant dockers on call, short hires. About one in five redundant dockers was back as temporary labour by 1994, on worse terms. Port jobs in Britain: 136,000 in 1968, 25,000 in 1998.
- Things that had been crimes under the Scheme became ordinary. At Hull, managers themselves drove a shipment of Lada cars off a Russian ship; before July 1989 that was an offence.
- Development corporations were buying old dock land by the hectare in 1989 to 1990 (Teesside 237 hectares in the year, Merseyside 57).
- Not found, though looked for twice: ex-dockers going into cab driving, what men did with the money, marinas, warehouse fires. If the story uses any of these it is invention and is marked so.

## 3. Dock life from inside (thin: summaries of oral histories)

- The call: men competed at the morning call in halls called controls; before that, in pens. The foreman's favourites were "blue-eyed boys" ("the blue-eyed system where they used to give you the tap on the shoulder"). Men who got what was left were "strags". A man missed at the call was paid a small retainer and told to come back at dinner time.
- Gangs chose their own ganger and covered for a man who needed time off. "You serve your time on the quay" before you "go down below". "You wouldn't scab out the fella who lives next door to you." Fathers vouched for sons: "What about the boy?"
- Hooks for bags in the hold; no helmets; ice the worst; asbestos in hessian sacks refused by the men in 1967; deal porters with leather over the neck. Clerks were said to be "sitting on radiators".
- Men were often known only by nickname, witty or cruel, and each has its reason. Bristol: Manhole (fell down a hold), Flags (took the bunting off a ship with a crane), The Bat (nights only), Zoomy (left early), Lord Ash (always smoking), Odd ha'penny, Splinter (son of Plank), Two note Bill (a mean superintendent), Dick Barton (a dock policeman). Liverpool: Cinderella (off just before twelve), The sheriff ("where's the hold-up?"), Swan Vesta (always on strike), The London fog (never lifts), The man in black (always off to a funeral), The broken boomerang (never comes back), The blister (turns up when the work is done), Sell the bed (always on nights).
- Mutual aid: one London fund paid a widow £75 on a docker's death. Dockers had no sick pay; the accident rate was the worst in industry. Nothing was reached on funerals, collections or dividing clubs.
- On severance in earlier rounds: "It was like becoming millionaires overnight", and the stewards' answer: "We'd encourage lads not to take the cash because we could see the jobs disappearing."

## 4. The town and the cab office in 1990 (firm on law and dates)

- A minicab operator outside London held a council licence under the 1976 Act. Section 56: before every journey he must enter the booking in a record in the form the council prescribes, including jobs passed to another firm, and must show it on demand to a council officer or a constable. Every fare goes in the book, by law, and the police may read it.
- The ambulance dispute ran from 7 September 1989 to 23 February 1990: crews answered emergencies only from November, and the army drove ambulances in eighteen areas.
- 25 January 1990, the Burns' Day storm: 47 dead, half a million homes without power, telephone lines down, ports shut.
- The poll tax began in England and Wales on 1 April 1990; a bill a head, set by each council; riots in March; mass non-payment and summonses through the year.
- Bank base rate 15% until October 1990, then 14%.
- 27 June 1990: the small 5p coin arrives. The old large one stays legal until 31 December, so all autumn there are two sizes of five pence in every till and tin.
- Petrol £1.85 a gallon and a tabloid 20p to 22p (1989).

## 5. What the story can take from this

1. The ending world has a date and a price. Abolition, 3 July 1989; the cheque, up to £35,000; the top rate closing in early 1991. A story set in the autumn of 1990 sits between the two: most men gone, a few holding their books, the cheque about to shrink.
2. The pen comes back. Casual hiring returning at the gate is the old world's worst thing returning as the new one. Whoever picks the men owns the Hook.
3. A debt book in nicknames. If men were known only by nickname, a dead man's book of debts could name nobody the law or a stranger can find: The Blister, Sell the Bed, Cinderella. Tom would have to learn the Hook to read it. (The practice is real; using it in a debt book is ours.)
4. The fare book is a legal record a constable can demand. A true line in it is evidence; a false one is an offence.
5. The winter with no ambulances. From November 1989 to February 1990 a cab office may well have carried the sick (an inference, not found stated). The story's old night can sit in that winter.
6. Two sizes of five pence. Any count of coin in autumn 1990 holds old and new money together.
7. Yesterday's crime is today's job. Managers driving cargo off a ship, lawful since July: what the men were for is now done by anyone.
8. The words: blue-eyed, strag, the book, the tap on the shoulder, down below, sitting on radiators.
