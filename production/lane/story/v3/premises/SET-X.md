# Premise set X (story v3, phase 1)

Six premises that start from the dead man: what Mickey did, what he owed, and what his book really is.

## X1. The Wrong Column

The top severance rate closes in early 1991 and the last registered men on the Hook are signing; the player meets it on his first fare, at the dock gate, where a man with a £35,000 cheque in his coat is left in the pen as a strag. Mickey's book lists a hundred and forty nicknames with a sum against each, and Tom takes it for money owed to the office. The question is who owes whom. Ron Kirby works the doors with him, Sheila Dunn banks what comes in, and the Widow, who holds a note on the office, is paid weekly from the takings. Tom collects with his hands, by threat, break-in and a beating on a stairwell, and then drives the same men, because they still need a cab. In week three he breaks the wrist of The London Fog, who keeps saying Mickey had his already. The truth comes in the cab, from Cinderella: the sums are deposits. Mickey was the Hook's bank, and put every cheque into an option on the old quay, against Geoffrey Agar. Tom has been robbing his own creditors. One line, Sell the Bed, is Ron's cheque, and Tom's choice to clear the Widow's note that week spent it; Ron goes to stand in the pen, and does not come back. Later the men Tom squeezed hardest decide whether the depositors trust him, and the fares he drove for nothing speak for him. At the end Tom sells the option to Agar and pays everyone out short, or holds the quay for the men and owes them for life.

Canon notes: adds that Mickey held dockers' severance as an informal bank and bought an option on dock land; adds a note the Widow holds on the office; gives Ron the nickname Sell the Bed and a severance cheque (he was a docker until 1989).

## X2. The Honest Book

After July 1989 half the Hook's crimes became somebody's job, and the other half are being arrested ahead of the land sale. The player meets it in week one: DS Carol Ellis walks in, asks for the fare book, reads it standing, and that night Tom's first fare is lifted where he was dropped. The question is what Tom owes a bargain he did not make. Sheila Dunn writes every booking before the journey; Ron Kirby holds the door; Darren Milner drives the second car; Ellis comes every Monday. The outfit's work is carrying: men to jobs, parcels off the docks, a getaway for Cammack, a night run for the Widow, with the break-ins and fights that go with them. What a fare says in the back, and where he gets out, is the deepest talk in the game and also a line in ink. Arrests follow the cab through the middle, bigger each week, until the firms want a name. Darren has been seen in Ellis's car. Tom beats him behind the rank and gives him to Keith Garbutt. The truth is that nobody talks. Mickey's bargain was only that the book be honest and that Ellis read it. The fares the player chose in weeks two and three were the evidence. Tom tells Sheila to write one false line. She does, her first, and Ellis proves it; Sheila is charged as keeper of the book and does not come back to her desk. On the last night the Widow and Cammack both book cars. Tom writes that page himself, true or blank.

Canon notes: adds that Mickey let the police read the fare book weekly as the price of staying open; makes Darren Milner a Suddaby driver; assumes a false entry is an offence charged to whoever keeps the book.

## X3. Booked in Advance

Mickey knew his heart, and in his last week he wrote seven weeks of bookings into the fare book: a nickname, a kerb, a minute, a destination. The player meets the end of the Hook through them, because Mickey knew the town by its habits and the habits are stopping. In week one everyone stands where the book says; each week more kerbs are empty. The question is whether Tom owes a dead man obedience. Sheila Dunn reads out the day's page without comment; Ron Kirby rides along when a line smells of trouble; Ada, the neighbour, is every Thursday, window to cemetery and back, and says more in the cab than anyone living. Some lines are jobs, a parcel off a boat for the Widow, and Tom does them blind, with his fists. In week three Cinderella is not at the gate at ten to twelve. Tom blames Danny Cammack's crew and puts one of their drivers through a shop front with the cab. The truth is that Cinderella cashed his cheque and left on the morning train; nothing happened to him but the end. Cammack's answer comes on a Thursday in week five that Tom has given to the Widow's parcel: Ada sets out on foot, is knocked down by a car looking for his, and goes to a home inland. Her window stays dark. The town remembers which empty kerbs he waited at and which lines he struck out. The last booking reads T. Nowak, office to station, one suitcase. He drives it, or writes the next line in his own hand.

Canon notes: adds that Mickey knew he was dying and wrote bookings weeks ahead (his death is still his heart); Ada leaves the map in week five; one car injury to a Cammack driver and one to Ada, neither fatal.

## X4. The Burial Club

The old dockers are dying faster now there is no work to get up for, and the player meets it on his first morning: a widow at the counter with a card stamped paid every week since 1953. Mickey's book is the Quay Street burial club, two hundred and twelve men, each promised a hearse, two cars and a stone, where the dockers' fund gives £75. The tin holds forty-one pounds, in both sizes of 5p. Tom owes two hundred funerals. Ron Kirby has carried sixty coffins; Sheila Dunn stamps the cards; Father Walsh counts the cars; The Man in Black, who attends every funeral on the Hook, marks each out of ten, aloud. Every funeral costs a crime: a hearse lent by the Widow against a night's smuggling, a stone lifted from the mason's yard, a debt beaten out of a living man to bury a dead one. The cab is the second car, and the bereaved talk in it. The withdrawals are initialled B.W., so Tom breaks into the presbytery and shames Father Walsh. B.W. is black weeks, the weeks without work, when Mickey stamped the cards himself. There was never a fund; the outfit existed to pay for funerals. Three come in week five, and Tom sends Ron alone to settle with Cammack's crew over a hearse. Ron is killed. His card is paid up. The Man in Black has kept score of every corner Tom cut, and the Hook turns out for Ron, or does not, by that score. Afterwards Tom closes the club with the dock, or stamps a card in his own name.

Canon notes: adds a burial club run from the office and makes it the reason for Mickey's outfit; adds one docker known as The Man in Black; Ron Kirby dies in week five or six.

## X5. Forty Men

With the dock land going by the hectare, the three firms are dividing what is left, and the player meets it at once: Keith Garbutt brings an envelope, Mickey's share, paid by all three to keep Suddaby's forty men off the quay. There are no forty men. The book of uncollectable debts is the roster, forty nicknames Sheila Dunn made up from dockers who left or died. Mickey ran no crime in eleven years; he ran a rumour, and it kept the peace. Nobody tells Tom. The question is what he owes a lie that fed three people. Sheila wrote it, Ron Kirby is its one real fist, Garbutt carries its money, and Darren Milner wants to join it. Collecting, Tom finds a real man called The London Fog and beats a payment out of him, and the man pays from his severance. Then Sheila tells him, and his hands keep a bluff standing: borrowed faces in the back of the cab, lights in empty warehouses, one fight Ron must win in public. In the cab Ron admits he has hit nobody since 1984. Cammack tests it harder each week, until Tom does one real thing and burns his car lot. He sends Darren to the meeting to stand for the forty, and Darren is killed there. On the last night the firms call the muster: all forty on the quay at six. Who walks down comes from the record, the fares Tom chose, the Fog he repaid or did not. The quay fills or stays empty, and Tom becomes what Mickey only pretended to be, or says so aloud.

Canon notes: strains "a half-dead criminal outfit" by making it a reputation with no crimes behind it; makes the debt book invented; Darren Milner is killed late; Cammack owns a car lot.

## X6. The Black Rank

The strike of July 1989 failed in three weeks, and the player meets it at once: nobody on the Hook will get into a Suddaby cab. In the strike's second week Mickey's cars carried the managers through the picket to drive a shipment off a Russian ship. The book of uncollectable debts is what Mickey lent the strikers in week one; nobody will pay a scab. So what does the Hook owe the dead man, and what does Tom owe the Hook for a thing done before he came? Ron Kirby is spat at for staying on the door; Sheila Dunn minds phones that do not ring; June Suddaby dropped her father, yet rides home from nights in the cab; Swan Vesta, who led the strike, now stands in the pen as a strag, and is June's man. The only fares are outsiders, Agar's surveyors, casual men bussed to the gate, and each one driven is a side taken. Tom collects from men with cheques, hard. In week four the office is set alight with Sheila inside. Swan Vesta had promised as much, so Tom runs him down and leaves him lame. The fire was Cammack's, for the rank. June does not speak to Tom again. Then Sheila opens July's fare book: Mickey took the managers' money and lent it all to the strikers. The strike ate scab money. The Hook remembers which outsiders Tom drove and whom he squeezed, and answers at the last call at the gate, where he reads the page out and cancels the book, or burns the page and collects.

Canon notes: adds that Suddaby cabs crossed the 1989 picket and that Mickey funded strikers with the money; adds a strike leader known as Swan Vesta as June's partner; an office fire injures Sheila (she lives).
