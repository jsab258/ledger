# Premise set Z (story v3, phase 1)

Written by the story session (claude-fable-5-2). Z1 is The Passenger at its best version (bible M3 with the fourth reader's six changes taken). Z2 to Z8 are new.

## Z1. The Passenger

A year ago, in the winter the ambulances stopped, a bonded store on the dock burned and one of Mickey's cabs was on the row; now the last dockers are signing for their cheques and the burnt plot is for sale. Tom meets it as a line in last year's fare book: car two, out and back, eleven miles, no name. What does he owe a night he was not there for? Ron Kirby, who cannot drive, rides beside him and is taught on the empty dock road, the player holding the handbrake. Sheila Dunn makes Tom write one true line a night. The Bishop, a burnt docker in grey gloves, is kept by a sick club the office secretly funds. The player's hands do a job a week to keep that club paid, each larger, and the car is where Ron almost tells it. The evidence says Ron drove away and left the Bishop, and the player says so to his face, in the cab, and puts him out on the road. Ron was on foot; he carried the man out. It was Sheila who drove off, and turned round at a bollard. Tom chooses one last job for the club, takes the wheel himself, and when it goes wrong and Ron is left inside, the player alone decides whether to go and how far before he turns. Ron is jailed or crippled by that distance; the Bishop dies either way. The miles on the dial and every false line the player wrote are read out by Ellis. Sheila will sign for her night only if Tom signs for his.

Canon notes: adds the fire of November 1989, the sick club, the Bishop and Shiner of Ron's old gang (as in the earlier bibles); one ending has Tom charged.

## Z2. Completion

Tom has not come to stay. The letter in his suitcase is an offer from Geoffrey Agar's solicitors for Mickey's, completion in seven weeks, and the player meets the dock's end by being it: to give clear title he must collect the debts, close the outfit and lay off the men, as the port did. What does a man owe a place he is paid to finish? Ron Kirby is on the door and is the last thing on the list. Sheila Dunn draws up the schedule herself, in ink, and will not be thanked. Lord Ash, a strag who sleeps in car three, has to go. The player's hands collect, strip, evict and break into rivals who want the yard cheap; each night's last fare is someone leaving the Hook with a cheque, and they talk because he is leaving too. The books are short the same small sum every week for nine years. Tom decides it is Sheila, says so in front of the drivers and takes her keys. The sum was Ron's wage: Mickey could not afford him after 1989 and she paid it from her own, so that Ron never knew he had been let go already. Tom chooses to keep the date, gives Ron his notice, and Ron goes to stand in the pen at fifty-eight and is crushed down below within the week. He lives; he does not know Tom. Each person the player squeezed or spared is at the ferry or is not. He banks the cheque and sails, or tears it up in an office with nobody on the door.

Canon notes: makes Tom arrive meaning to sell; adds Agar's offer and a named strag (Lord Ash); Ron is injured past recovery (alive); Sheila has paid Ron's wage since 1989.

## Z3. A Name a Week

The docks are being cleaned for sale, and DS Carol Ellis has seven weeks to clear the Hook before the buyers walk it. She meets Tom off the ferry. She knows what is in the suitcase; the player never does. Her price is one name every Monday, from the fare book. What does a man owe people who took him in, when what he owes the law is them? Ron Kirby stood four weeks on a picket and says you would not scab out the fellow next door. Sheila Dunn sees a page of her book has been turned. Darren Milner sells radios and wants a job. The player's hands earn the names: he drives the Widow's night runs, breaks in with Cammack's crew, fights beside men, and in the back of the cab they tell him things, and on Monday he picks one of them. Each name is bigger than the last. In week four the office is burned at the back and Ron's hands with it. Tom is sure it was Cinderella, off before twelve that night, and gives him to Ellis with a lie added. Cinderella goes down. He left early because he sits up with a dying brother; the fire was the Widow's, to find the grass. So Tom gives a truer name and then Ellis wants the one that is left. The player chooses, and whoever he names is gone for good, and if he names nobody, Ron is told. Every fare he was kind to is a witness for or against him at the last. He writes his own name in the book, or Ron's, or Sheila's.

Canon notes: Ellis holds an unspoken hold over Tom (his past stays unsaid); adds a named docker (Cinderella); an office fire injures Ron (he lives); one ending has Tom charged.

## Z4. The Knowledge

Ron Kirby's trade was abolished by Act of Parliament and he means to have another: he will be a cab driver before the top cheque rate closes, and he cannot drive. Tom cannot find a street in the Hook. The player meets the dock's end from the driving seat, Ron beside him naming every building by what it was and calling the turn a street late. What does Tom owe a man who is the only reason anybody opens a door to him? Sheila Dunn books Ron's test for the seventh Friday and prices the car he keeps hitting. The Widow, who sells the tap on the shoulder at the gate, wants Ron for her foreman. The player's hands do the outfit's work with Ron as the knowledge: which yard, which wall, who is in. Lessons are on the dock road at night, and that is where he talks. Then Ron is seen at the gate in a foreman's coat, picking men, and the Widow is waiting at Tom's next job. Tom calls him a seller of his book before the pen and gives his seat to Darren Milner. Ron was picking Mickey's debtors, one a morning, so that the uncollectable could pay. Tom chooses to do the big job without him; it goes bad, and Ron walks in for him and comes out with his hands broken by the Widow's men. He will not hold a wheel. The streets the player learned and the men he let Ron pick decide who stands at the gate on the last morning. On the seventh Friday the examiner waits. Tom drives Ron there anyway, or does not.

Canon notes: adds Ron's wish to become a driver and a council driving test; Ron's hands are broken for good (he lives); the Widow controls hiring at the gate.

## Z5. The Heir

Mickey had a daughter and left the office to a stranger. The player meets the dock's end through that: June Suddaby, a night nurse, rides home in his cab each dawn past the pen and will not say why her father cut her out. What does Tom owe the person whose inheritance he is sitting in? Ron Kirby says Mickey knew his own mind. Sheila Dunn typed the will and will not discuss it. The Widow visits to say the arrangement stands and that she will see him on the fourteenth of December. The player's hands run what Mickey ran, night runs off the last ships, a break-in at the harbour office, harder fights each week, and money comes in faster than it should. Then the office is turned over twice, a solicitor writes, and a lock is changed. Tom is certain June wants it back. He uses what he has heard in the cab, puts it about that she takes from the ward's cupboard, and she is struck off. June had been trying to make him leave. Mickey borrowed thirty-five dockers' cheques from the Widow's side of the gate to keep the Hook's men working one more year, the whole sum falls on the heir on the fourteenth, and he chose a nephew nobody would miss. Tom chooses to hold the office against the Widow, and June, with no ward to go to, takes the morning train and does not write. What the player earned, from whom, and who saw, decides whether the men stand with him on the fourteenth. He pays with the office, or with Ron, or becomes the Widow's man.

Canon notes: adds Mickey's debt to the Widow, due on a date, as the reason Tom inherited; June loses her work and leaves the map; Mickey used Tom.

## Z6. The Last Man to Pay

On the Hook they say Mickey Suddaby did not just die. A man stood over him that morning: Kipper, who collects for Danny Cammack, was seen leaving by the yard. The player hears it from four people in his first week, in the cab, each sure. What does a nephew owe a dead man he never met? Ron Kirby found the body and says leave it. Sheila Dunn says nothing at all. Kipper's wife Pat cleans the offices on Quay Street and rides home at six every morning. The docks are emptying, the cheques are being spent, and Cammack is taking the rank; the player's hands fight for it week by week until he has Kipper alone on the fish quay, and how far he goes is his. Kipper is dead or he never works again. Then Ron tells it, at two in the morning with the engine off. Kipper was the only man in nine years who came to pay what he owed. He put forty pounds on the desk and left Mickey alive. Ron found the envelope with the body, and took it for the widows' tin, and so could not say who had been there. Seven people saw some part of the fish quay. Each wants something, Ellis is asking, and Tom pays them in jobs that grow worse. Pat still books the six o'clock. Tom chooses whether Ron stands up and says what he took; if he does, the Hook is finished with him for robbing the dead. The fare book has a line for that morning. Tom writes it, and drives Pat home, or lets her walk.

Canon notes: adds Kipper and his wife Pat (new people); the player may kill Kipper (a killing by hand, permanent); adds a widows' tin kept at the office.

## Z7. Called In

The debts in Mickey's book are uncollectable because of who owes them. For thirty years he lent to people when they were nobody: a harbour clerk, a constable's wife, a young solicitor called Agar, the docker who became the Widow's husband. Now the dock is closing and those people own what is left, and the player meets its end as a list of the powerful, each with a sum beside a nickname. What does Tom owe a dead man's patience? Ron Kirby knows who each name is. Sheila Dunn knows what each debt was for and tells him only after. Keith Garbutt carries the answers back. Each debt called in is a job done with the hands, a door, a yard, a safe, a fight on a ship, and each lifts Tom a rung: the rank, the gate, the cold store, a seat at the sale of the dock. The confessions come in the cab from people he is ruining. When the gate is taken from him in a night, he decides Garbutt sold him and breaks him in the yard. Garbutt was carrying Mickey's old instruction: never the last three pages. The last page is Sheila's. At twenty-two she owed Mickey something no sum is written against, and calling it in gives Tom the Hook entire. If the player calls it, she pays, in full, and is gone by morning with the book squared. If he does not, the Widow takes the gate back and Ron goes into the pen. The people he ruined on the way up sit at the sale. He buys the dock, or closes the book unpaid.

Canon notes: makes the debt book a list of favours owed by the town's powerful, including Agar and the Widow's late husband; Sheila owes Mickey an unnamed debt from 1959; Garbutt is injured for good.

## Z8. New Management

Sheila Dunn calls him New Management and it takes the player three weeks to hear it. Jobs arrive from Mickey's old contacts, in envelopes, well planned; Tom does them with his hands, break-ins, a lorry off the dock road, a fight outside Cammack's, and the office rises while the dock around it is sold off and the last men sign. The question of what he owes the dead turns into another: whose outfit is this? Ron Kirby does what the envelopes say and laughs at nothing. Darren Milner wants to be taken on. Ada watches from her window and misses little. The cab is where Tom starts to work it out, because every fare seems to have been told where he would be. He decides somebody is steering him for Agar, picks Darren, and breaks his arm on the forecourt. That night Sheila does the count as usual. The envelopes are in her hand. Mickey was a cheerful man who liked the door held; she has run the Hook's quiet crime since 1964, she needs a name over the shop, and Tom's is new. He can take it from her in earnest, and the player does a job she did not plan. It works, loudly, and it brings Ellis, and Ron has to choose once whom he stands behind. He stands behind her and never rides with Tom again. What the player did off her plan is what the town holds against him or her. At the end somebody answers for thirty years of it. Tom gives Ellis the planner, or stays the front and is charged, and Sheila says his name.

Canon notes: makes Sheila the true head of Mickey's outfit since 1964 and Mickey her front; Darren is injured; one ending has Tom charged, another Sheila.
