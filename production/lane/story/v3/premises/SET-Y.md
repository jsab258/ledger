# Premise set Y (story v3, phase 1)

## Y1. The Tap

The dock hires by the day again. At six each morning a foreman picks from a pen of men who held books for thirty years, and Tom meets it in week one, because Mickey's one live contract is cabbing the picked men to the far berths. Tom wants the gate for himself: whoever supplies the men is the last employer in the Hook, and the Widow's people are selling the tap on the shoulder. What he owes changes the day he wins it, since every man he picks pays the office rent and every man he leaves is a strag by his hand. Ron stood in that pen until 1989 and writes the list, hating it. Sheila prices each man. Darren Milner runs the names and wants a tap of his own. The player's hands drive the dawn cabs, break the Widow's collectors and hold the gate in a fight; the deep talk comes on that run, one man in the back explaining why tomorrow has to be him. When cargo starts walking and Ellis starts reading the fare book, Tom decides the thief is Lord Ash, a strag he has long passed over, and breaks his hands before the pen. The thief was Ron's mercy: he had been letting unpicked men carry a little out. Tom chooses to hit the Widow's yard, leaving Darren alone at the gate, and Darren is killed there. The men the player picked most stand by him; those he never picked talk to Ellis. At the end Tom keeps the call or hands the list to the pen, each morning's cost on the ledger.

Canon notes: adds a named docker (Lord Ash, from the real nickname list) and a contract between Mickey's office and the port for carrying men; Darren Milner can be killed.

## Y2. Closing Date

The last dockers are being paid to go, up to £35,000 each, a rate that closes in the new year, and one in five still refuses. Tom meets them as his inheritance, because Mickey's debts are theirs, uncollectable only until they sign. Agar, buying the dock land, will pay Tom a fee for every signature, and Tom wants it badly: the office is mortgaged at Bank rate and it is the one thing with his name over the door. What he owes the living is set against what they owe him. Ron signed in 1989 and is called a seller of his book. Sheila knows why each loan was made. The London Fog, who never lifts, leads the holdouts and rides Tom's cab to the gate each morning, paying in old large fivepences. The player's hands collect: doors forced, a van taken; in the cab the men say what the cheque would end. Tom comes to believe the Widow pays the Fog to hold out, her smuggling needing a working dock, and reads the Fog's own debt page aloud to the men. They sign. The page was the Fog borrowing to pay sick men, who get nothing. Tom chooses to take the last signature too: the Fog signs, pays in full and leaves Meridian, lifted for good. How the player collected in week one, and whether he took a widow's tin of fivepences, is said back to him at the gate. On the mortgage date Tom banks Agar's fees and keeps an office in an emptied Hook, or burns the pages and drives for someone else.

Canon notes: adds a mortgage on the office and a named docker (The London Fog); makes the debt book's debtors the remaining dockers; Agar pays for signatures.

## Y3. The Night Gang

Eleven stevedoring firms have gone under and ships are worked by casuals and managers in borrowed gloves; what was theft last year is a job, so the old job becomes theft. Tom meets it when Ron's old gang, six men without a trade, ask for cabs at midnight: they mean to work the ships themselves by night, by the old rules, and keep what they discharge. Tom wants in as an equal, not as Mickey's nephew, and wants enough to buy the Quay Street freehold. Who is owed a share: the gang, their dead, the dead man whose cabs carry it? Ron runs the hatch. Sheila turns cargo into clean fares. Splinter, 34, son of Plank, never got his book and rides up front every night. The player's hands break sheds, drive loads through a watching town, fight the Widow's men over her consignment, and take cars off a Russian ship as the managers did. The men talk in the cab at four in the morning. When Ellis is waiting at a shed, Tom decides Cinderella, always gone before twelve, is the informer, and beats him past working. Cinderella left early because he was the night watchman leaving the doors unlocked. Ellis had simply read the fare book, each booking in the player's own hand. Tom chooses one more ship, unlit, and Splinter falls down the hold and dies. Which fares the player wrote truly, and who saw his cab on the quay, decide what she can prove. At the end she demands the book: the true one convicts Tom and clears the gang, the false one does the reverse.

Canon notes: adds Ron's old gang (Splinter, Cinderella and others from the real nickname list); one ending sends Tom to prison, which he survives; Sheila falsifies the fare book.

## Y4. Mickey's Scheme

Mickey admired the Dock Labour Scheme so much that he ran the Hook's crooked work on it: forty-one registered men, a book each, a call outside the cab office at seven, jobs shared by rota, no casuals, £75 to a widow. Parliament abolished the real one; Tom inherits the copy, and meets it when his first job goes by rule to The Blister, who turns up when the work is done. Tom wants an outfit that earns, and Danny Cammack's casuals undercut him on every job. What does he owe forty-one bad thieves promised work for life? Ron is the steward, rule book in his coat. Sheila stamps the cards. The Blister cannot be shaken off. The player's hands do jobs with whichever man the rota allots, the safe man who cannot drive, The Man in Black away at a funeral, Swan Vesta calling a stoppage in a warehouse; grievances are heard in the cab, engine running. When the widows' fund goes missing Tom is sure The Broken Boomerang, who never comes back, has run with it, drags him before the call and strikes him off. The Boomerang was working two sick members' turns and paying them from the fund, as the rules allow. Tom chooses to put Cammack's casuals on one big job, and Ron, who never struck in thirty years, will not cross and never works for him again. Every man the player passed over on the rota is read out at the last meeting. Cammack offers a cheque a man to close the register; Tom signs the abolition or calls the names at seven.

Canon notes: makes the half-dead outfit a registered closed shop of forty-one men, most of them light or unseen; gives Ron the steward's role; the named members (from the real nickname list) are new.

## Y5. The Arrested Ship

The last ship in the dock cannot leave. A stevedoring firm went bust owing Mickey, Mickey's lawyer had a Soviet freighter arrested for the debt, and the writ is still taped to her mast; Tom meets the dock's end as nine unpaid seamen on a ship that is now, in law, his problem. He wants the debt paid, then wants her to stay, because while she lies there the berth is still a berth and he is the man who holds it. What he owes runs past the dead man to strangers who cannot go home until he signs. The master books a cab every night to nowhere, to be off his ship for an hour. Ron becomes her watchman, happy for the first time since 1989. Sheila works out what a ship costs a day. The player's hands carry stores aboard, fight off Cammack's men, who want her burnt, break into Agar's office for the berth plans; fares are paid in tinned fish and the ship keeps Moscow time, so the night fare is always tomorrow's. When her brass and cable start vanishing Tom blames Ron's old workmates and breaks one man's arm at the fence. The crew were selling their own ship to eat. Tom refuses Agar's settlement; the owners abandon her, the crew is flown home and the master, last off, is deported. The master's log lists every kindness and every charge the player made. At the end Ron's old gang discharge her one last time to pay the debt, or she is towed for scrap with the cargo in her.

Canon notes: adds a ship under arrest for a debt owed to Mickey, her master and crew of nine (foreign seamen, new people), and the ship's deck as a place on the docks map.

## Y6. The Cost Book

The dock land is being bought by the hectare and Quay Street is next: Tom meets the end as a solicitor's letter offering £9,000 for the office. Three people want the Hook for three futures, Agar a marina with flats, the Widow a quay left dark and working, Cammack lorry parks for a trade nobody names. Tom wants to stay, and to make them pay the true price, which Sheila knows. Beside the accounts she has kept a cost book for thirty-one years: what Mickey paid, to whom, for what, every councillor the land sale rests on. What Tom owes is tangled in it: Mickey's debts are secured on the houses the buyers need. Sheila plans; Ron stands behind the plan; Garbutt carries offers between the three. The player's hands collect debts door by door to take the leases first, break into the harbour office, sell the same block to three bidders; the planning is done in the cab, Sheila in the back, book on her knee. When Agar's men arrive at a house an hour ahead of Tom, he decides Garbutt sold him, and runs him down on Quay Street; Garbutt never walks properly again. Sheila had sold Agar one page, the one about herself. Tom chooses to give the cost book to Alison Sedman, and Sheila, whose writing is on every line, goes to prison with those it names. She says his first name once, in the courtroom. Whom the player evicted and whom he let stay decides who holds the leases at the end, when the sale goes through or fails, house by house.

Canon notes: adds Sheila's private cost book and her own part in Mickey's payments; makes the debts secured on Hook leases; Sheila can be imprisoned.
