In the old harbour quarter of a northern port, the lead, a stranger and the dead owner's nephew, has not come to stay: his suitcase holds the landowner's offer for the radio-taxi office, completion in seven weeks. He is the dock's end: clear title means collecting debts, closing the outfit and laying off men, as the port did. What does a man owe a place he is paid to finish? The doorman is last on the list. The bookkeeper inks the schedule herself and refuses thanks. A leftover known as the Earl, who sleeps in car three, has to go. The player's hands collect, strip, evict and break in on rivals wanting the yard cheap; each night's last fare leaves with a cheque and talks, because the lead is leaving too. The books are short the same small sum weekly for nine years. The lead blames the bookkeeper before the drivers and takes her keys. It was the doorman's wage: unaffordable once the harbour labour register was abolished, it came from her own pocket, so he never knew he was dismissed. Keeping the date, the lead gives the doorman notice; at fifty-eight he stands in the pen and is crushed below within the week. He lives, not knowing the lead. Those squeezed or spared are at the ferry or not. The lead banks the cheque and sails, or tears it up in an office with nobody on the door.
