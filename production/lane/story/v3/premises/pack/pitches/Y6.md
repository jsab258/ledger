In a northern port in 1991, dock land is bought by the hectare, and the lead, a stranger left his dead uncle's radio-taxi office, meets the end in a solicitor's 9,000-crown offer for it. Three buyers want the old harbour quarter: the landowner a marina, the gate boss a quay left dark and working, the young crew boss lorry parks for a trade nobody names. The lead wants to stay and charge the true price, which the bookkeeper knows: her thirty-one-year cost book records what his uncle paid, to whom, and every councillor the sale rests on, and his uncle's debts are secured on the houses the buyers need. She plans, the doorman backs her, the go-between carries offers. The player's hands collect door to door to take the leases first, break into the harbour office and sell one block to three bidders, planning in the cab, the book on her knee. When the landowner's men beat him to a house by an hour, the lead decides the go-between sold him and runs him down, laming him for life. The bookkeeper had sold one page, her own. The lead gives the cost book to the journalist, and the bookkeeper, whose writing is on every line, goes to prison with those it names, saying his first name once in court. Who the player evicted or spared decides who holds the leases as the sale goes through or fails.
