In the old harbour quarter of a northern port, two summers after the harbour labour register was abolished by law, habits are stopping. Knowing his heart, the dead owner of a radio-taxi office spent his last week writing seven weeks of bookings into the dispatch book: nickname, kerb, minute, destination. Each week more kerbs stand empty. The lead, a stranger and his nephew, asks whether he owes a dead man obedience. The bookkeeper reads each page out without comment, the doorman rides when a line smells of trouble, and the old neighbour, window to cemetery every Thursday, says more in the cab than anyone living. Some lines are jobs, a boat parcel for the gate boss, done blind with fists. In week three a stevedore known for leaving before midnight is missing from the gate at ten to twelve. Blaming the young crew boss, the lead rams one of his drivers through a shop front. The stevedore had cashed his cheque and taken the morning train. The young crew boss answers on a week-five Thursday the lead gave the gate boss's parcel: the old neighbour walks, is knocked down by a car hunting his, and goes to a home inland, her window dark. The quarter remembers which empty kerbs he waited at and which lines he struck out. The last booking names the lead, office to station, one suitcase. He drives it, or writes the next line himself.
