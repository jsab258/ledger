In 1991, in the old harbour quarter of a northern port, the harbour labour register was abolished by law two summers before, and the last registered stevedores sign before the top severance rate closes. On the first fare, at the gate, a man with a cheque for 35,000 crowns is left in the pen as a leftover. The lead, a stranger and the dead owner's nephew, reads his uncle's book of a hundred and forty nicknames and sums as debts to the radio-taxi office. The doorman works doors beside him, the bookkeeper banks, and the gate boss, paid weekly, holds a note on the office. The player's hands collect by threat, break-in and a stairwell beating, then drive the same men. In week three he breaks the wrist of a stevedore called the Drizzle, who insists the dead man already has his money. In the cab a stevedore known for leaving before midnight explains: the sums are deposits. His uncle was the quarter's bank and put every cheque into an option on the old quay, against the landowner. He has robbed his own creditors. One line is the doorman's cheque, which he spent clearing the gate boss's note; the doorman goes to the pen for good. Those he squeezed hardest decide the depositors' trust; free fares speak for him. He sells the option to the landowner and pays everyone short, or holds the quay and owes the men for life.
