In a northern port in 1991, as the dock is sold and the last stevedores sign, a radio-taxi office in the old harbour quarter rises. The lead, its dead owner's stranger nephew, does planned jobs that arrive in envelopes from his uncle's contacts, and the bookkeeper calls him New Management, a name missed for three weeks. The question of debt becomes: whose outfit is this? The doorman does what the envelopes say; the young hustler wants taking on; the old neighbour watches. The player's hands break in, take a lorry off the dock road and fight outside the young crew boss's place, and in the cab the lead sees every fare knew where he would be. Suspecting someone steers him for the landowner, he breaks the hustler's arm on the forecourt. That night the bookkeeper does the count, envelopes in hand: his uncle was a cheerful man who liked the door held, and she has run the quarter's quiet crime since 1964, needing a new name over the shop. Taking it from her, the player does a job she did not plan; it works loudly and brings the detective. The doorman, choosing once, stands behind her and never rides with the lead again. What the player did off-plan is held against him or her. Someone answers for thirty years: the lead gives the detective the planner, or stays the front and is charged, and the bookkeeper says his name.
