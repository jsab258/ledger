In the old harbour quarter of a northern port, workless stevedores die faster; the first morning brings a widow's card stamped weekly for nearly forty years. The dead owner's book is a burial club: 212 men promised a hearse, two cars and a stone, where the stevedores' fund gives 75 crowns. The tin holds forty-one crowns, in old and new coins of the same value. The lead, a stranger and his nephew, inherits the radio-taxi office and two hundred funerals. The doorman has carried sixty coffins, the bookkeeper stamps cards, the priest counts cars, and a stevedore called the Mourner scores every funeral out of ten, aloud. Each funeral costs the player's hands a crime: a night's smuggling for the gate boss's hearse, a stone stolen from the mason, a debt beaten from the living. The cab is the second car, where the bereaved talk. Initials on withdrawals point at the priest; the lead burgles the presbytery and shames him. They mean black weeks, without work, when his uncle stamped cards himself; there was never a fund; the outfit existed for funerals. With three in week five, the lead sends the doorman alone to settle a hearse with the young crew boss's men. The doorman is killed, his card paid up. The turnout for him follows the Mourner's score of every corner cut. The lead closes the club with the dock, or stamps a card in his own name.
