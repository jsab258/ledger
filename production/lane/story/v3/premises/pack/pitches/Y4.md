In a northern port in 1991, the harbour labour register is abolished, but the lead's dead uncle ran the old harbour quarter's crooked work on a copy: forty-one registered men, a call outside his radio-taxi office at seven, jobs by rota, no casuals, 75 crowns to a widow. The lead, a stranger, inherits it; his first job goes by rule to a man who arrives when the work is done. He wants an outfit that earns against the young crew boss's cheaper casuals, and weighs what he owes forty-one bad thieves promised work for life. The doorman is steward, rule book in his coat; the bookkeeper stamps the cards; the latecomer cannot be shaken off. The player's hands work with whoever the rota allots, a safe man who cannot drive, a man away at a funeral, a man calling a stoppage, grievances heard in the cab. When the widows' fund vanishes, the lead is sure a man who never comes back has run with it and strikes him off before the call. He was working two sick members' turns, paying them from the fund as the rules allow. When the lead puts the casuals on one big job, the doorman, who never struck in thirty years, will not cross and leaves him. The last meeting reads out every man passed over. Offered a cheque a man to close the register, the lead signs or calls the names at seven.
