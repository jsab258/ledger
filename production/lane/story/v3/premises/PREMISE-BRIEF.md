# Brief for premise writers (story v3, phase 1)

You are writing competing story premises for a video game. Write them as the best games writer you can be. They will be ranked blind by readers who judge how players would rate the story.

## The game (fixed; do not change any of it)

LEDGER: a third-person open-town crime game for PC. Britain, autumn 1990, the fictional port town of Meridian; the playable map is the Hook (the old port quarter) and its docks. Tom Nowak, 32, a stranger with one suitcase and a letter, inherits from his uncle Mickey Suddaby (dead of his heart at his desk; nobody thinks otherwise) a minicab office on Quay Street, a half-dead criminal outfit and a book of uncollectable debts. Tom's life before the Hook is never told and nobody asks. He cannot die.

Inherited people: Ron Kirby, 58, old muscle, a docker until 1989, kept on by Mickey for the door and the rank. Sheila Dunn, 53, the bookkeeper, at Mickey's since she was 22; she withholds Tom's first name until she trusts him.

Others who exist in the town (use few; you need not use any): Maureen Jensen, "the Widow", head of the dockside syndicate (muscle and smuggling); Geoffrey Agar, head of the old-money machine (lawyers, corruption, land); Danny Cammack, head of a flashy, reckless new crew; DS Carol Ellis, detective; Darren Milner, 25, street hustler; Ada, an old neighbour at her window; June Suddaby, Mickey's estranged daughter, a night nurse; Father Brendan Walsh, the parish priest; Alison Sedman, journalist; Philip Danby, teacher (the school is closed); Keith Garbutt, a fixer between the three rivals.

What the player does, mostly with his hands: drives the cab (a large share of every hour; fares are few and chosen, each a person with something going on), collects debts, does jobs, breaks in, fights, works the street. The town watches: witnesses remember permanently, gossip spreads, people recognise him by degrees. Talk (typed, live) is where consequences surface. The office keeps a fare book: by law every booking is written down before the journey and a constable may demand to read it. The game runs on a calendar of about seven game weeks (week one the inheritance, about five weeks of middle, a short last act); the main line is about twelve hours.

Content rule (absolute): no alcohol and no gambling, shown or spoken of (pubs exist only as buildings). Tobacco is fine. No children anywhere, not seen, not mentioned. No sexual content, no slurs, no torture. Drugs only as an off-screen trade others run. Killing is possible, rare and permanent. A car can hurt a person badly and never kills. Guns rare and late. Tom cannot die. Era: late-analogue Britain, landlines, phone boxes, cash, paper; no mobiles.

## The eleven ideas the premise must answer (the first seven inside the premise)

1. The end of something. The real world dying around the story: the National Dock Labour Scheme, which since 1947 had reserved dock work for registered men, was abolished on 3 July 1989. A strike failed in three weeks. Within sixteen months four in five dockers in a middling port were gone, each with a cheque of up to £35,000; that top rate closes in early 1991. Casual hiring, the thing the Scheme was built to end, came straight back at the gate. Container ports elsewhere took the trade; old dock land is being bought up by the hectare. Tom arrives as the Hook's reason to exist goes.
2. Theme you play. Every system is a ledger of obligation: fares, debts, rent, favours, what witnesses know. One question: what does Tom owe the dead man and the living? The ending settles accounts.
3. Fewer people, deeper. Three or four relationships carried through the whole game (Ron and Sheila above all); everyone else light.
4. Make the player do it, then show what it meant. The player commits to a wrong belief with his own hands (accuses or hurts the wrong person) and only then learns the truth.
5. A loss that cannot be undone: someone the player cares about is lost for good (dead, gone, or broken with), as a result of the player's own choices.
6. The cab as the confessional: the deepest talk happens in the car.
7. The town remembers: the authored story takes up the player's own record later (name one or two acts it picks up).
10. Push against the habits of machine writing: too many characters, everything mended, tidy arcs. Specific, strange, restrained.
11. Real ingredients (use what serves; invent nothing that contradicts them):
- Dockers were often known only by nicknames, each with a reason: The Blister (turns up when the work is done), Cinderella (off just before twelve), Sell the Bed (always on nights), The London Fog (never lifts), The Man in Black (always off to a funeral), The Broken Boomerang (never comes back), Swan Vesta (always on strike), Manhole (fell down a hold), Lord Ash (always smoking), Splinter (son of Plank).
- The hiring words: the call; the pen; "blue-eyed boys" got "the tap on the shoulder"; men left with the dregs were "strags"; a registered man's place was his "book"; "you serve your time on the quay" before you "go down below"; clerks "sit on radiators". "You wouldn't scab out the fella who lives next door to you."
- After July 1989 things that had been crimes became jobs: managers themselves drove a shipment of cars off a Russian ship.
- Eleven stevedoring firms went bust in the months after, taking 868 men's jobs.
- A dockers' fund paid a widow £75 when her man died; dockers had no sick pay.
- The ambulance crews' dispute ran from September 1989 to 23 February 1990; from November crews took emergencies only and the army drove ambulances.
- 25 January 1990: a storm killed 47, cut power to half a million homes, brought phone lines down, shut ports.
- The poll tax began on 1 April 1990: a bill a head, mass non-payment, summonses.
- Bank rate 15%.
- A new small 5p coin came in on 27 June 1990; the old large one stays legal until 31 December, so every till and tin holds both sizes.

## What players reward and punish (from a study of what players write about story in eight shipped games)

Reward, in order: characters worth caring about (about half of all praise: a lead who is somebody, people they would miss, a bond), the writing, moments that hit hard, humour, an ending that lands, a turn that surprises. Punish: a forgettable story with no one at its centre, missions that force the way, a weak ending, a padded middle. The last story tried for this game was marked down for a quiet lead to whom little happens, small stakes and a slow middle; it was praised for the doorman and the bookkeeper and for comedy. Tom's past stays unsaid, but he must be somebody in what he does, wants and risks now.

## What to write

Six premises, each clearly different in kind from the others (not six variations). For each:

- A title (two to four words).
- One paragraph of 230 to 270 words, plain prose, present tense, covering in this order: what is ending and how the player meets it; the question of what is owed; the three or four people who matter; what the player's hands do, and the cab's part; the wrong the player commits believing something false, and what the truth turns out to be; the loss that cannot be undone and the choice that causes it; one or two of the player's own earlier acts that the story takes up later; the ending and what it settles.
- One line, "Canon notes:", naming anything that would add to or strain the fixed facts above (or "none").

Plain words, British spelling, no em-dashes, no italics, no lists inside a premise, no judging words about your own work.
