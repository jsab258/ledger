## What story v3 builds on

1. The dock scheme's end (3 July 1989) as the reason for the half-dead outfit and the debts nobody collects: found by the tone research in September and already the outline's hidden floor. Story v3's research (v3/RESEARCH.md) adds the dates, the cheque and its closing date, the pen coming back and the men's words, and brings it from under the floor into what the player sees. No disagreement; v3 goes further.
2. "British noir runs on class, not greed": a man wants to be treated as somebody. The name ladder (the new owner, Nowak, Tom, Tommy) is that engine. The first readers found the same from the other side: money as the plot sank every premise. Both picked bibles keep the ladder (Sheila's "Tom"; "the Admiral"). The revision must make the lead's want this want, said loudly: to be somebody here.
3. Beats fire on states, never on sequence; no single beat can fail the run; the simulation may take away only who is alive, who knows what, who is where, who will talk, and how the law stands. The three bibles were written to a week-by-week calendar, which is sequence. The revision brief now requires each main beat to name the state that opens it and what happens if the player comes late or not at all.
4. The 32 side-story drafts of 8 Nov (reviewed, twelve fixes applied). The bibles wrote about twenty-five new ones each without reading them. The revision writers are told to take from the 32 first and write new ones only where the chosen story needs them.
5. The warehouse fire backstory (approved 30 Sep) and the outline's five first-week threads. The Passenger was built on the fire; the two picked stories do not use it. See ruling 1 below.

Where v3 disagrees with the earlier work, and which wins. The outline's spine is a rise ("how big can it get before your two lives touch"), three rivals each attacking a different ledger, and a last act that is a VAT inspection. His brief of 21 Nov (the eleven ideas) asks for the end of something, three or four people carried deep, a loss, the cab as confessional. His newer words win, so v3 follows the brief; but the outline is his approved baseline and canon cites it, so the difference is not settled silently: rulings 1 and 2 below.

One thing the readers and his own pillar say together. All three v3 pages were marked down for a quiet week, small stakes and no danger the player feels. His words of 18 Nov: what the player feels is "tension, risk and a rise to power". The stories lost the rise when they took up the ending world. The revision is told to put it back: Tom must gain ground in position (people, cars, the street's name for him) while the dock ends round him, and be able to lose it.

## Rulings to revisit (each one question for him; until he answers, the ruling stands)

1. The story baseline (canon, 28 Sep: "Tom Nowak, the three acts, the three rivals and the empire roster are the baseline, as the story outline he approved that day tells them"). Evidence against keeping it whole: his own brief of 21 Nov (fewer people, deeper; the end of something); the aspect bar for the opponent (the best games have one opponent inside the lead's circle, faced in person); the checked reader put the story built on the baseline, after four rewrites, at 7.1, and the two new stories at 7.5 and 7.7. Evidence for keeping part of it: his words of 18 Nov on a rise to power, and the readers' complaint of small stakes. Question: the main story is (A) the baseline as approved: three rivals as equal heads, a rise, the inspection as last act; (B) one of the new stories, with one rival as the opponent, the other two light, and the rise in position kept inside it [recommended]; (C) one of the new stories with no rise.
2. The last act as a VAT inspection (ruled July, kept 28 Sep). Evidence: the fourth reader of 20 Nov called a VAT officer demanding a signed admission thin in law; readers mark down a last act of procedure and reward a reckoning with the opponent in person; neither picked story has an inspection. Question: (A) keep the inspection as the last act; (B) the last act is the chosen story's own reckoning, and the inspection, if kept at all, is a pressure in the middle [recommended]; (C) both, the inspection first.
3. The seven-week calendar with a weekly rhythm (his clock ruling of 21 Nov, the newest word, so it stands). Evidence to note, not to overturn: readers called both picked stories' weekly or nightly cycle slow and repeating ("the weekly funeral loop", "the nightly fare cycle across 42 nights"). Recommendation: keep the ruling; the revision breaks the rhythm inside it (no two weeks shaped alike). No question needed unless the revision fails on this point.
4. The love interests (canon's cast: Alison Sedman and Philip Danby as love interests; the outline's "someone worth being honest for"). Evidence: his brief asks for three or four people carried deep, and neither picked story has room for a romance in its main line. Question: (A) a love interest stays in the main line; (B) both stay in the town as side stories only [recommended]; (C) cut.
