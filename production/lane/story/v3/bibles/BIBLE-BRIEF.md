## What binds the story

- Canon outranks all. Anything that adds to or strains canon is listed at the end as a question for the owner with options and a recommendation; write the bible on your recommended option and say what changes under the others.
- Content rule, absolute: no alcohol and no gambling, shown or spoken of (pubs are only buildings; no drinks, no bets, no raffles, no bookmakers, no wakes with drink). Tobacco is fine. No children anywhere: not seen, not mentioned, no sons or daughters under eighteen, no schools in use, no pregnancies. No sexual content, no slurs, no torture. Drugs only as an off-screen trade others run. Tom cannot die. A car hurts and never kills. Killing possible, rare, permanent. Guns rare and late; hold-ups rare and late.
- Britain, autumn 1990; the playable map is the Hook and its docks. Landlines, phone boxes, cash, paper.
- The clock: 60 real seconds a game hour, so a game day is 24 minutes of play and seven game weeks are about 19.6 hours. The calendar is seven weeks: week one the inheritance, about five weeks of middle, a short last act. The main line is about twelve hours; about twenty-five side stories live inside the same weeks. Total 15 to 20 hours.
- The player mostly acts: drives (a large share of every hour), collects, does jobs, breaks in, fights, works the street. Talk (typed live by the player to characters who answer in voice) is where consequences surface, well under half of play. This is not a taxi simulator and not a talking game.
- Tom's past is never told and nobody asks. Tom's lines in conversation are typed by the player; so Tom must be somebody through what he does, wants, risks, refuses and is laughed at for, and through a few fixed deeds and fixed short lines at authored beats that you write.

## The four reader lessons (they sank or lifted every premise)

1. One warm, funny, specific bond at the centre lifted a premise every time. Put it in almost every week: trust, strain, breach, a settled last scene.
2. Money as the plot sank a premise every time (deposits, leases, signatures, cost sums). So did a lead who is a function, and people who stay roles.
3. The wrong blow must not be forced. A wrong the player is made to commit reads as a mission on rails and makes the lead hard to care for. The false belief must be one the player reaches himself from evidence that honestly points there; there must be more than one person he could fairly choose; there must be a full road for the player who holds back, and the truth must still land on him. Say exactly what each road plays like.
4. Bleak endings cost marks. An ending image that pays off the player's own record gained them. Loss stays loss; it is not mended; but the last image is earned and not merely grim.

## Where the last story fell short (do not repeat)

The lead was quiet, little happened to him, and the loudest scenes belonged to others (lead 6.7 against 10.0). The opponent was thin and nobody was faced in person at the end (6.0 against 8.3). Moments that hit, humour and side stories were each 2.3 behind. Stakes were small and the weekly middle was slow. It was level or ahead on choices that come back, the turn and play; keep those.

So: a lead with a plain want that is forced to change midway, the cost of not changing felt. One opponent inside the lead's circle who gains ground in view and is faced in person at the end. Losses prepared for hours. Comedy from who people are, in the main line and the side stories, steady beside the violence (dry British wit; never American satire). Stakes a player feels in the hands: bodies, the office, the people, the street's regard.

## Idea 10, against machine habits

Few people, counted: at most four carried through every week, at most eight more named in the main line. Not everything mended. No tidy arc for everyone. No speeches that explain the theme; nobody says "ledger" as a metaphor. Specific, strange, restrained details in place of general ones. No scene whose only job is to deliver information.
