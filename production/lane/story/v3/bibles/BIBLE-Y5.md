# BIBLE-Y5: The Arrested Ship

## 0. Head note

The last ship in the Hook cannot leave. A stevedoring firm went bust owing Mickey, Mickey had a Soviet timber ship held for the debt, and Tom Nowak inherits her with the cab office: nine unpaid people aboard, a paper taped to a mast, and among them a master from Riga who books a cab every night at ten to nowhere, to be off his ship for an hour, and pays in tinned fish. Tom wants what is owed, then wants her to stay, because the ship makes him somebody: the Hook calls him the Admiral. Ron Kirby becomes her watchman and is happy for the first time since 1989. Maureen Jensen, the Widow, sends the bread and wants her held for good. When the ship's brass vanishes the player hunts a thief among four fair suspects; the truth is that the master carried it out in Tom's own cab, to keep his crew fed and lit. A man dies on the dark ship. Tom's want turns over: to send the last ship away, against the Widow, with the old dockers working her one last time. The master's log has kept every kindness and every charge.

What changed from the premise, and why. The wrong blow is no longer fixed. There are four people the evidence fairly points at, a road for the player who holds back and a better one for the player who works it out, and one thing every player did with his own hands: he drove the brass out himself, a bag at a time. The settlement and the signature are gone. Nobody offers a deal on paper; the release is a deed with the hands, and the debt, Tom's first want, comes back once, in a biscuit tin, in the last scene. There is one opponent and she is inside the office's circle: the Widow, who feeds the ship. Cammack and Agar are light. The loss is a named seaman, prepared from week two. The master is deported only on the worst road. The endings are decided by who turns out on the quay and by what the log has kept. The law of holding a ship stays plain and in the background, and is marked as invented in section 11.

## 1. What is ending

The work of a port, and the last ship that proves the Hook is one. The real ground is this. The National Dock Labour Scheme, which since 1947 reserved dock work for registered men, ended on 3 July 1989, the day the Act passed. The strike began on 10 July and was called off on 1 August; a port manager remembered it as over in three weeks. By November 1990 a middling old port had lost more than four in five of its dockers, each with a cheque of up to £35,000, a rate that closes in early 1991. Eleven employers went insolvent and took 868 men's jobs with them. Casual hiring came straight back through labour supply firms. Things that had been crimes became jobs: at one port managers themselves drove cars off a Russian ship. Old dock land was being bought by the hectare. In Meridian this is the Old Basin in the autumn of 1990: one ship, half discharged since the spring, when her agents and stevedores, Hallam and Pask, became one of the firms that went under.

The player sees it end in seven steps, one a week, and he can count each of them from the driving seat.

Week one, 15 to 21 October. The Lodger, as the Hook calls her, is lit from end to end at night, the one bright thing on the dock. At six each morning sixty men stand at the gate fence and the Widow's foreman takes twenty. Three cranes stand on the timber berth and none has moved since May. The ship's bell rings at nine each morning, which is noon aboard, because she keeps Moscow time.

Week two, 22 to 28 October. The ship's deck lights go out to save fuel. Five of her seamen appear in the pen at the gate and are picked first, because they are cheapest, and men who held books for thirty years go home unpicked. A man of Ron's old gang signs for his cheque and takes a cab to the station with one case. On the Sunday the town puts its clocks back and the ship does not, so the Hook drifts another hour from her.

Week three, 29 October to 4 November. Things begin to leave the ship: brass, cable, and on the Monday the bell. Quay Street, which has set its mornings by it since May, notices before Tom does. Danny Cammack's lads start cutting up the first crane under a lawful contract. The lights in the ship's accommodation go out, deck by deck.

Week four, 5 to 11 November. The Harbour Board posts its notice: the Old Basin closes to shipping on Saturday 1 December and any vessel lying there will be removed. The pen is down to forty. The bell rings again, at six, from the Widow's gate hut.

Week five, 12 to 18 November. The ship goes dark altogether. Only a paraffin lamp in the galley and one in the master's cabin show from the quay. The second crane comes down. The master does not ring for his cab.

Week six, 19 to 25 November. The Widow's men stand on the quay under the ship. Her gate will not pick any man who works the Lodger. The last of Ron's gang who have not signed are told the cheque gets smaller after the winter.

Week seven, 26 November to 2 December. For four days the Old Basin smells of pine: the old gang, or what the player's record brings of it, discharges her by hand, once, for nobody's wages but the crew's. On the Friday night's tide she sails, or on the Saturday at noon she is towed out dark. On the Monday a fence goes up across the berth. Whichever way she goes, the Hook has no ship, and on the best road it is Tom who sent her.

## 2. What is owed

The one question: what does Tom owe the dead man and the living, when what the dead man left him is a claim on nine living men? Mickey left two things about the ship and they pull opposite ways. He had her held for £4,100, and he drove her master round the Hook every night for nothing much, because, as Ron says, "that was Mickey: he'd have the coat off your back and then give you a lift home." Tom inherits the paper and the lift. The game asks which of the two was the inheritance, and it asks through things the player handles, never in a speech.

Fares. Every booking goes in the fare book before the journey, by law, and a constable may read it. The night fare is written by Tom's own hand from his second night: time, car, from, to, how many, miles, what was paid. Sheila will not have "nowhere" in her book ("Nowhere isn't in the Hook. Write: as directed"), and the fare is paid in tins of sprats, which she enters at 31p a tin. The shelf of tins behind the counter is the count of what Tom has charged a man with no money.

The slate. Ron calls it the slate, though it is only a square of board painted black, and it hangs at the foot of the gangway. Whatever Tom carries aboard, Ron chalks: one sack potatoes, one drum paraffin, watchman eight hours. Chalked, it is charged to the ship and swells the debt Tom means to collect. If the player wants a thing given he must wipe the line with Tom's own sleeve, in front of the crew, and it is slow and it shows. Doing nothing charges it. The master copies the slate into his log every night in two columns.

Debts. Mickey's debt book holds five small debts and one large one in red: M.V. Onega, £4,100, held. The five are the game's lessons in collecting. The sixth cannot be collected from anyone aboard, and the player learns it one tin at a time. In week five Tom adds a seventh line in his own hand.

Rent. Tom owes £18 every Friday for the office. The ship is called the Lodger because she pays nothing for her berth. Each Friday Sheila does both sums aloud, his and hers, and the joke of the name wears through: a lodger who cannot pay cannot leave either.

Favours. The Widow sends forty-two loaves a week to the ship and has since May. She has never asked for anything. In week five she says what forty-two loaves a week for six months come to, and she does not mean money. The men at the gate keep a like account in shifts: every morning a seaman is picked, a docker is not, and the dockers know whose ship the seamen came off.

Witnesses. Three books watch the ship and they do not keep the same day. The fare book and Ron's watch book keep the Hook's time. The master's log keeps the ship's, three hours ahead, so the ten o'clock fare is always written aboard as tomorrow's. People watch too: a window on Quay Street that is never quite dark, a woman above the ferry landing, the men at the fence at midnight. What they saw is true and partial, and the player's false belief is built from true and partial things.

The fare book again. In week six DS Ellis reads it. A book kept true says "as directed", shows its miles, and convicts nobody. A book the player had tidied does not match the dial.

What the ending settles. Who is paid first. On the best road the eight living seamen are paid their wages from the cargo, the dead one goes home in his ship, the paper comes off the mast by Tom's hand, and Mickey's £4,100 is ruled through unpaid. Tom's own line in the debt book stays. Nobody rules it through, because nobody can collect it.

## 3. The lead

What Tom wants in week one. What is owed. He steps off the ferry with £23.50, one suitcase and a letter, and the only large thing in the inheritance is a debt with a ship attached. He is a man who counts and collects: he knows what is in his pocket to the penny, he squares what is crooked, and his whole case is two words, "It's owed." By the second week the want has grown into the ship herself. She makes him somebody for the first time on the page. Men come to the office asking for a shift minding her. The Old Basin stays open because a ship held on his paper cannot be moved off it. The Hook gives him a name. He wants her to stay.

What forces it to change, and when. Week four takes away his innocence: he learns that he carried the ship's brass out himself, a bag at a time, and that the men he has been feeding on the slate are his prisoners. He tries a half measure, to keep her and feed her better. Week five takes away the half measure: Juris Kalns dies on a ship that was dark because Tom held her. From the infirmary kerb on, he wants the opposite of what he came for: to send her away whole, crew paid, under her own master, and to do it against the woman who wants what he used to want.

What it costs if he does not. It is played, not told. The night fare stops ringing. The ship goes dark. The Widow takes the crew's papers, then the watch, then Ron. The third ending is the one where he gets exactly what he wanted in week two.

Five deeds only he would do.

1. He straightens his own writ. On his second day he goes to the mast to read the paper that makes the ship his, finds it hanging by one corner, and tapes it up square before he walks away.
2. He takes fish and cannot eat fish. He accepts two tins a night with a nod and stacks them label out behind the counter. He gives every one away. He eats one tin in the whole game, to the last sprat, at the crew's table in week six, and nobody aboard remarks on it.
3. He keeps her time. In week two he winds Mickey's dead desk clock, sets it three hours fast and stands it by the fare book. "That clock's wrong." "It's right for her."
4. He spells the name. For a month he gets every name aboard wrong, and his notebook shows his own spellings. At the infirmary desk he spells Juris Kalns right, letter by letter.
5. He books himself. That same night he opens Mickey's debt book and writes a seventh line in his own hand: "T. Nowak. One man." There is no sum.

The fixed lines. Everything else Tom says is the player's. These are his.

- Week one, the first night: "Where to?" And at the foot of the gangway: "It's owed."
- Week one, to Sheila, over the book: "That's where we went."
- Week two, to Ron, giving him the watch: "She's yours nights. I only own her."
- Week four, in the cab, stopped at the fence: "As directed." Then: "How much of her did I carry?"
- Week five, at the infirmary desk: "Kalns. K, A, L, N, S. Juris. Twenty-two."
- Week five, at dawn, to the master: "Tell me what you need. Not what you can pay."
- Week seven, to the Widow, over the tin: "It's owed. Not to me."
- Week seven, on the lock steps: "Ten o'clock."

What he risks and refuses. His body, at the gangway and in the dock water; the office, when Ellis has the book in her hands; and the only name the Hook has ever given him. He refuses to be driven: nobody else takes the wheel while Tom is in the car, until the last night, when the master says, "Tonight I drive."

What is funny about him. He has £23.50 and a ship of three thousand tons that he cannot sail, sell, pronounce or board without asking leave of her mate, who salutes him and then makes him wait. He is paid nightly in a food he cannot stand. He is sick over the rail of a vessel that is tied to a wall. The Hook salutes him across the street and calls him the Admiral; Danny Cammack asks after the fleet. He writes a fare line like a man filling in a customs form, and Sheila corrects him as if he were nineteen. Pauline tells the whole radio that he is walking out with a Russian. He never explains himself and never answers a joke, and that is why they keep coming: he would sooner be laughed at than leave a thing uncollected, and later he would sooner be laughed at than leave it ungiven.

## 4. The people who matter

Four are carried through every week: Captain Ozols, Ron, Sheila and the Widow (who has section 5). Eight more are named in the main line: Juris Kalns, Zina, Cinderella, Darren Milner, Danny Cammack, DS Ellis, Pauline Kitching and June Suddaby. The rest of the crew are known by their rank. Everyone else in the town lives in the side stories.

Captain Arvids Ozols, 56, master of the Onega. The bond at the centre.

What he wants for himself. To be under way. He has been at sea since he was nineteen and has never spent more than six weeks on land. Home is not the point: "A ship that does not move is a building, and I am not a caretaker." He wants motion, his command, and to bring nine people back as he took them out. The cab at ten is the nearest he can come to it: "At ten o'clock I am under way."

The deed only he would do. He treats the cab as a vessel. He asks leave to come aboard, sits in front, and keeps a pocket log of the voyages: "Voyage 12. Berth four to nowhere and return. Wind south-west, rain. Driver T. Nowak." He names the Hook's street lamps as lights ("Fixed, white. That one is occulting. It is a bad bulb"). He presses his one uniform jacket every night, and he wears it to carry his own ship's brass ashore.

How he talks. The English of an old textbook and of the sea forecast on the long wave. Asked how he is, he answers as visibility: "Good, becoming moderate."

- "To nowhere, please. One hour."
- "I am not Russian. I am from Riga. It is very near to Russia. That is the difficulty."
- "Your uncle drove very badly. I miss it."
- "On a ship, when a thing is missing, first you count the crew."
- "For us it is already tomorrow."

The bond, week by week. Trust in weeks one and two: the hour, the tour, the tins. Strain in week three: a bag, and a name said in the car. Breach in week four: the confession. Silence in week five, broken by one word, "Hospital." Repair in week six, when he books again and names places. The settled last scene in week seven, on his bridge.

How he changes or refuses to. He will not leave before his crew and he will not leave his dead. He changes in one thing: at the end he stops keeping another country's time, and sets the ship's clock by Tom's watch.

Ron Kirby, 58. The second bond.

What he wants for himself. A ship to mind. For thirty years he went aboard ships to empty them and was never once asked to sit down on one. He wants to be the last man on the Hook who has a ship. He would not say so, but he also wants the galley at ten, where Zina puts a bowl in front of him without being asked.

The deed only he would do. His first act as watchman is to take down the gangway net and rig it again from nothing, because "a net's not a net like that." He stands the whole watch on his feet and writes it up with a carpenter's pencil in docker's hours. He has not hit a man since 1984, and the Hook does not know.

How he talks. "Boss." Short, coastal, a building named by what it used to be, rain by four. The master's name defeats him in a day; he calls him Arthur, and the master answers to it.

- "You don't own a ship, boss. You mind her."
- "Arthur says it's tomorrow already where he is. I said, then tell us if it rains."
- "Thirty year on ships, and that's the first cup of tea I never brought meself."
- "It's my watch. Whatever's gone, it went past me."

How he changes or refuses to. Happy, then shamed, then a man dies on his watch and he hands in the book. When Tom says the ship must go, Ron hears that she is being taken off him, and the Widow has a coat his size. He refuses one thing throughout: he will not search the master. "You don't search a master coming off his own ship."

Sheila Dunn, 53. The keeper of the true line.

What she wants for herself. A book that anybody may read and that will stand. And one private thing: in thirty-one years of keeping the cab book of a dock she has never set foot on a deck, because nobody asked her.

The deed only she would do. Asked aboard at last, she goes up the gangway in her good coat on Ron's arm, sits at the master's table, sets his log beside her fare book and audits a Soviet ship. She writes out, in her own hand under the office stamp, what the Onega owes nine people for two hundred days. Nobody asked for it. It puts Tom tenth in the queue.

How she talks. Exact. Four words where four will do. "Mr Nowak", and no adjectives.

- "Nowhere isn't in the Hook."
- "Sprats, two tins, at thirty-one pence. I'll not guess at a herring."
- "Maureen Jensen has never once been late with a loaf or early with a kindness."
- "That's more than the book will stand, love. You sure?"

How she changes or refuses to. She will not write a false line for anybody, the master included, and it turns out to be the thing that saves him. She changes twice. In week five she reads the seventh line in the debt book, rules nothing through, and says, "Go to bed, Tom." On the game's last page she writes the word she has refused for seven weeks.

Juris Kalns, 22, motorman, from Liepaja. He wants to see the place he knows by ear. For five months he has listened to the cab office on the ship's receiver, and he knows the Hook by Pauline's directions without ever having passed the dock gate. His deed: in the passenger seat he calls each turn before she does, in her accent. "Past the chapel, second left, the house with the slippers on the step. Is it true, the slippers?" He does not change. He is the one who is lost.

The light ones.

- Zina, 51, the cook, from Odessa. She feeds Ron, argues dill against vinegar, and guards the last jar of jam. It was she who said it first: "A bell you cannot eat."
- The bosun, 60, from the White Sea coast. He chips and paints one plate a day so that she will not look abandoned.
- The Chief, 58, a Georgian. He sings to the generator and grows onions in a lifeboat.
- The mate, 34. By the book, waiting for orders from a company that does not answer, and frightened of going home having obeyed nobody.
- The radio operator, who calls the Line each day at the same hour and logs "No reply"; and two seamen, one who fishes from the stern and one who writes letters he cannot post.
- Cinderella, 57, of Ron's old gang, always off just before twelve. He keeps Ron company on the evening watch, has not signed for his cheque, and is too proud to say where he goes at midnight.
- Darren Milner, 25. He sells what he hears and what he is handed, runs the crew's errands for tins, and has named the tins Baltic caviar.
- Danny Cammack. Scrap, by lawful contract, and loud about it. "It's all scrap, pal. You, me, her."
- DS Carol Ellis, who comes for a dead man and reads the fare book standing up.
- Pauline Kitching, on the radio, forty a day, who says goodnight to a ship.
- June Suddaby, night nurse, Mickey's daughter, who nursed three of the crew in the summer and never told the office.

## 5. The opponent

Maureen Jensen, 60, the Widow. She runs the dock gate lawfully, as a labour supply firm that picks twenty men from the pen each morning, and behind the gate she runs what she has always run: cigarettes by the case, landed at night.

Why she is inside Tom's circle. She is the office's oldest friend. She and Sheila have had tea at the counter every Wednesday for thirty years. Ron stood in her husband's gang and carried his coffin. She and Mickey kept the Lodger between them: "Mickey had the paper and I had the bread. It worked very well. Nobody went without, and nobody went." She is the first person in the Hook who is kind to Tom, she is kind to him every week, and each kindness is real.

What she wants for herself. The ship held, for good. A ship that cannot move keeps a berth open. Nobody may search her or shift her. Nine people who cannot leave will work for bread. And when the Board's notice runs out, an abandoned ship is sold where she lies, to whoever is standing nearest. She wants exactly what Tom wants in week two, and she is better at it.

The deed only she would do. She finds out what a person is short of and sends it before they ask, in the right size. In week two nine pairs of winter boots come up the gangway and every pair fits, because her foreman read the footprints on a frosty quay.

How she talks. Quiet, domestic, exact. She never threatens and never raises her voice.

- "You'll not have eaten."
- "I send forty-two loaves. I don't like a man to have to count his bread."
- "I never ask anybody for anything, Mr Nowak. I find out what they're short of."
- "It's all lawful now. That's what they did for us in eighty-nine. I only pick up what they put down."

How she gains ground, where the player sees it.

- Week one: the bread van is at the gangway before Tom's potatoes.
- Week two: five of his seamen are in her pen, and nine pairs of her boots are on his ship.
- Week three: her foreman takes his tea with Ron at the gangway, and admires the watch.
- Week four: the ship's bell rings the call at her gate, and the crew's nine seamen's books are in her gate office, "for the hiring; it's the law now".
- Week five: she rides in Tom's cab and says what he owes her. That week her cases go into the ship's hold.
- Week six: her men stand the quay, her gate is shut to anyone who works the Lodger, Darren carries her messages unless Tom has kept him, and the coat she had made for Ron is on a hook in her hut.
- Week seven: she comes down the quay herself.

The meeting in person. Friday 30 November, nine at night, an hour before the tide (scene 5). She has never once been to the ship; she has only ever sent. She walks down alone ahead of her men with a biscuit tin and offers Tom, in notes, the thing he stepped off the ferry for. She does not change. What she says when she has lost is true, and he has to hear it: "You've sent away the last ship this town will see, and you'll call it a kindness. I'd have kept her lit till she rusted through. Ask them in a year which of us they'd have had."

## 6. The seven weeks

The night fare rings at ten on forty-two of the game's forty-seven nights. About two a week are authored. The others can be driven in a short form, with one new remark from the master, or left to the night driver, and the master's log notes who drove. A night fare is a staged drive of about three real minutes with the town's clock held while they talk. No middle week can be cut: two makes the ship worth wanting and puts Juris in the passenger seat, three is the hunt, four the turn, five the loss, six the change of sides.

Week one, 15 to 21 October: the inheritance. About 110 minutes.

Beats. Monday: the ferry. From the gangway Tom sees a freighter in the Old Basin with washing on a line. Ron, on the rank: "That's the Lodger. She's yours and all." Sheila's walk-round ends at the debt book and its red line. A first fare at dusk teaches the car. At twenty to ten Pauline calls the Russian's time and Tom drives the first hour to nowhere (scene 1). Tuesday: Sheila turns the book round and he writes the line himself. The first carry: potatoes and paraffin through the dock gate to the gangway, with an envelope under his coat for the gate man who looks away, and a constable on the quay. He reads the paper on the mast and tapes it straight. Wednesday: the Widow at the counter with a plate under a cloth. Ron asks for the watch. Thursday: Cammack's lads are stripping lead from the timber shed and walk up the gangway to look her over. The first fight is on the quay, with the crew at the rail, and Danny Cammack, from his van window, gives him the name that sticks: "Evening, Admiral." Friday: rent, and Sheila's two sums. Saturday: the match crowd salutes the Admiral. Sunday: Sheila, on her day off: "So which is it going to be? Collect on her, keep her, or let her go?"

The hands: driving; a carry through watched ground by three ways (the lit quay, the lane behind the cold stores, the cab); a fight, or a man faced down; the count; the slate.

In the car: the master shows Tom his own town by night. By day a talker: "That Russian boat's haunted. It's only washing."

Funny: Ron takes down the gangway net in front of nine seamen who rigged it and rigs it again in silence. The bosun, who has no English, tests it with his boot and shakes his hand.

Grave: at the mast. The paper is dated 5 May. The master, beside him: "One hundred and sixty-four days. I have counted them for you." Tom: "It's owed." "Yes. By men I cannot find, to a man who is dead. And here are we two, who did not do it."

Choices. Take the tins or refuse them (the shelf; what the log calls the fare). Chalk the slate or wipe it. Fight Cammack's lads, face them down or step back (whether they come for the timber in week seven; how the crew look at him). Take up the Widow's bread or carry his own (what she counts in week five). Sunday's answer, which is said back to him twice.

Week two, 22 to 28 October: the Admiral. About 100 minutes.

Beats. Monday: Tom gives Ron the watch. Men come to the counter for shifts; the ship has made the office busy. Tuesday night, the first crime with his own hands: a break-in at Hallam and Pask's sealed office for the Lodger's cargo book. Three ways in: the caretaker's key lifted from his pocket, the yard door his round leaves open at eleven, or force. The book says what is still in her, four hundred standards of sawn pine, and who is owed, which is everybody. Wednesday at six: five men in padded jackets are picked first from the pen. Cinderella, unpicked, to Ron: "Your lodgers have had my shift." At tea, the boots. Thursday: Juris rides. Friday: a man of the old gang signs for his cheque and books a cab to the station. Saturday: Tom winds the desk clock. Sunday: the clocks go back. The master, at ten: "Tonight your town goes one hour further from us."

The hands: the break-in; a tail on the foreman, which shows the seamen are paid in fuel chits and not in money; diesel drums carried aboard by cab; driving.

In the car: Juris calls the turns. The cheque man: "Thirty-one year. They've put a number on it, and the number goes down after the winter." He asks Tom to stop at the gate, looks through the fence for a minute, and gets back in.

Funny: Juris meets Pauline. He had pictured somebody of twenty. She is fifty-eight, with a cigarette going in each ashtray. He gives her a tin with both hands. Pauline, to every cab on the air: "Car two, your Russian's fetched me a suitor."

Grave: the station. The cheque man's case on the back seat, and nothing said after the gate.

Choices. Stand the seamen down from the pen by putting them on the slate to paint and mind their own ship (the dockers get their shifts back, the debt swells, the Widow notices) or leave them there (the crew eat; the dockers remember whose crew it was). Take Juris ashore or not. Tell Pauline she has a listener or not: if he does, she ends each night with "and goodnight to the Lodger". From this week Juris leans on the rail at ten and sends the cab off in her voice: "Car two, where are you, love?"

Week three, 29 October to 4 November: the brass. About 110 minutes.

Beats. Monday: nine o'clock comes and no bell. By Ron's count the bell, the wheelhouse clock, sixty fathoms of copper cable and four hose nozzles are gone. Sheila: "She's held in your name. What walks off her walks off you." Tuesday to Saturday: the hunt (scene 2). Three leads, worked in any order: Cinderella at ten to twelve, Cammack's lock-up, Darren and a ship's clock. The fourth nobody says aloud: the watchman. Thursday: the bosun pipes the Admiral aboard for an inspection, as a joke, and Tom is sick over the rail. Every night at ten: the master and his bag. Saturday, two in the morning: Ron, in the passenger seat.

The hands: tails on foot and by car; casing; a break-in; the lower rungs of the ladder; three books read side by side.

In the car: the master asks whom Tom suspects, and the player types a name or does not. "On a ship, when a thing is missing, first you count the crew." Zina rides to the Thursday market, pays in soup, and lets fall that the Captain has not eaten at table for a week. Ron at two: "It's my watch. Whatever's gone, it went past me. Say it, if you're thinking it."

Funny: the inspection. Also Darren's stall outside the caff, with a card in felt pen: BALTIC CAVIAR, AS EATEN BY ADMIRALS.

Grave: Ron at two in the morning, asking to be accused.

Choices. Whom to follow first, and how far up the ladder to go with him. What to say to the master, who writes down every name he hears. Whether to answer Ron. Whether to read the watch book closely enough to see what it says.

Week four, 5 to 11 November: the turn. About 110 minutes.

Beats. Monday, Bonfire Night: the display on the quay for the Hook's adults. The Widow's men hand out baked potatoes, and everyone the player suspects is standing in one firelit crowd. He acts on his belief or he does not (scene 2). Rockets come down on the Lodger's hatch covers; the crew run out the hoses, and the hoses have no nozzles. Tuesday at six: the Lodger's bell rings the call at the Widow's gate. Her foreman, asked: "Bought fair, off her master. It comes in your cab, Admiral. We thought you were in on it. We've been paying your fare." Tuesday at ten: the cab (scene 3). Wednesday: the Board's notice is taped to the gate and to the gangway. The Widow has the nine seamen's books. Thursday night, or sooner for the player who worked it out: Tom goes into her store behind the gate and brings out what one man can carry in one holdall, the bell or the nozzles. Sunday, Remembrance: at eleven the crew stand at their rail, unasked, facing the chapel.

The hands: the blow, or the watching; buckets and wet sacks on a burning tarpaulin; a night break-in by three ways (a key lifted in the potato queue, the gap the bread van uses at five, or force); a carry home past her men.

In the car: the confession. On one road Cinderella rides to the infirmary and says nothing until the door: "I'll thank you not to tell Ron where I was."

Funny: the Board's clerk comes to tape up the notice that she must leave. Ron makes him ask the mate's leave to come aboard. The mate salutes him and makes him wait.

Grave: the confession, and nine people beating out a fire with sacks because the brass that would have put it out was sold for bread.

Choices. The blow: who, and how far (scene 2). The bell or the nozzles: the bell rings at nine again and the whole Hook hears that he took it back from her; the nozzles are what the ship needs; whichever he leaves, she keeps. Whether to go on driving the master with his bag, to drive him without it, or to stop. Whether to ask Sheila to make the eight-mile nights read six: "That's more than the book will stand, love. You sure?"

Week five, 12 to 18 November: the dark. About 105 minutes.

Beats. Monday: the half measure. Tom will keep her and feed her himself. The first fuel lift: the Board's bowser on the fish dock at night, a hand pump, four drums, the cab low on its springs past the constable. At ten Pauline says, "The Russian's not rung." It is the first of four silent nights. Tuesday: the Widow books a cab and gets into the back. The sit-down is driven. Wednesday: by his answer, her tap closes or her cases go into the hold under an open hatch. Thursday, ten at night: Juris falls (scene 4). Friday, dawn, the infirmary kerb. The master, asked how he is: "Poor." Then: "Let us go home, Tom." Tom's line. He writes the seventh line in the debt book. Sheila: "Go to bed, Tom." Saturday: Ron hands in the watch book. Sunday: the mate comes to the office in uniform to report a death to the party holding the ship, by the book, and cannot finish the sentence.

The sit-down, and what each answer plays like. Accept: she keeps the lights on and the bread coming, her cases go into the hold that week, and the master will not ride: "I do not ride with my keeper's partner." Defy: she shuts her fuel tap, the ship is dark from Wednesday, and he keeps the master's regard. Counter with what he knows of her store and her gate book: she leaves the tap open a week and turns to Ron instead; the master, ashamed, orders the generator off at night himself: "We burn only what is ours." On every road the deck is dark on Thursday and a hatch is open: for her cases, or because the crew have been burning offcuts of the cargo to keep warm.

The hands: the lift; the choice on Thursday of whether Ron comes on the second lift or stays on the gangway; the fastest drive in the game.

In the car: the Widow's account. Then the master and Juris.

Funny: the hand pump squeaks. Ron hums to cover it. The constable stops to ask whether that is a hymn, and Ron, who has not been inside the chapel since his wedding, says which one.

Grave: the fall, and the kerb at dawn.

Choices. The sit-down. Ron with him or on the gangway: if Ron is with Tom, nobody on the quay hears for some minutes and it is Tom who took the watchman away; if Ron stays, he is the one who goes down the ladder, and a man has died on his watch. Juris dies on both. What changes is who carries it and how Ron stands in week seven. Nobody sends for an ambulance: the crew have no papers, and people with no papers do not ring for anyone in a uniform.

Week six, 19 to 25 November: letting go. About 95 minutes.

Beats. Monday: the master rings again, and for the first time names a place: the infirmary mortuary. "They will fly eight men. They will not fly a box. If she sails, he goes home in her." So the plan: cargo out, wages paid, fuel in, the paper down, and the night tide of Friday the thirtieth. Tuesday: Tom tells Ron the ship must go. "You're taking her off me." Wednesday: DS Ellis reads the fare book. Thursday: Sheila goes aboard, and at the crew's table Tom eats the fish. Friday night: the nine books. Saturday at six: Tom walks the fence at the pen and asks for a gang. Sunday: the Widow answers. Her gate will never again pick a man who works the Lodger, her men stand the quay, and there is a coat on a hook in her hut with WATCH across the shoulders.

The books, three keys. His own break-in at the gate office, planned by the player: which car, who comes, which way. Or Ellis's search, if he gives her the Widow's trade, and then the pen knows who talked to a detective. Or a trade with the Widow: the bell or the nozzles back and a week's quiet, for eight books; she keeps the master's.

Ellis and the book. Kept true, it says "as directed" and shows its miles, and she closes it. Sheila: "It has said as directed since 1961, Carol, and you have read every page." Tidied, it does not match the dial, and Ellis will be on the quay on the last night with a charge to read.

The hands: the job; calling on men one at a time; the last lift; driving the master to somewhere.

In the car: the master with destinations, and the tide table: "Twenty-two ten. You will be late for your own fare." Sheila rides to the berth, pays 80p, and enters herself in the book.

Funny: Sheila aboard. The mate salutes her and does not make her wait. She tells the master his sevens are crossed. "So that we do not mistake them for ones." "Mickey's looked like ones. It cost him a Tuesday once."

Grave: the mortuary. And Ron, who is told and not asked, unless the player asks.

Choices. How the books come back. Whether Ron is asked or told. Whom Tom asks at the fence, when all he can offer a man is the Widow's ban.

Week seven, 26 November to 2 December: the last discharge. About 90 minutes.

Beats. Monday at seven the hatch boards come off, and who is on the quay is the record (section 8). Monday to Thursday: four days of pine. Tom drives the loads to the timber yard on the north road in a borrowed lorry, through a watching town and past the gate. Cammack's lads come for a load, or do not. Wednesday: the yard pays on the tally, and Sheila pays eight seamen across the office counter in notes; each counts it twice in front of her, as she would; the ninth packet she seals and hands to the master. Thursday: fuel. Friday 30 November: at dusk the box comes aboard. At nine, the Widow (scene 5). At ten past ten, the tide. Saturday noon: the Board's tug. Sunday: ten o'clock.

The hands: slinging and stacking beside the gang; the lorry runs, each a load everyone can see, with the gate blocked on Wednesday (through, round by the cold stores, or a word); the fight for the gangway; the paper.

In the car: whoever rides the load. The last voyage is not in the car.

Funny: Saturday noon. The tug's skipper, to Ron, on an empty berth: "I was told there'd be a ship." "There was."

Grave: the box. The crew stand at the rail where they stood on Remembrance Sunday, and the bosun pipes.

Choices. The lorry's way. Whether to hold the gangway. The rest is what he has already done.

## 7. Key scenes written out

Scene 1. The opening night: to nowhere, please. Monday 15 October, twenty to ten.

The office. Pauline does not look up from the radio. "Twenty to ten. That's the Russian's time. Berth four. Mickey always took that one himself." Sheila is already writing: "Car two."

At the foot of the gangway a man in a pressed uniform jacket stands under the one lamp, holding a string bag. He salutes the cab's roof sign.

OZOLS: Good evening. Permission to come aboard?

He waits until the player answers or opens the door. He sits in front.

OZOLS: I am Ozols, master. You are the nephew. I am sorry for your uncle. He drove very badly.

TOM (fixed): Where to?

OZOLS: To nowhere, please. One hour.

No destination is given and none appears. The player drives where he likes, and whatever he passes the master knows, because he has seen it on a hundred and forty-one nights from this seat. A foreigner shows the heir his own town.

At the corner lamp on Quay Street: "Occulting. It is a bad bulb since June." At the tobacconist: "Here your uncle stops always for cigarettes. He leaves the engine. I am not permitted to touch anything." At a dark window across from the office: "There is a lady who does not sleep. I raise my hand. She does not. But she has seen." At the dock gate: "At six, sixty men. I watch from the bridge. It is like the selling of fish." Down a dead end: "Your uncle also."

If the player stops at the pierhead, the master gets out, stands at the rail for four minutes by his watch looking at open water, and gets back in. "Thank you. Now I have been somewhere." If the player asks how he is: "Good, becoming moderate." If the player drives past the hour: "Mister Nowak. My ship becomes jealous."

Back at the berth he takes two tins from the string bag and stands them on the dashboard.

OZOLS: Mickey's tariff. One tin, the half hour.

The player takes them, or holds a button and pushes them back. Left alone, Tom takes them.

OZOLS: You wish to ask about the four thousand one hundred pounds. Mickey also, each Monday. Yes. That I cannot pay in fish.

TOM (fixed): It's owed.

OZOLS: It is owed. Good night. Tomorrow at ten. (He looks at his watch, which says five past one.) For us it is already tomorrow.

At the head of the gangway he salutes the flag aft, and then something lower down, which the player can just see is a paper on the mast.

Upstairs, the money page of Tom's notebook reads: "£23.50 in your pocket. Two tins of sprats. Rent, £18, Friday." In the morning Sheila asks where he took the fare. He says nowhere. "Nowhere isn't in the Hook. Write: as directed." TOM (fixed): "That's where we went." She writes "as directed", and under paid: "Sprats, two tins, at 31p."

Scene 2. The false belief and its turn. Week three to Tuesday 6 November.

What the player holds by Saturday. Every item is true.

Cinderella keeps the evening watch with Ron and leaves at ten to twelve. Followed, he goes to the scrapyard fence and passes a heavy sack to a man with a van. He has not signed for his cheque and there is a poll tax summons on his mantelpiece. Cammack's van was on the berth road on two of the nights, and in his lock-up, among lead and quay brass, lies a porthole cover with Cyrillic letters on the rim. Darren is selling a ship's clock and lies about it badly. Ron's watch book is perfect, the Widow's foreman takes tea at his gangway, and at ten each night Ron leaves it for the galley. And there is a fifth thing, in Tom's own hand: most nights the fare book says six miles, and some nights eight.

What is behind each. Cinderella cuts up the dock's cranes at night for Cammack's contract and carries his share of the brass; he would sooner be taken for a thief than have Ron know. Cammack's lad bought the porthole cover from Darren for a fiver; the van was at the crane. Zina gave Darren the clock to sell for flour; frightened, he will name Cinderella to keep her out of it. Ron is innocent, and his book says so every night in his own pencil: "22.00 Master ashore, one bag. 23.00 Master aboard, one bag."

The roads, and what each plays like.

The fence. The player goes for Cinderella, at midnight by the scrapyard or in front of the whole Hook at the bonfire. He picks the rung: a word, a shove, the arm. Cinderella does not defend himself. "Not in front of Ron." It plays as a tail, a fence in the dark and a man who will not fight back. If the arm is broken it never mends straight, and the pen does not pick a one-armed man.

The lock-up. A break-in, then a choice of damage: take the porthole cover, put the van's windows in, or go for Danny's lad. Danny answers in person across the bonfire: "Wasn't me, Admiral. It is now." That night the rockets are aimed.

Darren. He runs. A chase through the yards and a hand on a collar. Shaken, he gives Cinderella's name. Handed to Ellis, he is out by morning and carrying the Widow's messages by the weekend.

The watch. A talk at the gangway. The player takes the book off Ron and stands the watch himself or gives it to the night driver. Ron goes home at ten for the first time in a fortnight, and Zina's galley door is shut to Tom.

Holding back. He drives, keeps the slate, follows whom he likes and touches nobody. Nothing stops him and nothing fails. One more eight-mile night goes by, and it takes the last two nozzles.

Working it out. A player who sets Ron's book beside his own lines can say it to the master, in the cab, on any night from the Thursday of week three; any plain accusation typed there is taken. The confession comes early, the master is relieved ("You count well, Mister Tom"), and there is time to bring the nozzles back before the fifth. On that road alone the hoses work, and the crew wash Cammack's lads off the quay.

The turn, the same for everyone. Tuesday, six in the morning. A bell Tom knows rings from the wrong end of the Hook. At the gate the foreman is hauling on its rope. "Bought fair, off her master. It comes in your cab, Admiral. We thought you were in on it. Mickey was. We've been paying your fare." The tins came off the Widow's shelf, two with every lot. The player who struck learns he struck a man for something he did himself. The player who held back has hurt nobody, has carried two hundred kilos of the ship out through the gate and written it down, and has the Widow's tins on his shelf, two for every lot.

Scene 3. The cab confession. Tuesday 6 November, ten at night.

He is at the foot of the gangway with the bag, as always. Permission to come aboard. To nowhere, please.

The player drives. On the eight-mile nights the master has always asked to stop by the gate fence, "to walk a little". If the player drives there now, unasked, and stops:

TOM (the player may type; if he says nothing, fixed): As directed.

OZOLS: Ah.

He puts the bag on his knees and opens it.

OZOLS: Tonight it is the hood of the compass. Seven kilos. You have very good suspension, Mister Tom. In your uncle's car one felt every kilo.

TOM (fixed): How much of her did I carry?

He takes out the pocket log and reads.

OZOLS: Voyage nine: clock, wheelhouse. Voyage eleven: nozzles, four. Voyage twelve: cable, copper, in three parts. Voyage fourteen: bell. I am sorry about the bell. Eleven voyages of twenty-two. Perhaps two hundred kilos. A master writes what leaves his ship.

OZOLS: Bread we are given. Light we must buy. Forty litres each day. When the light goes the men stop shaving, and after that it goes quickly; I have seen it. So first we sold what only shines. Then what we need. It is the wrong order to be hungry in.

OZOLS: Your uncle knew. He said: Arvids, a ship is metal. Sell the metal, I'll drive. I thought you were the same. On the third night I understood you were not. Then it was too late to be a man who had told you.

On a road with a blow, he adds: "On Thursday you said a name in this car. I said: perhaps. One word. I have written that also. Under mine, not under yours."

OZOLS: I do not ask you to forgive it. I ask you to drive. It is still my hour.

The player drives on to the fence, and the log will say "carried, knowing"; or drives him back with the bag unsold, and the ship is darker tomorrow; or puts him out, and the master walks home along the quay in his pressed jacket for the only time. At the gangway he pays. "These are from her shelf. I think you should know what you are paid in."

Scene 4. The loss. Thursday 15 November, ten at night.

Tom comes down the quay with the drums, Ron beside him or Ron already on the gangway. The ship is a black shape with one lamp in the galley. Then a shout in Russian that nobody translates, and the alarm: the bell, if the player brought the bell back, and the Hook comes to its doors; a tin tray beaten with a spanner if he did not.

Number two hatch has its boards off. At the bottom, on the timber, in the light of one torch, is Juris. He was crossing the deck to the radio room, for ten o'clock.

They bring him up on a hatch board. The bosun gives the orders, quietly.

MATE: Ambulance. We must telephone.

OZOLS: With what papers?

The seamen's books are in the Widow's gate office. He turns to Tom and gives a destination for the first time in the game.

OZOLS: Hospital.

Car two. Juris lies across the back seat with his head in the master's hands. Ron or Zina is in front. The player drives the fastest two miles in the game, through the gate, up the dock road, in the wet. The radio is on, and Pauline, who does not know, says what she says every night.

PAULINE: Car two, where are you, love?

JURIS: Past the chapel.

And then, a junction early, as he always called it:

JURIS: Second left.

It is the right turn for the infirmary. He says nothing else. After a while the master speaks, in English, so that it is said to Tom.

OZOLS: Slow now. There is no hurry now.

The player can go on driving flat out. Nothing changes, and the master does not say it twice.

The infirmary kerb. June Suddaby comes out in her cardigan, looks, and gets into the back of the car on her knees. Then she gets out.

JUNE: Name?

The mate begins to answer.

TOM (fixed): Kalns. K, A, L, N, S. Juris. Twenty-two.

JUNE: And who's bringing him in?

The player answers: the ship, the firm, or his own name. What he says goes on the form, and it is the form Ellis reads.

At eleven, in the empty car, the radio: "And goodnight to the Lodger." Only if the player ever told Pauline.

Dawn. The master sits on the infirmary wall in his jacket. If the player asks how he is: "Poor." Then:

OZOLS: Let us go home, Tom. Not for the ship. I have eight.

TOM (fixed): Tell me what you need. Not what you can pay.

At the office Tom takes down Mickey's debt book, and the game hands the player the pen and no words. If he closes the book, Tom opens it again that night. The line is his deed: "T. Nowak. One man." At seven Sheila reads it upside down and rules nothing through. "Go to bed, Tom." If he had her tidy the book, it is "Go to bed, Mr Nowak", and the name waits.

Scene 5. The last night and the meeting. Friday 30 November.

Nine o'clock. The last of the timber has gone from the quay. The box is aboard, under the forecastle, with the bosun beside it. The generator is running and every lamp she has is lit, deck by deck, in the order they went out. On the last stack sit the gang, as many as the record brought, with their tea.

The Widow comes down the quay on foot in her good coat, ahead of her foreman and four men. It is the first time she has come to the ship. She carries a biscuit tin.

WIDOW: You'll not have eaten.

She sets the tin on a bollard and takes off the lid.

WIDOW: Four thousand one hundred. It's what she owes Mickey's. Sheila can count it. I'll buy your paper, Mr Nowak. Take it down off that mast and hand it to me, and then I'm the one that's owed, and she stops where she is. Lit. Fed. Nobody goes without.

WIDOW: It's what you came for. You said so, the first Sunday.

She says back his Sunday answer, whichever it was. If he would not say: "You wouldn't say. I took that for wanting it."

The tin can be taken. The game asks twice. If he leaves it:

TOM (fixed): It's owed. Not to me.

WIDOW: Then she wants a watch on her, and from midnight the Board's given me the keeping of that berth. All lawful. Ron.

Where Ron stands is the record. If Tom asked him and never took the watch off him, Ron is at Tom's shoulder: "Evening, Maureen." He steps onto the foot of the gangway, the first man he has squared up to since 1984. If Ron is in her coat, he walks to the gangway to take it for her. The player can speak to him, stand aside, or go through him. If the record holds one thing in Tom's favour that Ron himself saw, Ron stops, takes the coat off, folds it and hangs it on the bollard over the tin. "It's a good coat, Maureen. It's the wrong ship." If it holds nothing, the player must hurt a man of fifty-eight or give way.

The fight is a gangway one man wide, the foreman and four. Whoever stands with Tom takes the second man. The foreman goes into the dock and climbs out by the ladder, soaked. The gang on the timber do not move until one of her men swings at the bosun, and then they all get up.

Tom walks to the mast and takes the paper down. It is taped square, with his own tape. He folds it in four.

The Widow puts the lid on the tin. She says her last line (section 5) and carries the tin home herself.

Ten o'clock. The radio in car two, door open on the quay: "Car two. It's the Russian's time."

OZOLS (at the head of the gangway): Tonight I drive. Permission to come aboard is granted, Mister Tom.

The bosun pipes him up. It is the same call as the joke in week three, and nobody laughs.

On the bridge:

OZOLS: Time?

The player raises Tom's watch.

OZOLS: Ship's clock to Hook time. Until the pierhead only.

On the quay Ron lets go the stern line. She moves: three hundred yards to the lock.

OZOLS: Fare.

He holds out his hand. Tom puts the folded paper in it. The master enters it in the log, turns back the pages and reads four lines aloud, chosen from what the player did (section 9).

The lock steps. Tom goes ashore.

TOM (fixed): Ten o'clock.

OZOLS: For you. For us it is already tomorrow.

## 9. The endings

Three. None is picked from a list. The player can read which is coming from signs he is shown: the count of men who stand up at the fence, the coat on its hook in the Widow's hut, Ellis asking and then telling, how long the mate keeps him waiting.

Under Way. It needs four things from the record. By Friday dusk enough pine has gone up the north road to fill eight pay packets and the fuel tanks, which takes a gang of ten or more for four days, and who came is rows five, six and thirteen of the table. The crew have their books back. The fare book stood, so that Ellis is not on the quay. And Tom holds the gangway, with Ron or in spite of him. A thin record cannot reach it by refusing the tin.

The four lines the master reads are chosen by rule from the player's own acts: the first fare, the largest thing given, the worst thing charged or done, and the night of the fall. For one player: "Day 164. Master carried to nowhere. Charged: two tins. Day 173. Motorman Kalns carried to see a house with slippers. Given. Day 184. A docker's arm, for my perhaps. Day 195. He came for my motorman in four minutes and spelled his name." Then his last entry, said as he writes it: "Day 210. Arrest lifted by hand of T. Nowak. Fare paid. Wind south-west, four. Visibility good." It is the only time he says good with nothing after it. By the Hook's clock she sails on the thirtieth of November; by her own, on the first of December, the day the notice names.

The last image. From the lock wall: the Lodger going out past the pierhead light with every lamp lit, her bell ringing if the player brought it back, and one box under the forecastle. Before the gangway is raised Zina comes down it once and puts the last jar of jam in Ron's coat pocket without a word. Ron stands beside Tom with the wet of the stern line on his hands. "Rain by four." On Sunday at ten Pauline starts to call the Russian's time and stops. Tom takes car two to berth four all the same, and the player may drive the hour alone. In the morning Sheila turns the book round, takes the pen from him and writes the line herself: "22.00. Car two. Berth four to nowhere and back. None. No charge."

Settled: eight people paid; Juris goes home in his ship; the paper is down; Mickey's red line is ruled through, unpaid. Unmended: the Hook has no ship and it was Tom who sent her; the Widow keeps the gate and never again picks a man who worked the Lodger; an arm, if he broke one; the seventh line; and Ron, who has nothing to mind. On Monday the fence goes up, and Cammack has the contract for it.

The Tow. The record fell short: too few hands and the timber still in her, or no books, or the gangway given up to Ron in the coat, or Ellis's charge read on the quay. And Tom did not take the tin. On Saturday morning the crew are flown home as distressed seamen, paid as far as the pine that went would stretch. At noon the tug takes her out stern first, dark, with the rest of the cargo in her. Juris is buried in the Hook on the Tuesday, and the name on the stone is right, because Tom spelt it.

The last image. The ferry landing, where Tom arrived. The master is last off, with one suitcase, and Tom carries it down for him. "You drive very badly, Mister Tom. Like your uncle. I shall miss it." He gives Tom the pocket log of the voyages. Behind them the tug has her halfway to the pierhead.

Settled: the crew are home. Unmended: the ship, the master's command, a Latvian in the wrong ground, and a berth that is the Widow's by Monday.

The Lodger Stays. This is the road of the player who took the Widow's side at the sit-down and stayed there: no books fetched, no gang asked, her cases in the hold. For him the tin is no temptation; it is partners settling up. A player who did the work and still takes the tin, in front of the gang he asked, gets this ending too, and the game asks him twice. He has £4,100, the busiest office on Quay Street, and the name. At midnight her men go aboard. In December the Board removes the crew from its berth and they are sent home unpaid; the master, last off, is deported, and his log goes into a police drawer as evidence of the brass.

The last image. Sunday, ten o'clock. Pauline calls the Russian's time, from habit, and nobody laughs. Tom drives to berth four. The ship is there, dark, one of the Widow's lamps on the gangway, and under it Ron, in the coat. "Not without her say-so, Mr Nowak." Ron has never called him that before. On the shelf in the office the biscuit tin stands beside the sprats.

Settled: Mickey's debt, in full. Unmended: everything else.

Six of them, told.

The Volna (6). Under a tarpaulin on number one hatch sit two Soviet saloons, consigned to a dealer who went under with Hallam and Pask. Before July 1989 nobody but a registered docker could have driven them ashore; now anybody may, and Ron will not watch it done. Tom drives one down the ramp through a pen of men who say nothing, and the customs officer charges duty on it in the voice of a man who has waited since May. It will start only for the Chief, who talks to it. It leaves car three on the rank: a brown saloon with a roof sign and a heater stuck on full, and fares who ask for it by name.

The customs seal (7). The crew's own tobacco, four thousand cigarettes, sits in a locker under a customs seal that may not be broken in port, and they have looked at that seal for six months. Tom can lift it whole with a key cut by Ian Megginson, or pay the duty across the counter in two sizes of five pence. Either way the locker is empty: the crew smoked it by August, through the back panel. The officer has sealed an empty locker every Friday since: "I seal what I am shown, Mr Nowak." It leaves a lead seal on the office key board, and an officer who is not at the gate on the last night.

Ada's bell (8). Ada has put her pot on at nine since May, when the Lodger's bell rings noon aboard, and when the bell goes her morning goes with it. Tom carries her Mickey's spare clock, she sends it back because it ticks, and over tea she says she always knew it was dinner time somewhere, and liked it. Then, as a kindness: "He goes out heavy and comes home light, your captain. I thought you knew, you were driving." A player who calls on her early has the answer a week before the gate rings. It leaves one window on Quay Street, never lit in seven weeks, that is lit on the last night.

The letters (9). One seaman writes home every Sunday and has had no stamps since June, so the letters lie in a biscuit box. Tom buys stamps with a tin of mixed fivepences and posts forty at once, a handful at a time, into a pillar box that will barely take them. Three weeks later one comes back: the street has been given another name since he sailed. He reads the envelope for a long time and asks Tom only how the new name is said. It leaves an unopened letter in the office drawer, which Sheila will not have thrown away.

Ron's hook (10). The hook in the pawn's back room is Ron's. When Tom lays it on the counter, Ron says he pawned it himself, the afternoon the Act went through, for the price of a fish supper, and ate the supper. He does not want it, and it hangs on a nail in the office while Ron walks round it for five weeks. On the first morning of the last discharge the nail is empty. It is one of the things Ron saw Tom do, and at the gangway it counts.

The ship's cat (11). In week three the Lodger's cat walks down the gangway and settles under the office counter. An animal landed from a foreign ship is a grave matter with the port health man, and getting her back aboard takes a chase over every wall in Mickey's yard. Tom meets the customs officer in the lane with the cat inside his coat, and the officer says, "I have not seen a cat." She will not stay aboard: on the last night she is asleep on the fare book when the ship goes, and Sheila writes round her.

## 11. Real and invented

Real, with its place in RESEARCH.md.

- The Scheme reserving dock work from 1947, its end on 3 July 1989 when the Act passed, the strike of 10 July to 1 August, and "over in three weeks": section 1, firm.
- Four in five dockers gone from a middling port by November 1990; the cheque of up to £35,000 until early 1991 and less after; eleven employers insolvent and 868 jobs lost: section 1, firm. Hallam and Pask is an invented firm placed among the eleven.
- Casual hiring back at once through labour supply firms: section 2, fair. The Widow's firm is invented on it.
- What had been a crime becoming a job, and managers driving cars off a Russian ship: section 2, fair. The Volna story stands on it.
- Dock land bought by the hectare: section 2, fair.
- The pen, the call, strags, a man's book, nicknames with a reason (Cinderella), the docker's hook and falls down holds: section 3, marked thin. They are used as names, objects and the men's own words. No line of dialogue states a thin item as a fact of history, and each needs a second source before it is recorded.
- The fare book as a legal record written before the journey and open to a constable: section 4, firm.
- The poll tax and its summonses; two sizes of five pence all autumn; the ambulance dispute ended in February 1990, so ambulances run in this story's weeks: section 4, firm.

Invented, and to be treated as invented.

- The holding of the ship. The arrest of a ship for a debt is real admiralty practice in general, but nothing in the research covers it. The paper on the mast, who may take it down, that a held ship keeps a berth open, that the keeping of a berth can pass to a hirer, and every other detail of it are invented and kept plain.
- The Onega, her nine people and their home towns, the Line that does not answer, her cargo of sawn pine counted in standards, the two saloons on her hatch and their make.
- A Soviet ship keeping Moscow time, the gap of three hours, and the clocks going back on Sunday 28 October. The last is a calendar fact that is not in the research and must be checked.
- The wording of sea forecasts; seamen's books and a hirer holding them; a crew without papers not ringing for an ambulance; distressed seamen flown home; that a coffin would not be flown.
- The Harbour Board's notice and its date of 1 December; the night tide at ten past ten on 30 November; the infirmary on the dock road; a cab office heard on a ship's receiver.
- Cammack's scrap contract and the cranes; the Widow's fuel tap and her cigarettes; customs seals on a crew's tobacco; duty on a landed car; the rule on a ship's cat.
- Every price and count: the £4,100, sprats at 31p, sixty men at the fence and twenty picked. The dates of Bonfire Night and Remembrance Sunday follow the game's own calendar, not the research.
- What any man did with his cheque. The research looked twice and found nothing, so the cheque man's fare to the station is invention.

## 12. Canon questions

1. The ship. A foreign ship held at the Old Basin for a debt owed to Mickey; her master and eight others as new people, one of them a woman, the cook; her deck, bridge, galley and hold as places on the docks map. (a) Allow [recommended]. (b) The ship without boarding: the gangway foot becomes the stage, scenes 4 and 5 keep their shape, and Sheila's audit moves to the office. (c) No: this bible falls.

2. The Widow. Canon makes her head of the dockside syndicate. Here she also runs the lawful hiring at the gate, has fed the ship since May and kept her with Mickey. (a) Allow [recommended]. (b) The gate and the bread, but no arrangement with Mickey: she moved in when he died, and section 5 loses its best line. (c) Another head as opponent: the other two cannot feed a ship, and sections 5 and 7 would need rebuilding.

3. Mickey. He drove the ship's brass ashore knowingly and took the Widow's tins. Canon says only that he left a half-dead criminal outfit. (a) Allow [recommended]. (b) Mickey never knew: the confession loses "Your uncle knew", and the master's silence becomes only shame.

4. A death. Juris Kalns, 22, dies of a fall aboard in week five. Nobody kills him. (a) Allow [recommended]. (b) He lives, badly hurt, and is flown home in week six: the box, and the reason she must sail and not be emptied by aeroplane, are lost, and the last act weighs less. (c) Nobody is hurt: the lead's change has nothing to force it.

5. Ron. He becomes the ship's watchman, can end in the Widow's coat, and on the worst record can be fought by the player; he is 58, two years under Tom's own line. (a) Allow all three [recommended]. (b) Ron can never be struck: on that record the player can only give way. (c) Ron never takes the coat: the breach is in words only, and scene 5 is one against five.

6. The Admiral. The Hook gives Tom a nickname beside the canon ladder of names. (a) Allow, as a dockers' joke outside the ladder [recommended]. (b) Only Cammack uses it. (c) Cut it.

7. Small additions: the lock and pierhead of the Old Basin and an infirmary on the dock road as places; a third car for the firm off the ship; a ship's cat in the office; the Old Basin fenced after 1 December. (a) Allow [recommended]. (b) Rule on each as it is built.
