# BIBLE-X4: The Burial Club

## 0. Head note

The old dockers of the Hook are dying faster now there is nothing to get up for, and Tom Nowak meets it on his first afternoon: a widow at the counter of the cab office he has inherited, with a card stamped paid every week since 1953. Mickey's book of uncollectable debts is the Quay Street burial club: two hundred and twelve men, each promised a hearse, two cars, six bearers and a stone. The tin holds forty-one pounds. Tom wants to find who emptied it, pay the men back and shut the book. Instead he buries one of them a week, beside Ron Kirby, who has carried sixty coffins and teaches him the step, and under the eye of the Man in Black, who marks every funeral out of ten, aloud. Nobody emptied the tin. There was never a fund, only Mickey and his night work. In the fifth week, with three to bury, Ron dies of what Tom asked of him. The Hook turns out for Ron, or does not, by how Tom buried the others. Then Danny Cammack, whose men put Ron on a step, holds out his own father's card, paid up.

What changed from the one-paragraph premise, and why.

The wrong blow is not ordered. The premise sent Tom into the presbytery to shame the priest. Here the initials B.W. fairly fit three people. The player picks one, picks none, or works the answer out, and every road is written (lesson 3).

Nothing is priced after the first page. A funeral is a hearse, two cars, six bearers and a stone, and each is got by a deed, a favour or a man's back. The tin is counted once (lesson 2).

Ron is not sent to his death by a line of script. One Thursday goes four ways, each from something the player asked of him, and Ron has already chosen, against a doctor, to go on carrying.

The opponent moved inside. Danny Cammack once drove car two for Mickey, owns the Hook's only hearse from week two, and his father is the one member who never missed a week.

Tom got a body: three inches too tall for a coffin, dressed by widows, a cab sign left lit at a graveside.

The ending kept its image and gained a second coffin. After the Hook turns out for Ron, or does not, Cammack's father comes up the same hill at noon with a paid-up card and nobody to carry him.

## 1. What is ending

The Hook's reason to exist ended on 3 July 1989, when the Dock Work Act abolished the Scheme that had kept dock work for registered men since 1947. The strike began on 10 July and was called off on 1 August. By November 1990 a middling old port had lost four in five of its dockers, each with a cheque of up to £35,000, a rate that closes early in 1991. Eleven stevedoring firms went under. Casual hiring, the thing the Scheme was built to end, came straight back to the gate. The game opens on Monday 15 October 1990, between the Act and the closing date.

The player reads none of that. He sees it in three counts that fall every week, each in a place where his hands work.

The Friday queue. Friday is stamp day, when members stand at the counter with their cards. In week one there are thirty-one men, out of the door and past the rank. In week three, nineteen. In week five, eleven. Some have died. More have signed, cashed the cheque and gone to a sister inland, and their cards come back by post with no letter in the envelope. Sheila files those behind a divider marked Gone Away.

The bearers. A coffin wants six men of a height. In week one Ron finds five in an afternoon and Tom is the sixth by accident. In week three the player stands at the dock gate at six in the morning, where men who held books for thirty years wait in a pen to be picked for a day's work by a labour firm's foreman, and looks for bearers among those left over. By week five there are three funerals and eight men on the whole Hook who can still lift.

The turnout. The Man in Black says how many walked behind before he says the mark. Forty behind Lord Ash. Twenty-six behind Cinderella. Eleven behind the Broken Boomerang.

Week by week it runs so.

Week one, 15 to 21 October. The dock still looks like a dock. One ship lies in the Old Basin, the queue reaches the rank, and a funeral finds its men by teatime.

Week two, 22 to 28 October. The undertaker sells up after forty years and the black hearse goes into a paint shed. The first card comes back in the post.

Week three, 29 October to 4 November. The pen. The ship in the basin is worked by casuals bussed in from away and by managers in clean gloves, doing lawfully what was an offence before July 1989. A funeral is carried by men nobody picked for work.

Week four, 5 to 11 November. A man who left in 1989 comes home by ferry in a box. On Remembrance Sunday the dockers stand at the chapel in one row where there used to be four.

Week five, 12 to 18 November. The first cold week. Three members die in three nights and there is one hearse, owned by a man who never stood in a pen.

Week six, 19 to 25 November. Nobody is on the office door for the first time since 1989.

Week seven, 26 November to 2 December. The last ship of the year sails without a registered man having touched her. There are now more of the Hook's dockers on the hill than on Quay Street.

Under the three counts sits a fourth that the player can hold in his hands: the long wooden box of cards on the counter. It starts with two hundred and twelve. It thins from the front (Buried), from the middle (Gone Away) and, from week four, from wherever Danny Cammack has been knocking on doors.

## 2. What is owed

The one question is the game's own: what does Tom owe the dead man and the living? Here it has a number. He owes two hundred and twelve funerals, promised by somebody else, to men he has never met. Sheila says why the book is called uncollectable in week one, without looking up: "A man has to die to collect, Mr Nowak. Then it's his widow at your counter."

Every system the player handles keeps part of the account.

Fares. A club widow rides free in a Mickey's cab for the rest of her life. Tom learns it by charging one. From then on the fares he drives for nothing are the ones that talk: Thursday runs up the hill with a woman and a trowel in the back. Paying fares are few and chosen, and what they pay is what keeps the telephone on.

Debts. The debt book is turned round. In every other game the lead collects; here the lead is collected from, across his own counter, by people in black with a card. The five loose pages at the front are small real debts owed to Mickey, and how Tom collects those in week one is the Hook's first reading of him.

Rent. Rent falls on Friday, the same day as the stamps, so every week the player stands at one counter paying a landlord with one hand and stamping cards with the other. If the office goes, the telephone goes, and the telephone is how the infirmary says who has died.

Favours. Bearers cannot be bought. A man carries for Tom because Tom carried for his, drove his mother, or asked his name at the pen. The notebook keeps a page headed Would Carry: each name, and the one thing that stands between that man and Tom.

Witnesses. The town watches a funeral exactly as it watches a break-in, and remembers it as long. Ada counts the cars from her window. The mason knows his own stone when he sees it on a grave. The Man in Black is the witness who says it aloud.

The fare book. By law every booking is written before the journey and a constable may ask to read it. Tom writes each funeral in as a journey, with the dead man's nickname as the passenger. The night jobs that pay for funerals sit in the same book as gaps, or as lines that are not so. DS Ellis can read a calendar.

The stamp. PAID, in purple, with a wooden handle worn to the shape of Mickey's palm. The player presses it himself every Friday from week one. It is the smallest verb in the game and the turn rests on it.

The marks. Each funeral ends in a number out of ten that is built from what the player did: the cars, the step, the stone, whether he was there. It is the only score in the game and it is a man's voice.

The ending settles four accounts. Ron's card, stamped Buried by Sheila's hand. The card of Cammack's father, the best-paid card in the box, held out by the man Tom has most cause to refuse. The box itself, kept or handed back door by door. And a card that does not exist yet, with Tom's own name on it. What stays owing is said plainly in each ending, and something always does.

## 3. The lead

What Tom wants in week one. Tom Nowak steps off the ferry wanting one plain thing: to owe nobody. He takes the office for a business. When a widow puts a card on his counter his first fixed line is "I'm not him." When he understands that the book is a promise, he wants the decent stranger's way out: find whoever emptied the fund, hand every man his money back, shut the book, run a cab office. The player shares the want, because it is the sensible one and because a tin with forty-one pounds in it looks like a theft.

What forces it to change, and when. Friday 9 November, week four, at the stamp. There was never a fund, and a promise cannot be refunded. There is nothing to hand back. From that night there are two things he can do: keep it with his own back, or break it to faces he now knows. The want that grows in its place is small and exact. He has seen one thing done properly since he landed, a man carried level by six who knew him, and he wants to be able to do that.

What it costs if he does not. A player may refuse the change and go on treating funerals as a bill: fewer cars, hired bearers, let Cammack see to it. The cost reaches the hands before anybody says it. The Man in Black's marks shrink to bare numbers. The Would Carry page empties. Ron moves him to the back of the coffin. In week five Ron walks to the parlour by himself.

Five deeds only he would do.

He charges a widow her fare on his first afternoon, to the penny, because nobody has told him. Next morning he carries the same coins back to her door, the two sizes of five pence she counted out, and not a penny added.

He writes the dead into the fare book. The law says every journey, so: "Lord Ash. Chapel to the hill. One way. No charge." Sheila says a funeral is not a fare. He writes it anyway, every week, and by week four she leaves the line for him.

He has himself altered. Three inches too tall for the old dockers, he has Ada sew a pad into the left shoulder of a dead man's overcoat, and later the right, so that a coffin sits level on him at either side.

He unbolts the roof sign with a spanner in the yard before every funeral and bolts it back after, having driven his first one with MICKEY'S lit up over a widow's head.

At the pen he will not tap a shoulder. He goes down the rail asking each man his name, writes it in the notebook, and asks him to carry, with the foreman looking on.

The fixed lines. Tom's talk is the player's, so his authored lines are few, flat and short enough to sit beside anything typed. Week one, to the widow: "I'm not him." Week one, at the grave, asked what he would give it: "I didn't know him." Week three, the opening of any accusation, offered and never pressed on the player: "Where is it?" Week four, at the stamp: "Then who paid?" Week five, to DS Ellis, who asks for Ron's next of kin: "The firm." Week seven, to Cammack at the counter: "Leave the card." Week seven, at the gate: "He's paid up." And the fare book, which is his voice in ink, first line to last.

What is funny about him. He is a stranger at the one occasion where everybody else knows when to stand. He is half a second behind the chapel all game ("He gets up like a man who's seen his bus," says the Man in Black). He is too tall, so the first coffin he touches goes up Quay Street like a wardrobe on a staircase. He owns no black, so the Hook dresses him: a tie from Ada, an overcoat from Lady Ash that stops short of his wrists, gloves from Cinderella's widow, the London Fog's shoes, a size small. "The second car's driver," the Man in Black tells the hill, "has been dressed by committee." He answers polite questions exactly. He is corrected by Sheila in four words or fewer. Being laughed at is how he is let in, and the game never lets him be laughed at twice for the same thing: each joke is a fault he then mends with his hands.

## 4. The people who matter

Twelve living people are named in the main line: four carried through every week (Ron, Sheila, the Man in Black, Danny Cammack) and eight light. The dead are known by nickname and their widows by the dead man's name. Cammack is in section 5.

Ron Kirby, 58, the door and the rank, a docker until 1989.

What he wants for himself. His own corner covered. He has carried sixty coffins and can count the men left who would carry his properly, and he would sooner go under one than say so. So he is making a bearer out of the only material to hand. He also wants to go on getting up. Since July 1989 the dead have been the only work that needed him, and a doctor on June's ward has told him to sit down.

One deed only he would do. He talks to the coffin. Front left, the head on his shoulder, he tells the dead man quietly where they have got to, by what each building used to be: "Rope walk, Albert. That was Stannard's. Kerb coming." He keeps every order of service in a biscuit tin, in order, sixty of them.

How he talks. "Boss." Short, northern: owt, nowt, summat. He never finishes a compliment.

"You're tall. It can't be helped."

"Left foot. Not that one. The other left."

"We've never said no to a card. Not in the strike. Not that winter the ambulances stopped."

"I'm not teaching you for them."

How he changes, or refuses to. He refuses three things: to pick a man out of a pen, to tell what B.W. means before Sheila lets him, and to sit down. He changes once. In week four, in the cab, he asks for something. He does not live to change again.

The Man in Black, 74, a stevedore until 1981.

Nobody on the Hook knows his right name and the game never gives it. A foreman named him for always being off to a funeral, which he was: he began the club in 1953 and was its first collector, door to door on Fridays with a satchel.

What he wants for himself. That somebody who knows how it is done should still be standing when it is his turn. He has no card and will not take one: "A man doesn't mark his own." And he would like, once, to give a ten. He never has.

One deed only he would do. He walks behind every coffin on the Hook with his hat on. When the grave is filled and the last car has gone he says a number out of ten, aloud, to nobody, and the gravediggers carry it down the hill by teatime. Families dread it and frame it. He marks the funeral, never the man.

How he talks. The number first, then the reasons, like a tally clerk reading back.

"Five. I've seen pianos moved with more feeling."

"I don't mark the weather. I note it."

"Seven. Six bearers, two of them the same height, which is two more than last time."

"He was a miserable beggar and I'd not have crossed the road for him living. Eight. I'm not marking him."

"A ten's the one where I've nowt to say. You'll know it. You'll not hear it."

In the cab in week two he says where it began: in 1938 his father went up the hill on the council's handcart at seven in the morning, with him and the gravedigger behind. "I gave it nought. I've been marking since."

How he changes, or refuses to. He walks to every funeral and rides back in Tom's front seat, explaining the mark; that ride is where Tom learns the trade. If Tom shames him he goes on marking and stops explaining: the number, and he walks home. At the end he says nothing, which is his ten; or gives a seven in Ron's voice; or does not mark at all. In one ending he asks for a card.

Sheila Dunn, 53, at the counter since 1959.

What she wants for herself. Not to be the last person who knows what the stamp means, and to hand it to somebody she has watched bury three. She promised Mickey she would size up whoever inherited. Her test is the only one she trusts: what he does at the counter when a card looks short.

One deed only she would do. She has never been to a club funeral. During each she sits with the dead man's card in front of her and minds the telephone, in case there is another. When the cars come back she stamps it Buried.

How she talks. Four words where four will do. She has never been heard to say a thing that was not so; when she will not answer, she says that.

"New management."

"Stamp it."

"It means what it says."

"A funeral's not a fare."

"It never added up, Mr Nowak. It wasn't for adding."

How she changes, or refuses to. She locks the office in daylight once in thirty-one years, for Ron. She says Tom's first name once, when she hands him Ron's card. On the worst road she does neither, and the stamp goes into her handbag.

The light ones.

Father Brendan Walsh says the Mass, keeps the tin overnight on Fridays because the office has no safe, and counts the cars at every funeral. He does it so that he can tell the widow how many came: "She's in the first one. She can't."

June Suddaby, night nurse, is the voice on the office telephone at three in the morning. She pays her dawn fare to the penny, the only Suddaby who ever paid the firm. "He was very good at funerals. It was the week before that he wasn't up to."

Maureen Jensen, the Widow, is the one widow on the Hook who has never come to the counter. She gives the night work that the dead cost, keeps her word to the hour, and stands at the back of every club funeral. Her husband's, in 1979, is the only nine.

Basil Wardle, 66, undertaker, has buried the Hook at cost for forty years and has not been paid since July 1989. He sells to Cammack and goes to a bungalow in Gullwing with his plane and his screwdrivers.

Ada, at her window, counts the cars out of Quay Street, lends a black tie and sews the pad.

DS Carol Ellis reads the fare book standing. She comes first about a stone and then about a body.

The Blister, Danny's father, sits at the window over the parlour with an oxygen cylinder. For forty years he turned up when the work was done, and for thirty-seven he paid his stamp every week in his own money.

Darren Milner, 25, carries for a fee, an inch too short; sells orders of service with the name spelt wrong; and is the one who runs up the hill.

## 5. The opponent

Danny Cammack, 30, head of the new crew. His money comes from a trade nobody names, run from lorry parks inland; none of it is ever seen.

Why he is inside Tom's circle. At eighteen he drove car two for Mickey, for two years, and was let go for carrying fares that never went in the book. He still knows where the kettle is and calls Sheila "Sheil", which nobody else living does. His father is a member. From week two he owns the only hearse on the Hook, so he is at the head of every funeral Tom arranges, and more weeks than not he is at Tom's counter or in Tom's cab.

What he wants for himself. A turnout. His father, the Blister, turned up when the work was done for forty years, and the Hook has not forgiven it. Danny knows that when the old man goes nobody will walk behind him. He believes a turnout can be organised like anything else, so he is buying the dead: the hearse, then the bearers, then the cards, until two hundred men are obliged to the name Cammack. He does not want the office. He wants what the Man in Black counts.

One deed only he would do. He rehearses his father's funeral. In week four the player sees it from the cab: the silver hearse, four men in grey coats carrying an empty coffin out of the parlour and round the block at a walk, Danny timing them on his watch, and the Blister at the upstairs window with the tube under his nose, saying through the glass, "Too fast."

How he talks. Quick, friendly, selling. "Pal." He calls the dead "customers" once, and hears himself.

"I'm not the bad lad here, pal. I'm the only one on this Hook spending money on the dead."

"You've the men and no motor. I've the motor and no men. That's a partnership, that's all that is."

"Silver. It's lighter. People want lighter now."

"He turned up, didn't he? Late's still turned up."

How he gains ground where the player sees it.

Week one. A man in a pale raincoat at the back of Lord Ash's funeral counts the turnout on his fingers. "Blister's lad," says Ron, and spits tidily.

Week two. Wardle's name comes down off the fascia. The hearse stands in the paint shed, half silver. Danny offers it free for the club, himself at the wheel, his firm's name on the order of service: Cammack and Father.

Week three. Four of the Hook's regular bearers turn out in matching grey coats, on his wage. Two sets of bearers arrive for one coffin.

Week four. The rehearsal. Cards start to go from the box: he is on doorsteps, swapping Mickey's card for a plan with a silver edge. He comes to the counter with Wardle's unpaid book under his arm.

Week five. He will not send the hearse unless he is given the box. His men put Ron on the parlour step.

Week six. He comes to the counter and offers to bury Ron: silver, six cars, no bill. He means it kindly, which is the worst of it.

Whatever the player does, the hearse, the coats and the parlour are Cammack's by week four. What the record decides is how much else. Every corner Tom cuts is one that Danny supplies, and a funeral Danny supplied is a household that goes over to him.

The meeting in person. There are three, in the last week. On Monday at the counter the opening is played again with the other man standing in it: someone bereaved, holding a card. "He's paid up. I checked. Every week since fifty-three, and never a black one. You owe him six men." On Thursday at dawn Danny books car two, parlour to the hill and wait, to see who comes for Ron. He sits in the back, and for the first time he has nothing to sell. At noon, at the cemetery gate, the silver hearse stops in front of the whole Hook with its back turned, four hired men who never knew his father standing ready in step, and Danny on the gravel in front of Tom with nothing left to offer.

What Tom can do there is set by the record (section 9). Danny is not arrested by the script, not beaten by it and not forgiven. If Tom takes the coffin, Danny walks behind his father through two hundred men who lift their hats to the box and not to him, and he can tell the difference. That is his settlement.

## 6. The seven weeks

One funeral a week is the spine, and no two ask the same thing of the hands. Driving runs through all of it: the night fetch from the infirmary, the cortege and its minute at the dock gate, the chosen fares between, and the night job that pays for the stone. The main line holds about 720 minutes.

### Week one, 15 to 21 October: the inheritance (about 120 minutes)

Main beats. Monday: the ferry, the counter, Lady Ash and her card, the first fare, and Sheila's walk-round, which ends at the book, the box of cards and the tin. After dark Ron brings the outfit's first ask, an envelope for the ferry landing, past the constable. Asked what it is for: "A wet day." Tuesday: the widow's coins go back. The five loose debts are collected on the street, and one comes to blows on the quay. Wednesday: Ron finds five bearers in an afternoon; the sixth has signed and gone. Thursday: Lord Ash is buried, with Tom on the back right corner (scene 1). Friday: stamp day, thirty-one men, and one with nothing in his hand. Sheila: "Stamp it." Sunday: Sheila comes in on her day off. "So which is it going to be?" Close it, keep it, or say nothing.

The hands. A fare by landmarks; a carry through watched ground; a collection and a fight; a coffin; a rubber stamp.

Fares and the car. Lady Ash, to the priest's house: "Thirty-seven years. Not in the strike, not when he was laid off. I'd say, where's it coming from, Albert, and he'd say, it's stamped, isn't it." Father Walsh, down from the hill: "Two cars, and one with its light on. I'll tell her two." The Man in Black, who gets into the front seat unasked: "I walk there. I ride back. Mickey knew."

Funny: the tilt. Grave: Friday evening, when Sheila tips the tin out and counts it in front of him, once, both sizes of five pence, beside two hundred and twelve cards.

Choices. How the coins go back: exact, or with a pound added ("I've a card. I'm not a case"), the first household's reading of him. How the loose debts are collected: squeezing is told on the street by Thursday. Whether he goes to the landing: the tin gets its cut or does not, and Ron notes it. Sunday's answer sets how soon Sheila lets him near the old books; on all three the club stays open until he refuses a widow to her face.

### Week two, 22 to 28 October: half a hearse (about 105 minutes)

Main beats. Monday, three in the morning: June's first call. Cinderella has gone on her ward. Tom and Ron fetch him from the mortuary door in Wardle's van. Tuesday: Wardle's name is coming down, the hearse is in the paint shed with one side silver, and Danny is at the counter with his offer. That night, the yard lesson: six men with the office sideboard on their shoulders, Ron calling the step, Ada at her window ("Are you burying Mickey's sideboard?"). Ron moves Tom to front right, beside him. Ada sews the pad. Thursday: Cinderella leaves the chapel at a quarter to twelve. "Off just before twelve," says the Man in Black. "Last time." Friday: twenty-six in the queue. Tom stamps a second card with no money under it and sees Sheila write two letters in the other book. Saturday: the first card comes back by post.

The hands. A night drive with a body; the carry practised under a sideboard; then the hearse.

The hearse is the week's choice. Take Danny's terms: silver, Danny driving, his name printed, and the household becomes his first. Or take it from the shed by night as it stands, black on the kerb side and wet silver on the other, and drive the cortege so that the mourners see only black: a route puzzle at walking pace, with the crowd on one pavement and the turns all the wrong way. Or do without, and shoulder Cinderella three-quarters of a mile uphill, the old way: the best mark, and afterwards Ron sits on the wall longer than he did last week.

Fares and the car. June at dawn, paying. Cinderella's widow: "Forty years, and I never once saw him at midnight." The Man in Black, on 1938.

Funny: the sideboard, and the hearse ("Seven. Half a hearse. I've marked the half I saw"). Grave: June on the infirmary kerb at six with her purse open, counting out her father's firm its fare.

Choices. The hearse. Whether Tom asks what the two letters are ("It means what it says"). A hearse taken by night is heard of by Ellis. A shouldered coffin is the first thing the Hook says in his favour.

### Week three, 29 October to 4 November: the pen (about 110 minutes)

Main beats. Monday: the London Fog dies, and his widow brings a card with no stamp on it since July 1989. Sheila says nothing and watches. Tuesday: four of the Hook's six regular bearers come out in Cammack's grey coats. Wednesday, six in the morning: the pen. Ron stops at the rail. "I stood in that. I'll not pick out of it. Not for this. Not for owt." Thursday: the Fog is buried by the club; or by the council, at nine, off the back of a van, with the Man in Black alone behind. On the chapel step two sets of bearers meet over one coffin. Thursday to Saturday: B.W. Everything that points anywhere is now on the table (scene 2). Friday: nineteen in the queue, and a message from June: the ward doctor would like a look at Ron Kirby. Sunday, in the cab, Tom asks Ron who B.W. is. "Ask me summat else." Ron gets out and walks, and for a day he does not say boss.

The hands. Working a crowd of men at a gate; a fight; one break-in of three; a search; the heaviest carry in the game ("The London Fog. Never lifted in his life, and here we are").

Fares and the car. The Fog's widow: "It was always stamped. I never saw to it. It was just always stamped." Wardle, with his boxes, to the Gullwing road, saying what forty-one funerals come to, unless Tom has already frightened him. The Man in Black; or, if he has been shamed, an empty seat.

Funny: the fight on the chapel step is conducted in whispers, hats on, because the widow is in the first car. Ten men dispute a coffin in murmurs and stand up straight each time she turns her head. Grave: the van at nine. Or, if the Fog was carried, Ron on the hill wall with a hand flat on his chest, saying it is the hill.

Choices. Bury the Fog or turn his widow away with the fund's slip. At the pen, tap shoulders or ask names. On the step: fight, give way, or split the coffin three and three. Whom to accuse, how publicly, or nobody. Whether June's message reaches Ron (it takes telling twice).

### Week four, 5 to 11 November: black weeks (about 110 minutes)

Main beats. Monday, Bonfire Night on the quay: the street has taken up whatever Tom said in week three and done its own shaming, and the person concerned is there, or pointedly is not. Tuesday dawn: the Broken Boomerang comes back. He left in 1989 with his cheque; his card came home by post; now a coffin comes off the ferry with a landlady from away, and Tom carries it past the Customs lamp. Wednesday: the rehearsal. Thursday: eleven walk behind the Boomerang. It is the low point: on most roads Tom is on a back corner and the mark is a bare number. Friday 9 November: sixteen in the queue and not one with money, because the ship in the basin was worked by casuals and managers. "Stamp it," sixteen times, and Sheila turns the other book round (scene 2). Danny arrives as she finishes, with Wardle's unpaid book. Saturday night: the first job Tom does knowing what it is for, a carry off the ship for Maureen Jensen against a stone for the Boomerang, with Ron beside him. Two in the morning: the dock road, the engine off (scene 3). Sunday 11 November: Remembrance at the chapel, one row where there were four.

The hands. A coffin through Customs; cartons of tobacco off a ship by night and a drive home with the lamps out, past the patrol car; or the mason's yard instead, and a blank stone lifted; the stamp, sixteen times.

Fares and the car. The landlady: "He talked about this place every night and never once bought a ticket." Maureen Jensen, in the back, on the night of the run: "I had a nine. Ask him what the other one was for." (Asked, the Man in Black says: "The hearse came early. You don't hurry a man.") Ron, at two.

Funny: the rehearsal; and the landlady counting the turnout. "Eleven? He said he'd hundreds of mates." "He had. They've gone and all." Grave: the turned book.

Choices. The stone: earned from the Widow or lifted from a man who will know it on the grave. Saturday's line in the fare book: a true gap or a fare that was never driven. Whether, and how, week three is put right, which can only be done by a deed: the tin walked back to the presbytery, Wardle's boxes carried, the cab left behind to walk beside the Man in Black.

### Week five, 12 to 18 November: three (about 115 minutes)

Main beats. The first frost. June rings three nights running, and the third time she says only, "It's June." Sell the Bed, who worked nights all his life. Swan Vesta, always on strike. Manhole, who fell down a hold in 1961. There are eight men on the Hook who can lift. Tuesday dusk: Sell the Bed is received into the chapel, and since somebody always sits with a club man on his last night, Ron does, with a flask of tea. "On nights to the last. I did a few with him." ## 7. Key scenes written out

### Scene 1. The counter, and the first carry (the opening; the scene players retell)

Monday 15 October, about four. Tom comes into the office with the suitcase in one hand and the letter in the other. A woman of seventy in a headscarf is at the counter ahead of him and does not turn round. She lays down a buff card, soft at the corners, covered on both sides in small purple squares.

Sheila, to the widow, not looking up: New management. This is him.

Lady Ash turns and looks him up and down, mostly up.

Lady Ash: Mickey promised a hearse and two cars and six to carry and a stone.

Tom: I'm not him.

Sheila: No. He's taller. Sit down, Nellie. (To the radio.) Priest's house. Car two.

Pauline: I've no driver.

Sheila looks at Tom until he puts the suitcase down.

He drives her by Pauline's landmarks. At the priest's house she opens her purse and counts the fare into his palm in two sizes of five pence, and he takes it. He has £23.50 and nobody has told him.

Thursday 18 October, outside the Tasker house. The black hearse, five men in dark suits, and Ron. They are one short.

Ron, looking at Tom's shoulder and then up: You're tall. It can't be helped. Back right. You go when I go.

The player holds the corner and steps on Ron's count; the stick keeps the box level, and Tom's shoulder rides high whatever the stick does.

Ron: Lift. Left foot. Not that one. The other left.

Down the step the coffin tips forward. Something inside shifts with a soft knock. Six men stop dead.

Ron, to the coffin, quietly: Sorry, Albert.

Lady Ash, from the door: He never could keep still.

They go on. Ron mutters the length of the street: Parade corner, Albert. That was Stannard's. Kerb.

Tom drives the second car with the widow's sister in the back and the roof sign lit; nobody tells him. At the dock gate the cortege stops for one minute with its engines running. The gateman takes his cap off. In the pen forty men turn round.

On the hill, after the last car has gone, an old man in a black overcoat and hat, who walked behind the whole way, speaks to the air in a voice made for a ship's hold.

The Man in Black: Forty walked. Five.

He turns to Tom, who is the only one left, because the second car is his.

The Man in Black: Six men. I'll grant that; it's getting to be a luxury. Black hearse, clean. The widow rode both ways. One off for the box going up the street like a wardrobe on a staircase. One for the back right corner getting up in chapel like a man who's seen his bus. And two for that. (He points at the roof of the cab. MICKEY'S, in orange.) You've advertised at a graveside. What would you give it?

The Man in Black: Nor did the hearse. I still mark it. (He opens the passenger door.) I walk there. I ride back. Mickey knew.

On the way down he says what a seven is, what the nine was, and why nobody has heard a ten. The player now knows how the game is scored, and by whom. That night Tom writes in the fare book, and the player reads it over his hand: "Lord Ash. Chapel to the hill. One way. No charge. Sign left on."

### Scene 2. B.W.: the false belief and its turn, on every road

What the player holds by the Thursday of week three. A book with thirty-seven years of stamps in, and a column of sums out, each initialled B.W., in three hands: an old copperplate, Mickey's, and Sheila's; since July 1989, nearly every week. A tin with forty-one pounds in it. Sheila: "It means what it says." Ron: "Ask me summat else."

The initials fairly fit three people. Father Brendan Walsh: they are his; the tin spends Friday nights at the presbytery; an envelope goes to the vestry at every funeral; the presbytery has a new roof since the January storm. Basil Wardle: they are his too; every funeral for forty years went through his parlour; he has just sold up for a bungalow by the sea. The Man in Black: the copperplate is his; he was the first collector and still goes round the housebound on Fridays with a satchel; he has no card of his own; and the Hook says he has had his dinner off the dead since the war.

The game sets no task. Nothing tells the player to accuse anybody. The things are simply there to be done.

The priest. On a Friday night the presbytery has a back window, a kitchen and a study, and on the desk the tin stands beside a notebook with CARS on the cover. Tom can take the notebook and go, and then all that follows is a priest who knows his window was forced. Or he can say it at the chapel door on Sunday as the congregation comes out: "Where is it?" The game asks twice. Father Walsh answers with one sentence about counting cars and goes in. By Bonfire Night the plate is a story on the Hook, some have stopped putting into it, and two old women cross the road when Tom comes.

The undertaker. In the parlour office by night is Wardle's own book, with a column headed M.S. owes. Read straight, it says the club owes him for forty-one funerals. Read by a man looking for a thief, it says forty-one fees. Tom cannot lay hands on a man of sixty-six. He can say "Where is it?" in the parlour's front room, before a family choosing a coffin. Wardle says nothing. That week he hands Cammack the rest: the van, the laying-out room, himself. From then on the Hook's dead are washed by lads from away. ("Who laid him out?" the Man in Black asks the hill. "I thought so.")

The Man in Black. The spur is a low mark. Tom can stop him on Quay Street on a Friday with the satchel on his shoulder. He hands it over himself. "Count it. Then count it again in front of Sheila Dunn." It holds the housebound men's five pences and a list. After that he marks and does not explain, and he walks home.

The player who works it out. The answer can be reached before any scene says it. The widow said it in the first fare. Sheila writes two letters after every stamp with no money under it. The copperplate begins in 1953 and the priest came in 1971. And five pence a week for thirty-seven years would not buy one hearse. Nothing stops Tom forcing the door of Mickey's inner office, which is his own: on the bottom shelf is the first book, and on its first page, "Black Weeks. No member's card to lapse for want of work." He has wronged nobody. Sheila sees the scratched lock on Monday. "You could have asked."

The player who holds back. He accuses nobody and forces nothing, and the road is whole: weeks three and four play as funerals, and he loses no scene. But the belief has an edge that needs no accusation. A man who thinks stamps are payments reads the London Fog's fifteen blank months as a lapsed member, and can fairly send the widow away with the fund's slip. If he did, the Fog went up at nine off a van. If he did not, he buried a man the book said he owed nothing, and could not have said why that was right.

The turn. Friday 9 November. Sixteen men. The first has nothing in his hand.

Sheila: Stamp it.

The second has nothing. By the fifth the player is stamping without being told. After the sixteenth Sheila turns the other book round on the counter and puts her finger under what she has written, sixteen times, in her small upright hand.

Tom: Then who paid?

Sheila: It stands for black weeks. A week a man's not picked, or he's out, or he's bad. His card's stamped the same. It always was. (She lifts down the first book and opens it at the copperplate.) Nobody took it, Mr Nowak. There was never a fund. There was your uncle. Then there was Ron. Now there's that.

She means the stamp, which is in his hand.

How it lands. After the priest: Father Walsh comes in on Saturday with the tin, as he always has, and a list of every burial fee he has waived since the strike, "so you'll know what the club owes me, which is nothing". He asks that the tin stay at the office from now on. After the undertaker: it is Danny who brings Wardle's book to the counter, because the debt is his. "Forty-one, pal. I'm not asking. I'm showing you." After the Man in Black: he is last in that queue with the satchel, and the hand that signs for the housebound is the hand in the first book. After the forced lock: "You knew last week. You stamped them anyway." After the Fog was turned away: Sheila takes his card from behind Gone Away and stamps fifteen months of squares, one at a time, while Tom watches. It is loud. "He was paid up. You weren't to know. I was waiting to see." After the Fog was buried: "You stamped a card you thought was short. That's all it ever was."

On no road is the truth a relief. Nothing is got back, because nothing was taken. What he was hunting was a kindness, and his own hand has been doing it since his first Friday.

### Scene 3. Two in the morning (the cab confession)

Week four, the small hours of Sunday 11 November. The cartons from the ship are in the boot. They have come down the dock road with the lamps out and passed the patrol car at a walk. By the cold stores Ron says, "Pull in, boss. Switch off." Rain on the roof. The engine ticks.

The player may type what he likes and Ron answers what he is asked. These lines come whatever is typed.

Ron: I went, you know. To that doctor of June's. (If her message never reached him: June Suddaby had a doctor look at me. She didn't ask. She stood there till I'd my shirt off.)

Ron: He says it's sitting down, or it's about six weeks. He'd a chart.

Ron: I've sat down, boss. I sat down in July of eighty-nine and I never got up again till a man died. Sixteen month. You get up when there's a coffin. There's nowt else on this Hook wants a man my size.

Ron: I'd a cheque. It went up that hill a man at a time. I've had worse out of thirty years.

If the player tells him to stop carrying:

Ron: You'll tell me, will you. Well, you're the boss. I'll mind the door Tuesday if you say.

He will mind it on Tuesday. He will not mind it on Thursday.

Ron: Sixty-odd I've carried. Front left, the lot. D'you know who's front left for me?

Rain.

Ron: Nor do I. That's it, do you see. It's not them I've been showing you for.

He looks at Tom's shoulder.

Ron: You're still too tall. Get Ada to do the other side.

Then the thing he has not done once in the game. He asks.

Ron: I'd take it kindly. If it was you. I'd not ask.

The answer is the game's own line: yes, no, or a line that keeps talking. Only a plain yes is taken. On yes Ron nods at the windscreen. "Right. That's that, then." On anything else: "No. Well. It's a lot to ask a man who's been here a month."

Ron: Drive on, boss. Them cartons'll not walk to Maureen Jensen's.

## 9. The endings

The record is read twice in the last week, and both readings are things the player watches. On Thursday at ten: which households come out for Ron. A household comes if its own funeral was marked seven or better, or if a poorer one was put right in person afterwards; with them come the pen, the Widow's gate and whoever was wronged in week three. On Friday at the counter: whether men bring their cards. Between the two stands the Blister's card. No screen offers an ending.

A card in his own name. The Hook came, Ron's six were men who knew him, and the Blister's card was honoured. At the hill gate, when the last car has gone, the Man in Black takes his hat off and says nothing at all. It is the first time anybody on that hill has heard him not speak, and the gravediggers carry it down by teatime. On Friday the queue forms: eleven men, and behind them some who went over to Cammack's plan and have brought Mickey's cards back. Sheila writes out a new card unasked, "Nowak, T.", and presses the first square herself. The last image is the counter. At the end of the queue stands an old man in a black overcoat with nothing in his hand. "Put down what they call me." Then he goes out and gets into the front seat of car two. Settled: Ron's card, the Blister's, a keeper for the club, a card for the man who never had one. Unmended: Ron. The dock. The queue will be shorter every Friday until Tom buries the last of them, and nobody stands behind Tom. Somebody on the hill still lies under wood.

Closed with the dock. The Hook came in part, with four or five bearers and one of them hired; or it came in full and Tom let the Blister go up through its backs. Either way the club ends, the first time for want of men and the second because a club that picks is not one. The Man in Black gives Ron a mark: "Seven. He'd have said six. He was hard on his own." Tom closes the club to faces. He walks the Hook with the box under his arm and hands each man his card, stamped up to that Friday, and the union fund's slip. Some doors shut on him. The last image is the empty box on the counter with its three dividers, and Tom on the hill at Ron's wooden marker, writing in the notebook: "One stone owing." Settled: every living member knows where he stands, and was told by the man himself. Unmended: a hundred and fifty men will be buried by whoever sells them a plan. Sheila gives a month's notice and leaves the stamp on the blotter.

The silver hearse. The Hook stayed indoors, because its own dead were done cheaply or by Cammack; or the box was given away; or there was a fight at the gate. Ron goes up behind silver, or on hired shoulders with one man at his corner who knew him. The Man in Black is there, since he is always there. When the last car has gone he puts his hat on. "I don't mark those." On Friday nobody queues. Sheila says "Mr Nowak" and the stamp goes into her handbag. The last image is Quay Street, some weeks on: a Cammack funeral leaving, silver, four grey coats in step. The second car is a Mickey's cab with its sign unbolted, and the player is driving it. On the seat beside him lies Ron's card. At the dock gate the cab stops for one minute, though nobody has paid for that. Settled: Ron is buried, the Blister is buried, the cards belong to somebody. Unmended: nearly everything. What is left is what Tom's hands learned, still being done where nobody marks it.

Where the rest stand on every road. June is on the ward and still pays her fare. Father Walsh still counts. Wardle is in Gullwing. Maureen Jensen keeps her nine. Ada's lamp is on. Ellis has a file she cannot close. Darren is an inch too short and has stopped charging. Danny has the hearse, and knows now what it does not come with.

Six of them, told.

The wrong body. In week two the mortuary porter hands Tom and Ron the wrong man, and they learn it in the parlour when the widow says, "That's not him. Mine had a moustache." Tom drives the van back through the night and makes the swap under the porter's eye before the day shift comes on. The turn is the spare man: no card, no family, and a ward number where a name should be. Left alone he goes to the council's van; if Tom buries him, the Man in Black walks behind and tells the hill, "One mourner and a minicab. Six, for nerve." It leaves a wooden marker reading Known to the Infirmary, and a blank card that Sheila files under Buried.

The dog. A whippet follows Lord Ash's cortege and sits through the committal, and Lady Ash asks Tom to fetch it home. The player chases it across the hill three times and loses. The turn comes from a neighbour: it was never Lord Ash's dog, nor anybody's; it has learned where the ham sandwiches are. "Better attendance than the Harbour Board," says the Man in Black. It leaves a dog at every funeral, which the Hook comes to count as a mourner, and from week four he includes it in the number that walked.

The Board's wreath. The Harbour Board sends a wreath to every docker's funeral, and afterwards a clerk in a mackintosh comes for it. Tom tails him to the Board's boiler room, where it hangs on a nail with a box of fresh ribbons under it. The turn is the player's: leave it, lift it, or wire it to the chairman's car where Quay Street can read the ribbon. Whichever he does, the clerk has seen him. It leaves the Board buying flowers again, and a clerk who nods to Tom in the street.

Ada's Thursdays. Every Thursday Ada rides up the hill with a trowel and back, no charge, and says more in the cab than anyone living. In week five, clearing leaves for her, the player reads the stone: beloved husband of Vera. Ada watches him read it. "I wasn't asked to the funeral. I wasn't anybody. I've been every Thursday since." She wants nothing altered, and nothing is. It leaves her lamp lit on nights the cab comes home late.

The hooter. The dock hooter used to sound a minute for a dead docker and has been silent since July 1989. A widow asks for it. During her husband's funeral the player forces the power-house door and holds the lever down. The harbour police arrive with the foreman who has the key to shut it off, and he stands in the doorway and takes his cap off. It leaves the hooter sounding for club funerals from then on, with nobody's leave, and a foreman who would like a card.

Cars off the ship. A manager in clean gloves cannot get export cars down a ship's ramp before the tide and offers cash to anyone who can drive. It is the dockers' own work, and since July 1989 anybody may lawfully do it. The player takes forty cars down a wet ramp at speed, which is the best driving in the game. The turn is Ron at the gate, watching and saying nothing, and one boot loaded for Maureen Jensen. It leaves the managers' picture in the paper, and in the pen a name for Tom that takes a funeral to wear off.

## 11. Real and invented

Real and firm, with its place in RESEARCH.md. The Scheme of 1947, ended by the Dock Work Act on 3 July 1989, and the strike of 10 July to 1 August (section 1). Four in five dockers gone from a middling port by November 1990, severance of up to £35,000 until early 1991, and eleven employers insolvent (section 1). The fare book: under section 56 of the 1976 Act every booking is entered before the journey and shown to a constable on demand (section 4). The ambulance dispute of 7 September 1989 to 23 February 1990 (section 4). The storm of 25 January 1990 (section 4). The poll tax from 1 April 1990 and its summonses (section 4). The small five pence from 27 June 1990, with the large one legal until 31 December (section 4).

Real, marked fair in RESEARCH (section 2). Casual hiring back at the gate through labour supply firms. Managers driving a cargo of cars off a Russian ship, which happened at Hull and is moved here to Meridian. Dock land bought by the hectare.

Thin (section 3), and used with that in mind. The nicknames and their reasons come from the Liverpool and Bristol lists: Lord Ash, Cinderella, the London Fog, the Broken Boomerang, Sell the Bed, Swan Vesta, Manhole, the Man in Black, the Blister, Splinter and Plank, Flags, Odd Ha'penny. The men who carry them here are invented. The pen, the strag, the tap on the shoulder and a man's book are spoken as the Hook's own words; they rest on summaries of oral histories and want a second source before a line is recorded. The £75 that a dockers' fund paid a widow comes from one London fund; here it is a printed slip and is never said as a general fact. That dockers had no sick pay stands behind "a week he's bad".

Inferred, not found: that Mickey's cabs carried the sick that winter (section 5, item 5).

Invented, all of it. The Quay Street burial club: its cards, stamps and five pence a week, its promise of a hearse, two cars, six bearers and a stone, black weeks and the letters B.W. The Man in Black's marking and the nine of 1979. The cortege's minute at the dock gate. Sitting up with a club man on his last night. The hooter sounded for a dead docker. The council funeral at nine off a van, and the handcart of 1938. The coroner keeping Ron a week (ordinary practice assumed, not researched). Stones blown flat by the January storm. Father Walsh's arrival in 1971 and his waived fees. The parlour, the mason's yard, the paint shed and the hill. What any docker did with his cheque (RESEARCH looked twice and found nothing). Bonfire Night and Remembrance Sunday come from the game's own calendar.

## 12. Canon questions

Each is the owner's to rule. The bible is written on the recommended answer.

1. What Mickey's outfit and debt book are. (a) The book of uncollectable debts is the burial club's book, two hundred and twelve funerals owed by the office; the outfit's night work exists to pay for them; the design's five small debts stay as loose pages at the front. Recommended. (b) The debt book stays money owed to Mickey and the club's book sits behind the locked door: week one keeps its collections as they are and the widow's card is first explained on Friday. (c) No club: this bible falls.

2. Ron Kirby dies in week five, on every road, by a fall in a fight with Cammack's men or by his heart. (a) Allow. Recommended: it is the loss the brief asks for, and no killing by Tom is needed anywhere. (b) Ron lives and never carries again: he is wheeled to the chapel door on the last Thursday, the turnout is counted at the Blister's funeral alone, and the bond's last scene is Ron watching Tom take front left. The ending loses its first coffin. (c) Ron leaves the Hook on the doctor's word: weaker again.

3. New people. The Man in Black; the Blister as Danny Cammack's father; Basil Wardle; Danny as a former driver of Mickey's who buys the funeral parlour. (a) Allow all. Recommended. (b) Allow, but Danny never drove for Mickey: he loses "Sheil" and the kettle and stands further outside. (c) The parlour stays Wardle's and Danny only lends: the opponent thins to a creditor.

4. New places on the Hook's map. The burial ground on the rise behind the chapel, the parlour on Quay Street's shop block, the mason's yard, the paint shed. (a) Allow. Recommended. (b) The hill lies beyond the map's edge: carries end at the chapel door, the marks are given there, and the long walks are lost.

5. Tom's build. He stands about three inches over Ron and the old dockers. (a) Allow. Recommended. (b) If the figure already built is no taller, Ron is the short one, the coffin tips the other way, and the pad goes into Ron's coat.

6. Sheila and his name. She says "Tom" once, with Ron's card, on every road but the worst, where she never says it. (a) Allow. Recommended; it keeps canon's rule that she withholds it until she trusts him. (b) She says it on every road: the third ending softens.
