# The Knowledge: story bible Z4

Story v3, phase 2, 23 November 2026 (claude-fable-5-2). Clock: 60 real seconds a game hour. Calendar: Monday 15 October to Sunday 2 December 1990. The seventh Friday is 30 November.

## 0. Head note

Ron Kirby's trade was abolished by Act of Parliament and he means to have another: at fifty-eight he will learn to drive. Tom Nowak, who has inherited the cab office Ron minds, cannot find a street in the Hook and will not ask. For seven weeks they are one driver between two men. Tom has the wheel. Ron has the town, named by what every building used to be and called a street late. Sheila Dunn books the examiner for the seventh Friday and prices everything Ron hits. Maureen Jensen, the Widow, who now picks the men at the dock gate, rides in Tom's front seat every morning and wants Ron for her foreman. When the office's jobs start being met, the player works out who is selling them, and may say so to Ron, to a driver, to Darren Milner, or to nobody. Nobody sold anything, and Ron took the Widow's coat to get Mickey's debtors a day's work. Then Tom takes cars off her quay on Ron's night, and Ron answers for it with his hands. He will not hold a wheel. On the seventh Friday the examiner waits, and Tom goes to fetch him.

What changed from the one-paragraph premise, and why.

The wrong blow is no longer written for the player. He builds the belief himself with a tail, a lifted parcel and a break-in; three people can fairly be chosen; he may choose none. The Ledger writes his belief down as he forms it and later strikes it through. The third reader lesson asked for this.

The leak has an answer the player can work out. Nobody sold the jobs. One of Mickey's radio sets has stood on the Widow's windowsill since the storm of January, and every job that was met is one where Tom had the way read to him over the air.

The big job is the player's own plan, with three ways off the quay, and it can go well. Ron's hands are lost on every plan, because the night was his to answer for under a rule stated in week one.

Tom is somebody: lost, proud, dry, laughed at all game, and driving on a dead man's badge number. His want is forced to change, and the cost of the old one is felt in every shut door.

The opponent sits beside him. The Widow has been Mickey's first fare every morning since 1971; she gains something visible each week and is faced twice in his own car.

Money is pushed down: the debts matter only as the list of men who are never picked.

The examiner still waits on the seventh Friday, but Ron goes to tell the man himself, and the appointment turns out to have been booked for two.

## 1. What is ending

The Dock Labour Scheme reserved dock work for registered men from 1947 and ended casual hiring in 1967. Parliament abolished it on 3 July 1989. The strike began on 10 July and was called off on 1 August. By November 1990 the old ports had lost between eight and nine in ten of their registered dockers (Hull 85%, Bristol 87%, Tees 93%), eleven employers had gone under and taken 868 jobs with them, and casual hiring, the thing the Scheme was made to end, had come straight back through labour supply firms. A man who goes before early 1991 gets up to £35,000; after that the top sum is £20,000. Development corporations are buying old dock land by the hectare. The game sits in the last autumn before the cheque shrinks.

The player reads none of this. He meets it as four things that get smaller every week, each of which he can count for himself.

The names. Mickey's letter tells Tom how to find the office: off the boat, left at the Mission, past the weighbridge, under the Board's clock, ask for Ron. The Mission has been a carpet warehouse since 1984, the weighbridge is a car park, and the clock is wrong. Ron's whole map is like that. Every place the player learns in this game he learns by what it was. By week seven he knows, street by street, a town that is not there, and the casual men bussed in to work the ships know none of it.

The pen. At six each morning the men who used to hold books stand in a railed yard inside the dock gate and wait to be picked. On the first Monday there are sixty for twenty places. Each Monday there are fewer, because men take the cheque or stop coming: fifty-six, fifty-two, forty-seven, forty-four, forty-one, thirty-eight. The player can count heads from the cab.

The crane. The last quay crane on the Old Basin is sold for scrap in week two and cut up where it stands. It is shorter each time the player drives the dock road, and by week six it is a square of rust on the quay. Ron names it every time they pass, in the present tense.

The work. Week one: a stevedore's nameboard comes down off a shed and the canteen stays shut. Week two: the Friday queue at the Harbour Board's counter, where men sign for the cheque. Week three: casuals arrive by minibus from away and ask Tom, of all people, the way. Week four: the Bonfire Night fire on the quay is built of dunnage and the rails of the old call stand. Week five: a Soviet ship lands cars at the far berth, and clerks and casuals drive them off, which until July of last year was a registered man's work and an offence for anybody else. Week six: surveyors with striped poles on the Old Basin, and the swing bridge chained. Week seven: the last morning at the gate.

Under these run the smaller true things of that autumn: two sizes of five pence in every till until 31 December; poll tax summonses on doormats; base rate down from 15% to 14% in October and nobody the better for it; telephone lines that in places have not been right since the storm of 25 January.

## 2. What is owed

The one question: what does Tom owe a man who is the only reason anybody opens a door to him, and what does he owe the dead man whose number he drives on?

Each system the player already handles keeps part of the account. None of them is a screen.

Doors. In weeks one to three a knock is answered because Ron is standing behind Tom. Alone, Tom is looked at through the glass. With Ron at his side a collection, a call or a yard opens; without him it has to be forced, which is loud and a crime. The Ledger's People page says it plainly under each name: opens to Ron, or opens to you. From the end of week five the account turns over. Ron cannot work a handle. He waits at every door, as a man at Tom's side always has, and the player opens it for him: every door, for two weeks.

The way. Pauline gives the way over the radio and says it again when asked. Ron gives it from the passenger seat, by what things were, a street late. Each has a price. The radio is heard on the Widow's windowsill. A man asked the way knows where Tom was going. Ron's way costs nothing and has to be learned like a language. The paper map in the Ledger is Tom's own drawing, and he writes two names on every place: what Ron calls it and what the street plate says. How much of that map is filled in, and how far he has driven with the radio quiet, is the record the last act reads.

Fares. By law every booking is written down before the journey and a constable may demand the book. Sheila writes the driver's badge number against each line. Tom has no badge. From the first night she writes Mickey's, 712, and says so once: "You are on his number. I write it every night. I would sooner I did not." The oldest line in the book is the Widow's: twenty to six, office to the dock gate, one passenger, every working day since 1971.

Debts. The front page of Mickey's debt book has five names. The back pages have thirty-one nicknames and no addresses: Lord Ash, The Blister, Sell the Bed. Only Ron can read them, and the debts are uncollectable for one reason: these are the men who are never picked. From week four small sums begin to be paid at the counter by men Tom never called on, and Sheila receipts each with its date.

Favours and witnesses. Who saw Tom follow his own doorman; who was in the pen when he said what he said; who was let off in week one and who was hit; which drivers had the gate tax paid for them. These are the people who stand at their kerbs on the last night, or do not.

The Kirby account. Sheila prices everything Ron hits and keeps it in a book of its own. It is the only account in the office that is funny, until it is not.

Rent falls on Fridays and is paid or missed as the game already has it. It is never the plot.

What the ending settles. Number 712 goes back into the book at Mickey's last line, and Tom is written under a number of his own or is not. The Kirby account is ruled off and nobody says what paid it. The back pages are closed by the men themselves. The Widow's standing line is not settled: she books the twenty to six for the Monday after.

## 3. The lead

What Tom wants in week one. To be nobody's passenger. He has arrived in a town where he has to be led to his own front door, where every knock is answered for the man standing behind him, and where his own bookkeeper will not use his name. He wants the Hook in his own head, fast, so that he need never ask anybody anything again. The player shares it from the first minute, because the game has no markers and he is lost too.

How it is played. Tom does not ask. If the player stands lost for ninety seconds, Tom reads a street plate under his breath and tries another turning. The player can ask the way at any time, of Pauline with one key or of anyone on the pavement. The game only makes sure he notices that Tom would not.

What forces the want to change, and when. Two blows, a week apart. On Sunday 11 November the Ledger strikes through what he believed about his own people, and he learns that Ron has been standing in the pen for him. He answers in the old way: he will put it right alone, with a job, without asking. In the small hours of Friday 16 November that job costs Ron his hands. After that the old want is not on offer. He cannot need nobody while a man cannot do up his own coat because of him. The new want is as plain as the old: to be Ron's hands, as Ron was his eyes, and to get him to his appointment on the seventh Friday in whatever state the two of them are in.

What it costs if he does not change. The player can go on alone through week six, and the game lets him feel it. Fares fall away at the gate. Two against one is deadly and there is nobody at his shoulder. Doors that opened to Ron stay shut. And the Widow offers him the foreman's coat, which is the old want granted in full: a man who knows every name in the Hook as a price and needs no one. If he puts it on, he gets the gate, the office fills, and the passenger seat is empty for good.

Five deeds only he would do.

One. On his first fare he drives round the chapel block four times with the priest in the back sooner than wind the window down. The player can end it on any lap. The fourth lap is there for the player who will not, and the priest has a line for it.

Two. He draws his own map of the Hook, on foot, at night, and writes two names on every corner: Ron's and the council's. He chalks a small N on each kerb he has learned. The street takes the marks for the gas board's.

Three. Whatever he comes to believe in week four, he says it to a man's face or not at all. The game has no way to accuse anyone behind his back. It is said at the pen, at the rank or on the pavement, where people are.

Four. On the infirmary kerb at five in the morning he does up Ron's coat for him, top button first, without being asked.

Five. On the seventh Friday he pulls up outside Ron's house, sits there with the engine ticking until Ron comes out, and holds the car door for his own doorman.

His fixed lines. Tom's talk is the player's. These few are his own, and short.

To Ron, in the second minute: "I wasn't lost. I was early."

To anyone who offers the way: "I know where I am."

To the Widow, the first morning: "Front or back?"

In the learner's car: "Stop." And once: "Stop means the car as well."

At the pen, if he chooses Ron: "You sold your book. Now you're selling mine." At the rank, if he chooses Kevin: "Keys." At the railings, if he chooses Darren: "What did you get for it?"

On the kerb: "Hold still."

To the Widow, the last morning: "The long way."

To the examiner: "Nowak. I've been driving on seven one two. It isn't mine."

The last line of the game: "Which way, Ron?"

What is funny about him. He is the only minicab proprietor in England who has to be taken to his own office. His fares direct him: Ada, seventy years on Quay Street, thanks him for showing her parts of the Hook she had never seen. He will not be told and he will not pretend, so he says true, proud, useless things. The town's joke about him changes by the week and the player hears it change: the new owner asked a gull; Nowak went to the infirmary by way of the fish dock; and at last, from a casual off the minibus, with respect, "Ask the cab bloke. He knows."

## 4. The people who matter

Four people are carried through every week: Ron, Sheila, the Widow and Darren. Eight more are named in the main line: Kevin Needham, Pauline Kitching, Lee Drewery, Lord Ash, Ada, June Suddaby, DS Carol Ellis and Mr Pybus. The Widow has section 5 to herself.

Ron Kirby, 58.

What he wants for himself. To fetch somebody. Thirty years a registered docker, and a docker is a man who is sent for. He has been carried everywhere he has ever been: the works bus, the black cars at funerals, Mickey's cabs. He does not want a driver's wage. He wants to pull up outside a house, the once, and have somebody come out because he has come.

One deed only he would do. On the first afternoon he watches Tom walk past the office twice and does not call out, because he does not like to shout at a man in the street. And he takes the Widow's coat, the most shameful thing a man of his kind can put on, to tap one of Mickey's debtors on the shoulder each morning, and tells nobody, because telling would be asking for thanks.

How he talks. Slowly and exactly, never hurried, never loud. "Boss." "Owt", "nowt", "the once". Every building by what it was. The turn after it has gone. He swears at road signs and at nothing else.

"You'll be him. You've walked past it twice. I didn't like to shout."

"Give way. To who? There's nobody there. I'm not giving way to nobody."

"That's the crane." (Week six, of a square of rust.)

"I'm a doorman, Miss Ellis. I shut them in a door." "Both of them, Mr Kirby?" "It were a big door."

How he changes, or refuses to. He will not learn a street by its plate: "It's the Mission." He will not blame anybody for his hands, the men who did it least of all. What changes is one thing: he lets a door be held for him. The bond can be heard in one word: he calls Tom "boss" from the first minute, "Mr Nowak" from the moment he is named, and "boss" again as he walks out of the shed.

Sheila Dunn, 53.

What she wants for herself. One clean page. Thirty-one years of lines in that book, and for seven weeks every one of Tom's has gone down against a dead man's number. She wants to write a number that belongs to a living driver, and she will not ask for it. On the first Friday she telephones the council: "Suddaby's. One candidate. The thirtieth of November." She has two men who need an examiner. She never says which she meant.

One deed only she would do. She prices the damage. The Kirby account, read aloud as she writes it: "Bollard, dock road. No charge. It was ours already. Wing mirror, nearside, six pounds forty. Harbour Board fence, eleven feet. They have invoiced. Gull. No charge." At the count each night she tests Ron on road signs she has drawn herself on the backs of fare slips.

How she talks. Four words where four will do. She has never rounded a number. "Mr Nowak." "Mr Kirby."

"Mirror, signal, manoeuvre, Mr Kirby. In that order. It is not a choice of three."

"Do not ask me to price it."

"I booked a candidate. I did not say which."

How she changes, or refuses to. She will not be driven, thanked or told what Tom meant by a thing. She never once says his first name. On the last page she writes it.

Darren Milner, 25.

What he wants for himself. The seat. To be taken on; to be in the car when it goes somewhere. He asks every week, and every week it is a different job he would be good at.

One deed only he would do. He sells Tom's car radio back to him out of Tom's own car. He borrows the new owner's hand-drawn map "to check a thing", copies it with its mistakes, and sells it at the dock gate to the casuals at fifty pence. In the passenger seat he is Ron turned inside out: he knows what every building is going to be, and calls every turn early, wrong, and with confidence.

How he talks. Fast, friendly, always closing a sale.

"That's flats. That's a marina. That's flats. That's nothing yet but it'll be flats."

"Left. No. Yeah, left. Other left."

"I could do Ron's seat. I've the legs for it."

How he changes, or refuses to. Not tidily. After the hands the paper and DS Ellis both ask him what he saw at the gate, and for the first time in his life he has a thing he will not sell. The same week he sells the paper a photograph of Ron's plaster for ten pounds and puts the ten pounds on Sheila's counter for the Kirby account.

The light ones.

Kevin Needham, the day driver, twelve years in car one and never once late. He carries the pen's dinners down to the gate at noon and has since the canteen shut. It looks worse than it is.

Pauline Kitching, on the radio. Gives the way by landmark with opinions attached: "the house with the slippers on the step, and she's no better than she should be."

Lee Drewery, a docker with the gate behind him, on the front page of the debt book for fifteen pounds. He does what he is picked to do.

Lord Ash, of the back pages, always smoking, picked eleven times in sixteen months. He rides to the gate at half past five and pays in five pence pieces of both sizes.

Ada, at her window. She sees who leaves the street at five in the morning and who goes after him.

June Suddaby, Mickey's daughter, a night nurse, who tells the truth about hands.

DS Carol Ellis, who reads the fare book standing up and notices whose number is in it.

Mr Pybus, of the council's licensing office, who once failed his own brother-in-law.

## 5. The opponent

Maureen Jensen, the Widow, 61.

Who she is. Head of the dockside syndicate, as canon has her. Since July 1989 she also holds, quite lawfully, the Harbour Board's contract to supply labour at the dock gate: sixty men at six, twenty picked. Her reader calls the names and her men sell the tap on the shoulder for a cut of the day. She has never stood in the pen herself. No woman did. Her husband went down a hold in 1968, and she has run the far side of that rail for twenty-two years and the rail itself for sixteen months. She cannot drive: "I never learned. There was always a man."

Why she is inside Tom's circle. She is the first person in his car every day. She was Mickey's friend in the way that two people who have never told each other a lie are friends. She opens the front door of car two and sits in Ron's seat, and Ron climbs into the back. She pays on Fridays, exact, in an envelope, and has never tipped. She times each run on her husband's watch and gives the driver the figure. And what she wants is the one thing Tom has.

What she wants for herself. A foreman the pen will stand for. She is tired of holding the gate with muscle, and the men will take a call from Ron Kirby that they will never take from her. She believes she is offering him the only trade left that fits him, and that the car is a toy.

How she works. She never threatens. She states a rule and keeps it. She finds out what each person wants, offers exactly that, once, and honours every deal. She never lies to Tom. Everything she tells him about his own office is true, which is what makes her hard to fight.

"I'll save you turning round."

"Fourteen minutes. Mickey did it in nine. I have had time to think about you."

"And where is it you want to go, Mr Nowak? I'd guess nobody has asked."

"I collected it for you. You would have had a dog bite and a broken door. I only wanted you to know it can be done politely."

"I watched that pen from the wrong side of the rail for thirty years. Now I own the rail. Do not expect me to be sorry."

How she gains ground where the player sees it. Week one, the seat: she takes the front, and Ron gets in the back without a word. Week two, the debt: her receipt is in Iveson's cash box before Tom reaches it, she hands him the eighty pounds in full on the Friday, and her man at the gate starts taking fifty pence from every Mickey's car. Week three, the ground: two of her men are at the job first. Week four, the doorman: Ron is in her coat, and at the next job she is waiting herself with a cup of tea from the hut: "You are eleven minutes late, Mr Nowak. You came by the fish dock. You asked twice." Week five, the hands. Week six, the office: Kevin drives her men, nobody at the gate will ride in a Mickey's car, and she offers Tom the coat and a fair price for the back pages. Week seven, nothing, for the first time: the set on her windowsill tells her something that is not so.

The meeting at the end. Tom's own line is that he never lays hands on a woman or on anyone of sixty or more, and she is both, so there is no blow to be struck and the game does not pretend otherwise. She is faced as she has been met: in the front seat of car two at twenty to six on Friday 30 November, with Ron in the back in plaster, the player driving and choosing the road. It ends at the gate at six, with a ship on the tide and a pen that is full or is not.

## 6. The seven weeks

The main line is about 720 minutes. Driving is about a fifth of every hour and carries most of what is said.

### Week one, 15 to 21 October: the inheritance (about 125 minutes)

Beats. Monday: off the ferry with Mickey's letter of landmarks that are no longer there; Ron on his bollard; Sheila's walk-round, with "New management"; the first fare, the priest to his house by the chapel, Ron in the passenger seat uninvited; the debt book, five names on the front page and the back pages that are "Mr Kirby's to read"; Darren's £22; after dark, the envelope to the ferry landing past the constable. Tuesday: at twenty to six the Widow, in the front. At the gate, sixty men in the pen. A crate is short off the night's work, and her night foreman is put off the gate in front of all of them: "The foreman answers for his night. That is all the gate is." In her hut, on the windowsill, is a cab radio with a strip of sticking plaster across it marked MICKEY'S, and Pauline's voice coming out of it, small, calling car one. Nobody remarks on it. Then Lee Drewery's £15 on the quay, and the count, where Sheila writes 712. Wednesday and Thursday: Wayne Harrison's £30, Julie Kettlewell's £9, Ada's tea, and the first door tried without Ron, which stays shut. Friday: rent. Ron comes to the counter in his good jacket and asks Sheila, not Tom, whether a man of fifty-eight can be learned to drive. She rings the council. By evening car two has L-plates under its roof sign. Sunday: the first lesson, on the dock road. Then Sheila on her day off: "So which is it going to be?"

Hands. Walking lost; two fares and a lesson at the wheel; a carry through watched ground; three collections; a first fight; the handbrake.

In the car. Father Walsh, who lets Tom miss the house. The Widow: "And where is it you want to go, Mr Nowak?" Lord Ash, not picked, walking home past the cab: the player can offer him the ride, and he takes it only as far as the corner.

Funny and grave. Funny: the laps of the chapel block, and the lesson. Grave: the pen at six, seen from the driver's seat: twenty men walking in and forty walking home.

Choices. Darren: fronted up, paid in what he hears, or struck out. Lee: beaten in public, allowed to stand, or left to pay down, which sets Lee's part in week five. Julie: mercy or shame, which decides whether Tom has a driver who can be trusted on the night of the cars. The bollard, hit or missed. Sunday's answer: if he winds the outfit down, the night work of weeks two to four becomes the firm's own lawful carrying off the dock, and the Widow meets it just the same.

### Week two, 22 to 28 October: two in the car (about 110 minutes)

Beats. Monday: fifty-six at the gate. Ron reads the back pages aloud and three calls are made with him as the knowledge: which yard, which wall, who is in. Every door opens, to him. The third is Lord Ash's, who brings out what he has: a tobacco tin of five pence pieces, large and small together. Tuesday: the Widow's man at the gate asks fifty pence of every Mickey's car. Wednesday: DS Ellis reads the fare book standing at the counter. "Mr Suddaby has been busy for a dead man." Thursday night: Iveson's hut. Ron knows the back way that the yard hand leaves open at nine and the name of the dog. Inside, the cash box holds one sheet of paper: "Received on account of M. Suddaby, deceased, £80. M. Jensen." Friday: she hands Tom the £80 in the car. Saturday: the second lesson.

Hands. Collections that work because of who is standing behind him; a break-in three ways; the dog quieted; the handbrake again.

In the car. Lord Ash at half past five, to the gate, explaining without being asked why tomorrow it has to be him. Ada on Thursday, directing her own cab. The Widow with her watch.

Funny and grave. Funny: the second lesson. Ron takes the whole dock road at eleven miles an hour with the commentary of a man bringing a ship up a river ("Coming up on the ice house. That's the ice house. Leaving the ice house. We're shifting now"), will not sound the horn at a gull ("It were here first"), and waves to a night-watchman with his whole arm, so that the car follows the arm into eleven feet of the Harbour Board's fence. Then one thing that is not funny: he changes down for the bend, cleanly, by ear, which nobody showed him. In passing he names the swing bridge: "That's off an hour either side of high water. You learn that the once." Grave: Lord Ash's tin on the counter.

Choices. The tin: taken, counted and receipted, or pushed back across the counter. Either is remembered by the man and told in the pen. The gate tax: Tom pays it for his drivers from his own pocket, lets them pay it, or has it out with her man at the gate. It decides how long Kevin and Terry stay. And each time the player has the way said again, the Day book writes that he did.

### Week three, 29 October to 4 November: five o'clock (about 105 minutes)

Beats. Monday: fifty-two at the gate, and a minibus of casuals from away whose driver winds his window down and asks Tom for the fish dock. Tuesday night: Darren's tip, a van left loaded overnight in the cold store lane. Lee Drewery and a second docker are leaning on it. With Ron at his side it is two against two, and a fair fight. "She says use the gate like everybody else." It is the last time the player sees Ron's hands as fists. Wednesday: Ada, over the tea. "He goes out at five now. Every morning. In his good boots." That evening Ron asks for his mornings: "It's not money and it's not you." Thursday to Saturday belong to the player, in any order or none. He can follow Ron at five to the gate hut and see him come out with a paper in his hand; if he is careless, Ada sees him go. He can open the boot of car one at noon, find parcels with the gate's chalk on them, lift one, and find it warm: haddock and chips, the pen's dinners, which Kevin has carried down since the canteen shut, with the Widow's dinner money in his coat. He can watch Darren at the gate taking coins from the casuals, from too far off to see that what he sells is a copy of Tom's own map. He can get into the gate hut at night, three ways, and find the set on the sill and beside it a pad in a neat hand: times and places, in the order Pauline said them. Saturday night: the third lesson, when Ron says why. Sunday: Sheila at the count. "He has paid for the fence. In notes. I did not ask where a doorman finds notes."

Hands. A lift off a van; a fight beside Ron; a tail on foot at dawn; a lifted parcel; casing; a break-in; the handbrake.

In the car. Ron's confession (section 7). Kevin, riding as a passenger while car one is at the yard: "Twelve years. He never once asked me where I'd been at dinner."

Funny and grave. Funny: the new owner breaks into his own driver's boot and steals a docker's haddock, and the pen has heard by one o'clock. Grave: "I'd rather you didn't ask me that this week, boss."

Choices. Whom he follows, and whether he is seen. Whether he goes into the hut at all. Whether he asks Ron straight what the mornings are for, which Ron will not answer and will not forget being asked. Each writes a line under a name in the Ledger, as sure as the evidence: "Ron takes the Widow's wage, he is sure." "Darren is paid at the gate, he thinks."

### Week four, 5 to 11 November: the coat (about 115 minutes)

Beats. Monday 5 November: forty-seven at the gate. Tom drives the Widow down as he does every day, and Ron is in the pen in a foreman's coat with GATE across the shoulders, reading names from her list. He taps Lord Ash. The Widow, getting out: "He looks well in it." That night, at the dockers' bonfire on the quay, Ron stands across the fire and does not come round it. Tuesday: the outfit's next job, a carry off the fish dock. The Widow is sitting at the place before Tom arrives. Wednesday to Friday: the window (section 7). Saturday: the first job after it. If Darren has the seat, he directs Tom with total confidence to the wrong yard, and Tom forces the door of a pigeon loft belonging to a man in pyjamas. If Ron still has it, he directs Tom, correctly, to a yard that was pulled down in 1979: "It were here." Either way, at the right place, there are warm cigarette ends on the step. The job has been met again. So it was not whoever he chose. Sunday 11 November, Remembrance Sunday: the chapel, and the dockers' wreath carried by a branch with eleven members left. At the count that night Sheila puts the back pages on the counter. "Read it out, Mr Nowak."

Hands. Driving her to the gate; a carry; whatever the player's own blow is; a forced door; and the reading of the receipts, line by line, on the player's own key.

In the car. The Widow on Monday. Darren in Ron's seat, selling Tom the future of every building they pass.

Funny and grave. Funny: the pigeon loft, or the yard that is not there. Grave: the pen on Friday morning, and the count on Sunday.

Choices. The road taken through the window, and where. And from Monday the player decides, every morning that he drives the Widow down, whether Ron goes on standing there. He can order his own doorman out of her coat, and Ron will go home. Or he can swallow it. Each morning Ron stands, one more man from the back pages gets a day's work and remembers who let it happen; and each morning the street reads the coat on Mickey's doorman as Tom's shame, and says so to his face.

### Week five, 12 to 18 November: the ship (about 110 minutes)

Beats. Monday: forty-four at the gate. A Soviet ship is on the far berth with cars on her deck, and the Widow's casuals and the Board's own clerks are driving them off by night. Ron is night foreman for the week, on every road: the men who owe Mickey money are on that ship, and he will not come off the gate for anybody. Lord Ash and four others from the back pages come to the office counter. They mean to drive six cars off for themselves, as the managers do now, and they have nobody who can find the north road in the dark and no way past the gate. Tom takes it on. He takes it on to put last Sunday right in the only way he knows: alone, with a job, so that no man of his need ever stand in that coat again. Tuesday and Wednesday: the casing, from a parked cab with a cigarette, and the plan, made at dusk at the office with no screen. Three ways off the quay: the barrier, which is Ron's, and means asking him; the swing bridge, which is off for an hour either side of high water; the fish dock lane, which Lee watches. Six cars want six drivers, and each driver is somebody from the record. Thursday night: the job, as planned. Friday, small hours: the transit shed. At the count that day Sheila rules off the Kirby account. By the weekend the ship has sailed.

Hands. A plan; a convoy of strange cars through streets the player must already know; a fight or a wait at the bridge; fists on a steel door; the fastest drive in the game; a row of coat buttons.

In the car. Julie Kettlewell on the way down, if he was kind to her in week one, who has not driven since she was laid off and asks to go first. Ron, to the infirmary.

Funny and grave. Funny: The Blister, recruited as a driver, arrives at the compound as the last car leaves it and asks which is his; Darren stops on the ship's ramp to try the radio. Grave: the shed (section 7).

Choices. Whether to do it at all. The hour, the way and the drivers. Whether to ask Ron to lift the barrier, which puts him in it knowingly, or to go round him and keep him clean. Whether anybody touches the radio. The job can come off whole, in part, or not at all, and each plays differently. What the plan cannot change is whose night it was.

### Week six, 19 to 25 November: the door (about 90 minutes)

Beats. Monday: forty-one at the gate. Ron is at home on Staith Row with both arms in plaster to the elbow. Tom works Monday alone, and the game lets the old want run: shut doors, a fare who waits for the other firm, two men by the car. Tuesday: the Widow, in the hut, with the coat on its hook behind her. "You have cost me a foreman, Mr Nowak. I shall want another. You have the list in your office and nobody to read it to." While she talks, the set on the sill says, in Pauline's voice, "Car two, car two, where are you, love?" It is his own office, in her hut, and it has been since January. The Ledger strikes through the last of what he believed in week four. Then the change, when the player makes it: he drives to Staith Row and opens the passenger door. From there Ron is at his side again, and waits at every door, and Tom opens it. The lessons turn round. Each night, with the radio off, Ron names a place by what it was and Tom has to find it, and they stop outside the house of one man from the back pages, and Tom chalks his N on the kerb. On Thursday they do the outfit's first job with the radio dead. Nobody is waiting. Any morning this week Tom can walk into the pen at six and unsay what he said there, if he said it: "I said a thing here. It wasn't so." DS Ellis calls about the hands. Lee Drewery is on the fish quay every dinner time, alone. Six Soviet cars are under sheets in Iveson's yard.

Hands. A day of shut doors; then opening every door for another man; night runs by a map in his own head; a silent job; possibly a fight that can end in a killing.

In the car. Ron, directing with his elbows. June Suddaby, coming off nights, who says in the back seat what she would not say on the kerb. A casual off the minibus who asks the way to the cold store, and Tom tells him, and then sits for a moment with his hands on the wheel.

Funny and grave. Funny: "It were a big door." And Sheila at the count, reading the Highway Code aloud to two grown men, one who cannot turn the pages and one who has not noticed that the questions are now put to him. Grave: Ron and a cup of tea he cannot lift, and what he says if Tom has been at Lee: "He were doing a day's work, boss. He got the tap that morning same as I'd have give it."

Choices. The coat, taken or left on its hook; taken, Tom reads the list himself at six next morning and every door at the gate opens to him. The back pages, sold to her or kept. Lee, left alone, beaten, or killed by a fall, which the town never forgets and the men at the kerbs least of all. The cars: sold, which the street calls selling Ron's hands; driven back in the night and left in a row outside her hut with the keys on the sill; or left under their sheets. The unsaying. And how many streets, on how many nights.

### Week seven, 26 November to 2 December: the seventh Friday (about 65 minutes)

Beats. Monday: thirty-eight at the gate. Monday to Wednesday: the feint. Darren, who can describe anything that is not there, makes up three jobs, and Pauline reads them over the air as ways for car one. Sheila will not have them in the book. Pauline cannot act. "Bring it down, Pauline." "I am down." For three nights the Widow's men stand in the rain at the fish dock, the cold store lane and Iveson's gate, and the player can drive past with his lights off and look at them. Thursday night: the fetching, door to door, with the radio dead. Friday 30 November: twenty to six, the Widow; six, the gate; nine, the examiner.

Hands. Driving dark; the horn, once, at each door; her last ride; a car door, held.

In the car. Thirty-one men, one at a time, each with his line. Then the Widow in the front and Ron in the back. Then Mr Pybus.

Funny and grave. Funny: Pauline's performance; Ron trying the horn with his forearm and raising the whole of Staith Row. Grave: the gate at six.

Choices. None that is new. Every line offered on the last morning is one the record has already earned or lost.

## 7. Key scenes written out

### 7.1 The opening, and the scene players will retell

Monday 15 October 1990, twenty to four. The ferry, twenty seconds. Control on the gangway. In Tom's hand is a letter the player can raise and read at any time, in Mickey's small hand: Off the boat. Left at the Mission. Past the weighbridge. Under the Board's clock. Ask for Ron.

There is no Mission. The player walks. A woman asked the way says, "The Mission? You're twenty years late, love." If the player stands still, a hint offers the Board's clock, which says ten past nine. Whichever way he goes, inside two minutes he passes a carpet warehouse with a chapel's doorway, and further on a big man of fifty-eight sitting on a bollard outside a cab office with his hands on his knees. From that side nothing says Mickey's. The player walks past. On the second pass the man speaks without getting up.

RON: You'll be him.

RON: You've walked past it twice. I didn't like to shout.

TOM (fixed): I wasn't lost. I was early.

RON: Aye. Well. You're on time now. (He looks at the letter.) That's his hand. Left at the Mission. (He nods back up the street, at the carpets.) You were stood in it.

He gets up, opens the office door and holds it: the first door in the Hook, opened for Tom.

Ten minutes on, Pauline has a fare and no car. Tom takes car two for the priest's house. Ron gets into the passenger seat unasked.

RON: I'll come. I can't drive it, mind. I'll tell you where things were.

PAULINE (radio): Past the chapel, second left, the house with the slippers on the step.

RON: Garside's. That were Garside's. Left at Garside's. (The turning is behind them.) That were it.

The player learns the rule inside a minute and laughs at it: the building comes before the turn and the word comes after. Each lap of the block, Father Walsh in the back says a little less. On the fourth:

WALSH: I'd an idea you would find it. I have the time. I've buried three men off this street who never found it either.

The scene players will retell is Sunday 21 October: the dock road, and a minicab with L-plates under its roof sign. Ron is at the wheel with the seat right back and his knees against the dash. The player has two things: a key that makes Tom say "Stop", and the handbrake.

RON: You've the brake, boss. I'll see to the going.

He goes, at eleven miles an hour. Then the thing the scene is for. The dock road has no turnings. It is one straight mile with the water on one side.

RON: Which way, boss?

There is no line for Tom. The player looks down a road with no turnings and finds he does not know either.

RON: You don't know where we are.

RON: It's the dock road. There's only it. (He drives on.) We'll go where I can see, then.

At the far end stands one iron bollard with a stripe of the firm's blue paint across it at bumper height. Ron sees a night-watchman he knows and lifts his whole arm to him, and the car follows the arm.

TOM (fixed, on the key): Stop.

RON: I'm waving.

TOM (fixed, on the key again): Stop means the car as well.

If the handbrake is in time the car stops a yard short, and Ron looks at the bollard for some while. "That's been there since nineteen fifty-one. I've never seen it from this side." If it is late, the bollard, not hard. On Monday Sheila opens a new book and reads its first line aloud as she writes it: "Bollard, dock road. No charge. It was ours already. Mickey had it twice."

### 7.2 The false belief and its turn, on every road

The evidence is all true. By Tuesday 6 November three jobs have been met. Ron takes the Widow's wage, leaves at five and stands in her coat. Kevin carries parcels with the gate's chalk on them and has her envelope in his pocket. Darren is at the gate each morning taking coins from her casuals. Any of the three can fairly be believed to be selling the office. None of them is.

What a careful player can see first. Each met job has a line in the Day book that says Pauline gave the way. Kevin was off shift for one. Darren knew of only one. Ron did not know the second until he was standing at it. The only work never met was done on Ron's word alone, with the radio untouched. And the player who went into the hut has seen the pad.

The window is Wednesday 7 to Friday 9 November. No prompt opens it. The men are where they always are. The deciding line is offered only when the player walks up to one of them and raises it himself, and a plain yes is asked for twice.

Road one: Ron, at the pen. Friday, six o'clock. Ron is reading the list. The Widow is at the hut door with her cup.

TOM (fixed): You sold your book. Now you're selling mine.

Ron looks down at the list. He finds his place with his thumb, steps along the rail and taps the next man, who is Lord Ash, on the shoulder.

RON: I'll bring the key round, Mr Nowak.

Then somebody at the back of the pen laughs, and it is not at Ron.

A VOICE: He can't find Quay Street and he's found a seller.

The pen knows what the coat is for. Every man Ron has tapped was told, as he was tapped, where to take two pounds of the day's money. Nobody has told the new owner, because nobody tells him anything.

WIDOW (as Tom passes the hut): That was well done, Mr Nowak. I could not have made him stay.

The key is on the counter by eight. Darren is in the seat by nine. Said in the car at night instead, the same line has Ron stop the car himself, get out and walk home; there are fewer witnesses, and the Widow only hears of it.

Road two: Kevin, at the rank.

TOM (fixed): Keys.

KEVIN: Twelve years. (He puts them on the bonnet of car one, and an envelope beside them.) That's hers. Thirty-one dinners. You'll want to see they get it.

He walks down the dock road. By Monday he is driving her men to the far berths. The firm has no day driver, so the player drives the day's fares himself or watches the book thin. Thirty-one men have no dinner that Friday and know whose doing it is.

Road three: Darren, at the railings, with a shove or a blow.

TOM (fixed): What did you get for it?

DARREN: Fifty pence each. (He holds one out: Tom's own map, in Darren's hand, with Tom's own mistake at the chapel block.) It's a good map. They keep getting lost on it. I've told them it's the new edition they want.

He is off the pavement for a week. There are no tips and nothing told for nothing.

Road four: nobody. Holding back is three different things, and each is felt. The player who followed Ron and then said nothing hears it on Saturday, in the car: "You had me followed, boss. Ada said. You never said owt after. I'd sooner you'd shouted." The player who asked Ron straight what the mornings were for, and then let it lie, has Ron come to the counter himself on the Monday: "You asked me straight and then you left it lay. I've not known many do the second." The player who found the pad gets a line low on the screen that night: It is the wireless, he thinks. Nobody sold anything. From then on he can keep his work off the air, and it is not met, and he can begin telling the box lies three weeks before anyone else. He still does not know what the coat is for.

The turn, on every road. On Saturday the job is met again, with the accused man gone or the silence kept, so the player already knows on Saturday night that he was wrong, or that he was right to wait. On Sunday night, at the count, Sheila puts the debt book on the counter open at the back, and a spike of receipts beside it.

SHEILA: Read it out, Mr Nowak. I write. You read.

The player turns each receipt on his own key. Tom's voice is not heard. She says each one over as she enters it: the name, the sum, the morning the man was picked.

SHEILA: Lord Ash. Two pounds. Monday the fifth.

SHEILA: Sell the Bed. One pound fifty. Tuesday the sixth.

SHEILA: The Blister. Three pounds. Wednesday the seventh. He brought it on the Thursday.

SHEILA: Lord Ash. Two pounds. Friday the ninth.

She stops writing.

SHEILA: Those are the mornings he has stood in that coat. One of yours a morning. He taps them because nobody else will, and they come in here with it. He asked me not to say and I have not said. You have read it.

Then one line more, and which one depends on the road. If it was Ron: "You said it in front of the pen. He will want it unsaid there. I would." If it was Kevin: "I have priced the haddock. It is on your account, not his." If it was Darren: "Mr Milner's lip will mend. I have had his map in. It is yours, with the mistakes." If he followed and said nothing: "You had him followed, Mr Nowak. He would have preferred to be asked." If he found the pad: "You knew it was the wireless. Then you knew it was not him. You might have told him so." If he asked and waited, she says nothing, and closes the book more gently than usual.

In the Ledger, under Ron Kirby, the line the player's own looking wrote is struck through in Tom's hand: "11 November. He did not. He was getting my debtors a day's work."

### 7.3 The cab confession

Saturday 3 November, night, the dock road, rain, the third lesson. Ron drives. The player has the handbrake. A learner cannot look at his passenger, and that is why this can be said.

RON: Don't say owt for a bit, boss. I can only do the one thing.

Half a mile. The crane, a third of it gone, against the sky.

RON: That's the crane.

RON: You know what a docker is? He's a man that's sent for. That's all it is. Somebody shouts your name of a morning and you go where you're put. Thirty year. I never minded. I were good at being sent for.

RON: I've been carried everywhere I've ever been. Works bus. Mickey's cars. Them long black ones, more times than I'd like. I've never once got anywhere and been the reason.

The bend. He changes down for it, cleanly, by ear. Neither of them says so.

RON: Day they stopped it, I come out the gate and stood at the bus stop, and I didn't know which bus went where. Fifty-seven. I'd never wanted to go anywhere I weren't sent.

RON: So it's not the driving. Tell Sheila it's the driving, she's priced it. I'd like to fetch somebody. The once. Pull up outside a house and sit there with it ticking. And they come out, and it's because I've come.

RON: I'd not sound the horn, mind. I'd just wait. They'd know.

The bollard is coming, and the handbrake is under the player's hand. If he stops the car short: "There. I saw it that time. I were talking and I saw it." If he lets it go: "Put it on the account." Either way the Ledger writes one line under his name: He wants to fetch somebody.

### 7.4 The loss

Friday 16 November, about two in the morning. This is the version in which the convoy is bottled. Tom goes down under four men and comes round on a fish box inside the transit shed, with a split lip and the door shut. Lee Drewery is one of the four. The Widow comes in from the hut in her husband's coat with the cup still in her hand.

WIDOW: Six cars, Mr Nowak. Off my quay, on my night. I am not angry. I want you to understand that. I have a rule, and it is not about you.

WIDOW: A man who drives off this quay what is not his does not drive again. It was the rule when it was shovels.

Lee looks at Tom's hands. The player has control and nothing to use it on; he can type, and nothing he types moves the rule. The side door opens. Ron, in the foreman's coat, the list still in his pocket, not hurrying.

RON: Morning, Maureen.

WIDOW: Go back to the gate, Ronald.

RON: It's my night. I'm foreman. Barrier's mine, bridge is mine, lane's mine. What goes off this quay between ten and six, I answer for. You said it in front of sixty men.

WIDOW: Not for him.

RON: I picked the night. I told him the tide.

He did not, and she knows it.

WIDOW: You will not drive, Ronald.

RON: I can't drive now. Ask him. Ask Sheila, she has a book of it.

She puts the cup down on a crate.

WIDOW: I do not bend it. If I bend it for a man I like, it is not a rule, it is only me. Lee.

WIDOW (to Tom, as he is walked out): I don't want your hands. They are no use to me broken. His are. A foreman wants a voice and a list.

The door slides shut with Tom outside it. The player is on the quay beside the car, in control. Nothing is heard. He can beat on the steel door, sit in the car, or drive away, in which case Ron walks to the infirmary. It is about a minute. Then the side door opens and Ron comes out by himself, walking as he always walks, with his hands held a little in front of him, the way a man carries something he has been told not to drop. He comes to the passenger side and stands at it.

RON: You'll have to do the door, boss.

The player opens it. It is the first. Inside, Ron cannot pull it shut, and Tom leans across.

RON: Infirmary. You'll not want the wireless. (A moment.) Left at the Mission.

He says it on time: the word before the turn, for the first time in the game. He does it at every turning for two miles, early and clear, as if he were reading the list, and then stops talking.

On the infirmary kerb June Suddaby is out for one cigarette. "Dad's car. Still pulls left." Then she sees the hands and stops being anybody's daughter. At five o'clock Ron stands on the same kerb in wet plaster to both elbows, with his coat hanging open, in November.

TOM (fixed): Hold still.

He does it up, top button first. Ron looks over his head at the road.

RON: It were quick. Lee's a good worker.

JUNE (to Tom, at the door): He will hold a cup by Christmas. He will not hold a wheel. Don't let anybody tell him different, and don't you.

At that day's count Sheila rules a line under the Harbour Board's fence and writes one word. If the player asks what paid it: "Do not ask me to price it."

The other plans. If the cars got clean away, the rule is kept at the six o'clock call instead, in front of the pen: "Who answers for the night?" Ron steps out from behind his own list. If Tom asked him to lift the barrier, he answers for a thing he did: "I lifted it. I'd lift it again." If the player refused the job, Lord Ash's men try it without a driver who knows a road, are taken at the bridge, and Ron answers for them. On every plan Tom arrives in time to open a car door, and no sooner.

### 7.5 The last act, and the meeting

Thursday 29 November, night. The radio is busy: Pauline is sending car one, which is in the yard, to the fish dock. Tom drives dark. Ron has thirty-one nicknames in his head and not one street name. "Sell the Bed. Back of the ropery, the house with the green gate." The player finds each door from what he has really learned. At each he taps the horn once. A man comes out to the kerb, or a curtain moves and nothing more, and what each says is one line out of the record. Lord Ash, if his tin was pushed back across the counter: "I'll be stood here. I'd have been stood somewhere." A man who paid in a week when Tom kept Ron at home: "Six? I've the pen at six."

Friday 30 November, twenty to six. The Widow comes out of the dark and opens the front door of car two. Ron is in the back.

WIDOW: Ronald. How are the hands?

RON: Where you left them, Maureen.

She gets in.

WIDOW: The gate, Mr Nowak. I expect you know the way by now.

TOM (fixed): The long way.

It is a sit-down in a moving car, and what matters is where the player steers. At the first kerb a man stands under a lamp in his work coat with his bag, not walking to the gate. At the next, two. She looks at her husband's watch and not at them.

WIDOW: My wireless said the fish dock.

WIDOW: So you have learned the streets. I wondered when you would tire of asking a box. He lent it me in the storm. The lines were down a fortnight. I would have given it back if anybody had asked.

The gate. Six. The hooter. On the best record there are eleven men in the pen out of thirty-eight, a ship on the far berth and the tide making. For the first morning in nineteen years she does not know what is at her own gate. She makes her offer once, as she makes all of them.

WIDOW: The coat is on its hook. You have the list, you have the streets, you have him to read it. Take the gate with me. Or I get out and call eleven men and send for a bus, and we do this every morning until one of us is short.

The answers are the game's own lines, and only those the record allows. "Your gate, Mrs Jensen": Tom gets out, walks round and opens her door, and she goes into her pen alone. That is always there. "Ron reads it. In turn. Nobody pays": that is there only if the kerbs outnumber the pen.

WIDOW: In turn. That is the Scheme, Ronald. They abolished it.

RON: Not on this quay. Not this morning.

WIDOW: It will not last. There is nothing coming up that river after the spring.

RON: No.

WIDOW: Monday, then. Twenty to six. I shall want the front.

Nine o'clock, the forecourt. Mr Pybus has taken the L-plates off car two himself and laid them on the bonnet. Tom has been to Staith Row. He did not touch the horn. He sat there with it ticking, and Ron came out in his funeral suit with the jacket over his shoulders, because the sleeves will not go over plaster. Tom got out and held the door.

RON: I've come to tell you myself. I can't sit it. I'd not have you stood out here waiting on a man that weren't coming.

PYBUS: I cannot examine a man who cannot hold the wheel, Mr Kirby. The appointment is void.

SHEILA (from the office door, the book on her arm): It is not void. I booked a candidate. I did not say which.

PYBUS: Your badge, Mr Nowak?

TOM (fixed): Nowak. I've been driving on seven one two. It isn't mine.

PYBUS: Seven one two is Suddaby, M. He is dead. (He writes for some time.) The route. I name two points by street. You take me by the shortest way. You do not ask. Nobody in the car assists the candidate.

Ron gets into the front.

RON: I'm not assisting. I'm just in it.

The route is the player's own knowledge: four pairs of street plates, no radio, no hint. Ron does not speak. At one turning his plastered forearm lifts an inch off his knee, a street late, and goes down again. Pybus, if the player knew it: "You know it by the wrong names, Mr Nowak. Every one. But you know it." If not: "The second week of January. Bring the same car. Bring him, if you must."

Sheila writes the last line of the book. On a pass it has a new number, and in the driver's column, for the first time, a word that is not Mr or Management. She turns the book round so that he can read it, and goes to fill the kettle.

Tom walks round the car and opens the passenger door, because the man inside cannot. Ron does not get out.

RON: Go on, then. Somewhere.

TOM (fixed): Which way, Ron?

RON: You know which way.

Tom waits, with it ticking. The car pulls out, and is through the turn at the carpet warehouse before Ron says a word.

RON: Left at the Mission, boss. That were it.

## 9. The endings

Two things from the record decide the ending. The seat: whether Tom put on the Widow's coat in week six, or drove to Staith Row. The kerbs: whether the men at their doors on the last night outnumber the men in the pen, which is the sum of the mornings Ron was left to pick, the debtors spared or squeezed, the back pages kept or sold, the drivers looked after, whoever was wrongly named and whether it was unsaid, Lee, and how many doors Tom could find in the dark. No line is offered on the last morning that the record has not earned.

The Knowledge. The seat kept and the kerbs full. The list is read in turn, from the top, by a man who cannot hold it, so Lord Ash holds it for him. The last image is the passenger door held open, the question asked, and the turn taken before the word. Settled: 712 is back in the book under Mickey's last line; the tap is not sold on this quay this winter; the Kirby account is ruled off; the back pages are paid or struck out by the men's own hands. Not mended: Ron's hands; the wish; the dock, which has thirty-eight men and nothing coming after the spring; Kevin, who does not come back; whoever was wrongly named, who remembers; the Widow, who keeps her contract and the front seat. Darren gets his seat at last: the back one, on Mondays, behind her.

A Street Late. The seat kept and the kerbs too few. "Your gate, Mrs Jensen." She walks into a full pen and calls her twenty, and nothing at the gate changes. The forecourt at nine plays as written. The last image is the same door and the same question, but this time Tom waits for the answer and takes each turn as Ron calls it, a street late. "That were it." They go round the block. Settled: the two of them, and whose number Tom drives on, in January if not now. Not mended: the pen, and all the rest.

The Coat. Tom wore it. The last ride has nobody in the back and begins, "Good morning, foreman." The gate is his and hers, the office is full, and he reads the list at six knowing each man by name, nickname and street. Ron stands on Mickey's door, which he can no longer open, and nods people in. On the seventh Friday no car waits on Staith Row. Ron walks up to the forecourt to tell Mr Pybus himself, and Sheila says nothing about candidates. The last image: a casual in the back of the cab asks for Mission Street, and Tom knows it at once, and there is nobody in the front seat to tell. Settled: the want he arrived with, granted in full. Not mended: everything he would now give for it.

No ending puts Ron at the wheel.

Six of them told.

The Blister. A man on Sluice Lane has a poll tax summons and the bailiff's van is due at ten. Tom carries a settee, a wardrobe and a cooker out over the back fences. The van comes at half past eleven and out gets The Blister, sixteen months off the quay, with a clipboard. He studies the bare room, writes "no goods", and says, "I'm always late. Ask anybody." It leaves a settee in Mickey's waiting room that nobody comes back for.

Cinderella. For thirty years he left the quay at ten to twelve and his gang called him idle. Followed, he goes home with one hand on the wall: the quay lamps go out at midnight, and in the dark he cannot see his own feet. The player can force the timer box behind the Harbour Board, or tell nobody. It leaves one lamp burning all night at berth four, and a man working his first whole night, on the Soviet ship.

Mrs Postill. All through the winter of the ambulance dispute car two took the Hook's sick to the infirmary. She still rings every other Tuesday for the ambulance, lies down across the back seat under her coat, and directs Tom by the ceiling. Nothing has ailed her since February: Mickey never charged, and the lying down seemed to be expected. She asks what a fare is, pays one, and sits up. It leaves a folded blanket in the boot, which is there on the night of the shed.

Danby's class. With no work to go to, Philip Danby teaches the Highway Code in the chapel hall to six dockers and Ron. Tom carries them, and lifts a GIVE WAY sign off the dock road for the wall. Ron gives stopping distances in ships' lengths. Danby turns out to be the pupil: he is filling a ruled book with what they call every building. It leaves the sign on the wall and the only written copy of Ron's map.

Splinter and Plank. Splinter is thirty-four and never had a book; the register shut before his turn. He stands in the pen beside his father, who held one for thirty-one years. As the reader's eye comes down the line, Plank steps back half a pace. Splinter has known for a year and takes the tap anyway, because refusing would undo the one thing his father has left to give. It leaves a worn half-moon in the dirt, a pace behind the rail.

Flags. He drove the Old Basin crane for twenty years and once took the bunting off a ship with it. Alison Sedman wants a photograph before it goes, and Tom gets them both in by night, learning the three ways off the quay as he does. Her paper has no use for the picture: she keeps a box of things on the day before they went, and Mickey's office front is already in it. It leaves the crane's cab plate on the office wall.

## 11. Real and invented

Real, with its place in RESEARCH.md.

Section 1 (firm): the Scheme of 1947 and the end of casual hiring in 1967; abolition on 3 July 1989; the strike of 10 July to 1 August; severance of up to £35,000 until early 1991 and £20,000 after; the losses by November 1990 at Hull, Bristol and Tees; eleven employers insolvent and 868 jobs; a man's registration as his book.

Section 2 (fair): casual hiring back at once through labour supply firms; managers driving cars off a Russian ship at Hull, lawful only since July 1989; dock land bought by the hectare. RESEARCH looked twice for former dockers going into cab driving and found nothing, so Ron's wish is invention.

Section 3 (thin): the call, the pen, the tap on the shoulder, and the nicknames with their reasons (Lord Ash, Manhole, Flags and Splinter from Bristol; The Blister, Cinderella, Sell the Bed, The London Fog, The Man in Black, Swan Vesta and The Broken Boomerang from Liverpool). They are used as the characters' own words and names, never stated as history, and every line that uses them waits on a second source.

Section 4 (firm): the 1976 Act, section 56, the record of every booking and the constable's right to read it; the ambulance dispute, 7 September 1989 to 23 February 1990; the storm of 25 January 1990 and the telephone lines down; the poll tax from 1 April 1990; base rate 15% until October 1990 and 14% after; the small five pence from 27 June 1990, the large one legal until 31 December.

Section 5: a debt book in nicknames (the practice real, the use ours); a cab office carrying the sick in the winter of the dispute (an inference, not found stated).

Real, but from general knowledge and not in RESEARCH.md: the Highway Code with its mirror, signal, manoeuvre; L-plates; and the two calendar days the game design already keeps, Bonfire Night and Remembrance Sunday.

Invented, and marked so. Ron's wish to drive. The council's examination with its route test, Mr Pybus, badge numbers in the fare record, and number 712. The Widow's labour contract with the Harbour Board, the sale of the tap, the foreman's coat, and the rule that a foreman answers for his night. The gate tax. The loan of a radio set to the gate hut in the storm. The Soviet ship at Meridian, her cars and her week. The swing bridge and its tide. The crane, the canteen, the cheque queue at the Board's counter, the bailiff's van, the quay lamps on a timer, the dockers' branch with eleven members, and the head count at the gate. Mrs Postill. Every street and building name: Mission Street, Staith Row, Sluice Lane, the Mission, Garside's, the weighbridge, the ice house.

## 12. Canon questions

The bible is written on the recommended option in each case.

1. Ron's wish and his hands. (a) Ron wants to drive and is entered for the council's examination; his hands are broken for good, and he lives. Recommended. (b) The wish stands, but the hands mend by spring: the loss shrinks to a missed appointment, the last scenes keep their shape, and Ron's last "boss" costs less. (c) Neither: the bond loses its want and this bible does not stand.

2. The Widow at the gate. (a) She holds the lawful labour contract, sells the tap, is 61, cannot drive, and has been Mickey's first fare since 1971. Recommended. (b) She controls the gate but has no standing booking: both meetings move to the gate hut and lose the car.

3. The dead man's number. (a) Tom has no council badge, and Sheila writes Mickey's 712 against his fares. Recommended. (b) Tom has a badge of his own: the forecourt scene ends at "The appointment is void", and the last drive is the examiner's route driven for Ron alone.

4. A killing. (a) The player may kill Lee Drewery by a fall in week six; it is permanent and it empties the kerbs. Recommended. (b) Lee can be beaten and never killed.

5. People. (a) Allow Lord Ash, Mr Pybus and Mrs Postill as new people, the nicknamed dockers in side stories, and Lee Drewery as the Widow's hand. Recommended. (b) The owner renames or cuts any.

6. Places and things. (a) Allow as working names Mission Street, Staith Row, Sluice Lane and the old building names; the swing bridge, the transit shed, the gate hut and its radio set; and one Soviet ship for one week. Recommended. (b) The owner names them. (c) No ship: the big job becomes general cargo and loses the cars.
