**A. Expected player marks**

**K: 9.5.** The lead wants something, changes, and pays for it. The illness turns the back half into a redemption arc that players feel. Around him is a large cast with wants that cross his, a leader whose fall plays out across the whole story, the widow and the principled gunman as bonds, and real comic beats. The ending lands on the ridge. The long epilogue and the island stretch are the only drag, and players forgive both.

**M: 9.** The bickering lead and friend pair is the heart of it: tested in the pillory, mended, rescued, and given a last talk about the wedding. It is funny in a crude, warm way, and the revenge question is settled by the vision of the parents, which weighs what the player did. Some players will mark down the twenty-minute servant stretch and the throw-outs, but they love these people.

**B: 8.5.** Three leads who are each somebody, with loud, broad satire and plenty of humour. The betrayal at the grave gives the trio a real wound, and the third ending pays off the friendship. Players will grumble that the heist cycle repeats and that the agent work is forced, but the characters carry it.

**R: 8.** A lead with a clear double want, and a cousin whose warmth and debts drive the plot. Facing the traitor is a strong reckoning, and the wedding tragedy in either branch hits hard. The chain of employers in the middle reads as errands and sags, which will cost it with some players.

**T: 8.** Torn loyalty is a hook players grasp at once. The friend's arc and death, the wedding massacre, and the restaurant scene are memorable. The middle is a repeating turf-war pattern, and the superintendent's comeuppance is tidy rather than moving.

**W: 7.** The war secret is a genuinely surprising turn, and the homicide cover-up is grave. But the lead is stiff and hard to love, the affair comes from nowhere, and control is taken from him late, which players resent. The ending kills him off and hands the eulogy to his betrayer.

**H: 7.** The bond with the doorman is lovely and fully earned. It reverses, and the doorman's line "You'll have to do the door, boss" is the kind of moment players retell. It has warmth, humour and choices that come back. But the stakes are small and the plot is very intricate, so many players will find it slow and hard to follow. It is niche rather than broad.

**P: 6.5.** The couple's bond is the selling point, and the closeness meter backs it. But the page shows long fixed stretches: "the player cannot change" the turn, and a run of missions spent apart. The cast is thin, many characters are names with no scenes, and the 60-hour middle repeats job, escape, celebration.

**F: 6.** A near-silent lead, with targets who are props. The plot runs in the background between contracts. The handler bond and the twists help, and the disguise comedy is fun. Most players remember the sandboxes, not the story.

**N: 4.5.** There is no lead by design, which is players' top complaint: nobody at the centre. The organiser twist is decent and the program has charm. But the story scenes play "the same whoever plays them", and the forced stretches at both ends will grate.
