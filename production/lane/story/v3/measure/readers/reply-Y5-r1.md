## A. Expected player marks

**B: 8.2.** Three leads, each with a clear want, and a lot of loud, broad comedy, which players reward rather than punish. The robber–partner grudge and the reveal at the grave give the story a spine, and the third ending pays off all three men. The heist choices change nothing in the story, the agents force much of the work, and the middle is a visible cycle, so it stops short of the top.

**N: 4.5.** This page shows the players' largest complaint: no one at the centre. The recruit is interchangeable, "story scenes are the same whoever plays them", and the most vivid people (the organiser, the program) are voices in an earpiece. The organiser's betrayal has some force, but players will find the story forgettable despite the gravity of the organ-theft and house-assistant scenes.

**H: 7.4.** The page is full of people a player would miss: the master's "To nowhere, please. One hour", the doorman's silent re-rigging of the net, and the motorman calling turns as he dies. The ending lands on what the lead has become ("It's owed. Not to me"). But it is quiet, ledger-driven and dense, and the nightly fare cycle across 42 nights risks feeling padded. The lead's want is also less vivid than the cast around him.

**R: 8.4.** The cousin bond is the heart of the story, and the lead's hunt for the traitor ends in a scene that deliberately denies relief. The deal-or-revenge split makes the wedding loss land on someone the player cares about. The middle is a chain of employers handing the lead on, which players do mark down, and the lighthouse finale is a generic chase.

**F: 6.0.** The lead is nearly wordless and the plot moves "in the background" between self-contained sandbox contracts, so for most players the story is wallpaper. The handler bond and the train finale give it a late lift, as does the self-injection ending. The comedy comes from disguise play rather than from characters.

**P: 6.5.** The couple's bond is a real centre, and "what will they do to protect each other" is the kind of premise players respond to. However, the middle repeats "a job, an escape, a celebration", and the page says many turns are fixed. The ending is decided by hidden meters and then resets to before the final mission, which players tend to find unsatisfying. The supporting cast is thinly drawn on the page.

**K: 9.0.** It has a lead who is somebody and who changes: the diagnosis turns him from enforcer to someone trying to get others out. The gang's decay under the leader is felt across the whole story, and the ending on the ridge or at the cave lands hard. The stranger strands (the inventor's bird, the one-armed veteran) are the kind of side stories players retell. The island stretch and the long epilogue are the obvious drags.

**T: 8.0.** The undercover premise creates constant pressure between two loyalties. The friend's arc ends in a brutal, well-prepared death, and the wedding massacre and the restaurant scene hit hard. The middle is a repeating turf-war loop, and the dating thread is shallow, but the lead's anger and the betrayal by the superintendent carry it.

**M: 8.6.** The friend bond is the engine: bickering and crude jokes, a falling-out in the pillory, a rescue, and a possible romance at the end. The lead has three concrete wants, and the vision of his parents weighs his deeds into a judgement. There is plenty of humour, both in the main story and in the side quests. The roughly twenty-minute servant task, with ejection for mistakes, is a forced stretch players will grumble about.

**W: 7.2.** The war secret is a strong, dark turn, and the false charges in the homicide arc hit hard. But the lead is stiff and hard to love, the case structure is openly repetitive, and players feel the switch to the investigator as the hero being taken from them. Drowning the lead and leaving the eulogy to his betrayer is bold, but players will split on whether it lands.
