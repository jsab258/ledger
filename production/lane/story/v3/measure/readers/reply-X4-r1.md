## A. Expected player marks

**T: 8.6.** The undercover cop's split loyalty gives players a lead who is somebody. The boss, the boss's mother and the doomed childhood friend are people they would miss. The wedding massacre, the restaurant revenge and the friend's death hit hard, and the last-scene payback on the superintendent is satisfying. Players will forgive the repetitive turf-war middle because they care about the people in it.

**B: 8.2.** Three distinct, loud, funny leads with real wants, a family sitcom and a satire. Players love this kind of mix. The heist cycle repeats, the heist choices change nothing in the story, and much of the work is forced by the agents. Still, the three-way ending, which lets players keep everyone alive, lands well.

**R: 8.4.** A bitter, sympathetic immigrant lead and a warm, hopeless cousin make a central bond players remember. The traitor confrontation and the wedding tragedy are strong reckonings. The long chain of employers sags, and the sense of being passed from boss to boss is a classic complaint. The bleak, choice-driven ending sticks with players.

**K: 9.4.** The fullest character page in the set. The lead's want, decline and redemption under a death sentence are clear, and the gang's slow collapse around a leader the lead loves gives players many people to miss. The side strands are memorable, and the redemptive death at dawn is the strongest landing here. The island stretch and the long epilogue are minor drags.

**H: 8.0.** On the page the people are vivid and specific: the doorman, the old mourner's marks out of ten, the bookkeeper's four words. The bond with the doorman is built up, tested and ended with a prepared "would you carry him." There is real humour, and the ending reads back the player's whole record. Against that, the weekly funeral loop is quiet, slow and repetitive. Some players will find a stretch of carrying coffins and stamping cards too still.

**F: 6.3.** The lead is deliberately blank, and the plot is background between self-contained contracts. Players come for the sandbox, not the story. The handler bond and the train finale help, but most targets are strangers, and the middle could be reordered without loss.

**N: 5.0.** "No fixed lead" is exactly the "nobody at the centre" complaint. Recruits are interchangeable, and the organiser-as-villain turn has little emotional weight because no bond was built. The mother-in-the-assistant scene is strong but isolated. The ending is flat and imposed on the player.

**P: 7.4.** The couple's bond is the heart of the page, and that is what players prize. Their ordinary want is relatable. However, the page admits long stretches the player cannot change, a run in which the two are kept apart, a repeating job-escape-celebrate middle, and a late villain who arrives from nowhere. Players will love the pair more than the plot.

**M: 9.0.** The lead has a clear revenge want and is tested on whether he still deserves his parents. The bickering bond with the friend is funny and goes through rupture and mending. There are crude jokes, a surprise half-brother, and a siege climax that pays off the axe. The long servant sequence on rails is the one sore spot.

**W: 7.2.** The detective's rise and fall and the hidden war crime make a strong turn. Players, however, resent charging innocent men with no recourse, the repeating case loop, and the late switch away from the lead to another man. The lead's death and a funeral eulogy from his betrayer is a bitter ending players argue about.
