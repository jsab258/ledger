## A. Expected player marks

**R: 8.6.** The lead has a clear want: a clean start and the traitor. He also has a warm, boastful cousin whose bond runs through every act and is paid off with a death in either branch. The traitor reckoning and the wedding shooting are moments players will retell. The long employer-chain middle, three quarters of the story, will draw "padded" complaints, but players forgive it because the people in it are vivid: the car thief, the arms dealer, the filmmaker.

**F: 6.0.** The handler bond is real, and the betrayal-that-was-a-ruse ending pays it off, with a good last line. But the lead is near wordless and has no past. Four fifths of the story is a chain of self-contained contracts whose targets matter little, and the circle's partners are faceless. Players will enjoy the disguises and the play more than the story, and the cliffhanger second ending will annoy some.

**T: 8.3.** The lead's want and divided loyalty are clear. The bonds with the district boss, the boss's mother and the childhood friend are concrete and pay off hard: the wedding massacre, the cooked-gunman revenge, the friend's death. The villains are distinct, there are funny beats, and the superintendent twist lands. It loses a little for the repetitive turf-war middle and the throwaway dates.

**H: 7.0.** The doorman bond is the whole story, and it is the kind players fall for: shown in many scenes, inverted in the last act, settled in every ending. The driving lessons supply real comedy, and choices visibly come back. Against that, it is quiet, low-stakes and dense with counts and systems. Many players will find it slow or opaque, and some will find it forgettable for lack of action and a central threat.

**N: 4.2.** No fixed lead is the single biggest thing players punish, and here it is the design. The program and the organiser carry the cast, but the recruits are interchangeable and the scenes "are the same whoever plays them." The organiser twist surprises, but there is no lead for it to land on. The district cycle repeats, and the ending is a press release plus a drone.

**M: 9.0.** The lead has three concrete wants and changes visibly. The bickering, bawdy friendship with the young nobleman is exactly the "people I'd miss" players praise most. There are strong side stories with turns, real comedy, a foster-brother reveal, and an ending that weighs what the player did. The forced twenty-minute servant stretch and the slow opening will draw complaints, but they won't dent a cast this loved.

**B: 8.1.** Three leads with distinct wants, a betrayal turn on the robber's faked death, and a big slate of comedy. The planner and the family scenes give players people to enjoy. It is broad and loud, which players don't mark down. They will mark down the repeating heist cycle, forced agent work, a choice that changes nothing, and two of three endings that feel like betrayals of the cast.

**K: 9.4.** The strongest case on the page for "characters worth caring about." The lead's want shifts under a terminal diagnosis, and the leader's slow collapse turns a father-son bond into tragedy. The camp is full of distinct people, the stranger strands have turns (the widow, the inventor's bird), and the ending is a death that follows from who he has become. The long epilogue and the island detour are the only real drags.

**P: 6.4.** The couple's bond is clear, and players will care about it, with some good comic scenes. But the page repeatedly says things are fixed: arrests, fates, a long forced-apart stretch, a rescued woman taken "whatever the player does." The supporting cast is a list of employers, and the late smuggler and senator villains arrive without build-up. The endings are bleak and swing on opaque systems.

**W: 6.7.** The war-crime revelation and the forced false charges are strong, grave material. But the lead is stiff and hard to warm to, and the affair that wrecks him comes from almost nowhere on the page. In the last act control passes to another man, and the lead dies off-centre. Players typically call this kind of thing a betrayal of the protagonist, and the case-procedure middle repeats.
