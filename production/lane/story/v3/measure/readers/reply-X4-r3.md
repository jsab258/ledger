## A. Expected player marks

**K: 9.5.** The lead has a clear want, changes, and the change is paid for: the diagnosis turns him from enforcer to someone getting others out. The page is crowded with people players will miss: the leader whose decline is the spine, the younger fellow, the widow, the principled gunman. The stranger strands (the fishing widow, the clockwork bird) give players moments they retell, and the cliff ending lands on the central bond. The long epilogue and the island stretch are the only drags, and players forgive both when they love the cast this much.

**M: 9.0.** The friendship between the commoner lead and the hot-headed nobleman runs through pillory, prison, ransom, rescue and a last talk about the wedding. It is exactly the bond players praise most, and it carries crude, concrete comedy (the goats in the herb beds, the hatchet and the ear). The ending weighs the lead's actual deeds and settles his revenge. The twenty-minute servant stretch where mistakes throw him out is a rails complaint waiting to happen, and the axe and parents thread is tidy more than surprising.

**R: 8.5.** A lead with a dry voice, a buried want (the traitor) and a warm, boastful cousin whose lies and debts drive the plot. The traitor confrontation is a strong anti-climax that players respect, and both wedding-day endings hit hard because each kills someone the player knows. The middle is plainly a chain of employers, and the page shows it, which costs it against K and M.

**T: 8.0.** The divided-loyalty premise gives the lead a real want and a real cost. The district boss, his mother and the friend are distinct, and their losses (the wedding massacre, the friend on the scaffolding) land and change what he does. Some of the comedy is broad and good (the roosters, saying the plan out loud). The cast thins after the turn, the superintendent payoff happens off his hands, and the ending is neat rather than moving.

**B: 7.8.** Three loud, distinct leads, broad satire, plenty of comedy and set-pieces players love. The robber and partner grudge gives it a backbone, and the third ending pays off the friendship. But the heist cycle repeats, the heist choices change nothing in the story, and two of the three endings undo the bond players built.

**H: 7.4.** It is beautifully built: the doorman is a character players would miss, his death is prepared and lands, and the endings follow from how the player treated each funeral. But it is small, grave and quiet. The middle is a weekly loop, humour is sparse (the coffin like a wardrobe, the sideboard drill), and the lead is withdrawn by design. Open-world players reward this less than writers would.

**W: 7.0.** The homicide cover-up and the war revelation hit hard. But the lead is stiff and hard to love, the affair comes from little on the page, and the last act hands play to another man. The lead then dies off-centre, and outcomes ignore the player's work. Those are the "rushed ending" and "no one at the centre" complaints players voice.

**P: 6.8.** The couple's bond and the closeness meter give players someone to hold. But the page shows few scenes of the two together that change them. The supporting cast is mostly labels, the middle is a repeating job, escape, celebrate cycle, and much is stated to be fixed. The bridge ending may land, but the road there feels padded at about 60 hours.

**F: 6.0.** The disguise play and comedy (the mascot fox, the party clown) are memorable, and the handler bond gets a genuine poisoning-ruse twist. But the lead is a near-blank by design. The plot runs in short background scenes between contracts, and the conspiracy cast is faceless old money. Players praise these missions far more than this story.

**N: 4.5.** There is no lead and no one at the centre, which is the largest complaint players make. Story scenes are identical whoever speaks them. The organiser twist is undercut because the player never had a bond with her, and the deputy thread is left hanging. Only the house-assistant mother scene and the program's banter will stick.
