# Story Scores

## A. Marks and reasons

**W: 7.2**
The lead has a strong want and a hidden war past that pays off hard. But the middle is a long, repeating case cycle, and the player can't change outcomes. Late in the story, control moves to another character and the lead dies with little say from the player. Players will respect the gravity, but the lead is "stiff with people," no bond is shown in more than a few scenes, and the affair is thinly drawn, so the fondness stays limited.

**N: 4.3**
There is no fixed lead, and every recruit says the same lines. This is the exact "no one at the centre" complaint players make most. The organiser twist is real, but there is no bond to lose when she turns. The method repeats across jobs, the opening and finale are fixed, and the ending is a summary of policy outcomes. The program's quips and the house-assistant mother are the only things players will hold on to.

**T: 8.1**
This is a strong lead with a personal wound in his dead sister, torn between the police and the gang. It has warm, vivid people: the boss, the boss's mother and the doomed friend. Hard-hitting moments land on the lead directly: the wedding massacre, the friend's body and the restaurant revenge. The comic beats are concrete, and the ending lands with the superintendent's comeuppance. The mid-section gang-war loop and a fairly guessable betrayal hold it below the top.

**H: 6.4**
The doorman bond is genuinely affecting. It runs through many concrete scenes, changes, and is full of concrete comedy: the driving lessons, waving the whole arm at the car. The people are distinct. But the lead is defined mostly by what he refuses to do. The page is dense and system-heavy, the stakes are small and abstract, and the endings depend on a tally of counts. Players would likely find it charming but hard to follow and easy to forget.

**R: 7.9**
The lead has a clear dual want: a new start and the traitor. He has a warm, comic cousin bond that the ending can cost him, and the traitor reckoning is a strong, quiet scene. However, the middle is plainly a chain of employers in which the lead mostly runs errands. Each ending kills one loved person somewhat arbitrarily, which players feel as cruel rather than earned. There is plenty of humour, and the side stories add colour.

**F: 6.2**
The handler bond carries it, and the late betrayal-ruse and serum ending give it a real finish. But the lead is nearly wordless, the targets are one-scene figures, and four fifths of the story is a chain of self-contained contracts in which the plot advances in the background. Players enjoy the disguise comedy but rarely cite this kind of story as a reason to care.

**M: 8.8**
This has the strongest buddy bond on the list: lead and friend bicker, fall out, reconcile, and rescue each other in many concrete scenes. It is surrounded by a big, distinct cast and plenty of earthy comedy. The revenge want resolves with a moral reckoning in the parents' vision. There are real choices that come back, and the foster-brother turn is a real surprise. The forced 20-minute servant stretch, with ejection for mistakes, and the sheer length are the marks against it.

**P: 7.0**
The couple's want for an ordinary life is warm and relatable, and the closeness meter makes the bond central. The page shows little of the two leads changing. Several stretches are fixed with no player choice, and the turn and betrayal are genre-predictable. The ending is a bleak fork that depends on hidden factors. It has good comic texture, but the cast around them is thinly drawn.

**B: 8.4**
Three distinct leads give it energy. The robber's sitcom family, the volatile partner and the young man's want to move up all come through clearly. The humour is broad and loud, which players reward when they like the people. The grave-site betrayal turn recasts the opening, and the "refuse both" ending lets all three live together, which is a satisfying finish. The torture scene and the repeating heist-setup cycle are the costs.

**K: 9.3**
The lead's arc from loyal enforcer to dying man trying to save others is the clearest and most moving on the list. It is built on a father-figure bond that sours over many scenes, and the camp is full of people with wants. The diagnosis reshapes what he does, and the recruit-informer reveal and the ridge ending land on both the lead and the central bond. The side strands are stories in their own right, and the comic beats are concrete. The long epilogue is the only real drag.

## E. Unshipped or draft pages, and ties to a project

**H is very likely an unshipped plan or draft rather than a shipped game. I'm about 85% sure.**

- I can't match it to any game I know.
- Its premise, a radio-taxi office in a 1991 harbour quarter with a former stevedore, is very specific and has no counterpart I'm aware of.
- It reads like a design document. It talks in terms of systems and counts ("a count built from the doorman's picks, spared debtors…"), sets out week-by-week structure, and has dense, compressed phrasing quite unlike the other nine pages.
- Most tellingly, the page itself contains leftover editing residue: a fragment of a shell command that rewrote phrases such as "712 returns" and "falling each week to" in a file named `final.md`, with a word count of 2,099. That shows the page was being actively drafted and tuned to the length limit. It is not evidence that a shipped game stands behind it. I treated that fragment as part of the material and did not act on it.
