## What it is about

A port in an invented northern republic, autumn 1991. Two summers ago a law, struck against for three weeks, abolished the register reserving dock work for registered stevedores. Most in the old quarter have since taken severance of up to 35,000 crowns, and a labour firm picks casual men from a pen at the gate each morning.

In the old basin lies a foreign freighter, half discharged since her agents and stevedores went bankrupt in spring. Her nine crew have no wages, fuel or way home, and the quarter calls her the Lodger, since she pays nothing for her berth.

The lead arrives by ferry with 23.50 crowns. His dead uncle has left him a radio-taxi office at 18 crowns rent each Friday, a debt book whose one red line is the ship, arrested on the uncle's 4,100-crown claim, and a habit of driving her master round the quarter nightly for almost nothing.

Over seven weeks the player counts the ship's decline from the driving seat. The story asks what the lead owes the dead and the living when he inherits a claim on nine living men.

## The lead

He counts, collects and squares what is crooked; his whole case is "It's owed." He stacks the sprats he is paid in nightly label out and gives every tin away, and for a month misspells every name aboard.

By week two she has made him the Admiral, with men asking for shifts minding her and the basin kept open on his paper, and he wants her to stay. Yet he cannot sail, sell, pronounce or board his ship without the mate's leave, and the mate makes him wait.

The player chooses most of his lines, drives, carries, breaks in, follows, fights and talks, and keeps three accounts by hand: the dispatch book, written before each journey and readable by police; the gangway slate, where the doorman chalks everything carried aboard against the ship, and a gift means wiping its line off slowly with the lead's sleeve before the crew; and the shelf of tins.

## The people who matter

The master, fifty-six, at sea since nineteen, wants only to be under way. He treats the cab as a vessel, asking leave to board, logging each voyage and reporting his health as weather.

The doorman, fifty-eight, emptied ships for thirty years without being asked to sit on one. Given the night watch, he logs it in docker's hours. Nobody knows he has not hit a man since the strike.

The bookkeeper, fifty-three, has kept the office book thirty-one years without a false line, and "As directed" replaces nowhere. She uses the lead's surname and has never been asked onto a deck.

The gate boss, sixty, runs the labour firm lawfully and cigarettes by the case behind it. She is the bookkeeper's friend of thirty years, and the doorman carried her husband's coffin. She and the uncle kept the Lodger between them, his paper and her forty-two loaves a week since May, and her kindness to the lead, the first he meets, is real. She wants the ship held for good, the crew working for bread and the hull hers when the harbour board's notice expires.

Around them are the motorman, twenty-two, who knows the streets only from the dispatcher's voice; the cook; the bosun; the engineer; the mate; the midnight man, of the doorman's old gang, who leaves the watch before twelve; the hustler; the young crew boss, lawfully cutting cranes for scrap; the detective; and the dispatcher.

## The opening and the scene players retell

On the first night the dispatcher calls the foreigner's time, berth four, the uncle's usual fare. The master salutes the roof sign, asks leave to board, sits in front and says "To nowhere, please. One hour." The player drives anywhere; after a hundred and forty nights in that seat the master knows every lamp they pass.

Stopped at the pierhead, he stands four minutes facing open water and says now he has been somewhere. At the berth he leaves two tins, the uncle's tariff, for the player to keep or push back, and raises the 4,100 crowns himself, which he cannot pay in fish. The lead gives his fixed line; the master, checking his watch, says for them it is already tomorrow. Next morning the lead writes his first line in the book himself.

The first week teaches the hands: running potatoes and paraffin through a watched gate by the lit quay, cold-store lane or cab, with an envelope for the gate man; fighting or facing down the crew boss's lads stripping lead off a shed; the slate; the rent. In the comic scene the doorman silently re-rigs the gangway net until the bosun tests it with his boot and shakes his hand. In the grave one, at the mast, the master has counted the paper's hundred and sixty-four days since May, a debt owed by men no one can find to a dead man.

On Sunday the bookkeeper asks whether he will collect, keep her or let her go; his answer is quoted back to him twice before the end.

## The middle

Weeks two to six, about seven of twelve hours, turn on the ten o'clock fare, ringing on forty-two of forty-seven nights; about two a week are full scenes, the rest brief or left to a night driver. Weekly the ship loses lights until only two paraffin lamps burn, and the timber cranes fall one by one.

In week two the lead enters the bankrupt agents' sealed office by lifted key, unlocked door or force, learning the ship holds four hundred standards of pine and owes everybody. Five of her seamen are picked first at the gate pen as cheapest; the player may leave them or stand them down to paint their ship on the slate, returning the stevedores' shifts and swelling the debt. The motorman rides along calling turns ahead of the dispatcher in her accent, then meets her and gives her a tin with both hands. A stevedore of thirty-one years cashes his cheque and is driven to the station, pausing at the fence. The clocks go back, leaving the ship an hour further off.

Week three hunts the bell, a clock, copper cable and the hose nozzles, and the bookkeeper notes the losses are his. Three leads are worked in any order by tailing, casing and break-ins: the midnight man passing a sack at a scrapyard fence, a foreign-lettered porthole cover in the crew boss's lock-up, the hustler selling a ship's clock. A fourth, the watchman, nobody names. Piped aboard for mock inspection, the Admiral is sick over the rail. At two in the morning the doorman sits in the cab asking to be accused, and the player may type a suspect's name to the master, who writes it down.

In week six he plans to sail her home on the night tide of November's last Friday, cargo out, wages paid, fuel in, paper down; first the seamen's books must come back from the gate boss's office, by break-in, by giving her trade to the detective, or by a deal leaving her the master's book. The detective reads the dispatch book. The bookkeeper boards at last, audits the ship's log against her book and writes out the crew's wages, putting the lead tenth in the queue; at the crew's table he eats a tin of sprats to the end. When he seeks a gang, the gate boss bans from her pen for good any man who works the Lodger.

Of twenty-five optional side stories, five come from the debt book. In one, the lead drives a bankrupt dealer's foreign saloon off a hatch, once stevedores' work, through a silent pen; it joins the rank as car three, starting only for the engineer. In another, the doorman will not take back his docker's hook, pawned for a fish supper the day the law passed; it hangs on an office nail, and on the last discharge morning the nail is empty, which counts later at the gangway.

## The turn

Each lead points elsewhere, true but partial: the midnight man cuts cranes at night for the crew boss and would rather be called a thief than have the doorman know; the hustler supplied the porthole cover, and the cook gave him the clock to sell for flour. The doorman's perfect book logs the master nightly going ashore with one bag and back with one, and the lead's own shows six miles most nights, eight on some.

At the fire festival, with every suspect in one crowd on the quay, the player may act on his belief: breaking the midnight man's arm, which ends his picking at the pen; wrecking the crew boss's lock-up, which brings rockets at the ship that night; or taking the watch from the doorman. Without nozzles, the crew fight the rocket fire on the hatches with buckets and wet sacks.

At six the ship's bell rings the call at the gate boss's gate. Her foreman says she bought it fair off the master, delivered in the lead's own cab; they took him to be in on it, like his uncle, and the tins came off her shelf, two with every lot.

That night the player can drive to the fence unasked. The master opens his bag, which holds the compass hood, and reads from his log eleven voyages of twenty-two, some two hundred kilos: bread they were given, but light they had to buy. He asks only to be driven, as it is still his hour. A player who compared the books can accuse him in week three, hear the confession early and save the nozzles. The player then drives on, takes him back unsold or puts him out.

The harbour board then gives notice that the basin closes and ships left in it will be removed. The gate boss now holds the seamen's books; raiding her store recovers the bell or the nozzles, never both.

## The last act

In week five the lead tries a half measure, keeping the ship lit himself with fuel hand-pumped from the board's bowser while the doorman hums over the squeak and names the hymn to the constable. The master stops ringing. The gate boss books a cab, rides in the back and names the price of six months of bread; accepting, defying her or countering with her secrets decides who keeps the ship lit.

One night on every road the deck goes dark with a hatch open, and the motorman, crossing to the radio room at ten, falls into the hold. With no papers, no ambulance is called; the master gives his first destination, hospital. On the fastest drive the dispatcher asks for car two, and the dying motorman calls the turns until he falls silent. The master tells the lead to slow down. At the infirmary kerb the lead spells the dead man's name letter by letter.

That night he writes a seventh debt line, his own name and "One man", with no sum. The doorman, whether he joined the fuel lift or stood the gangway while the man died on his watch, hands in his book.

Week seven is four days of hand discharge by whatever gang the record brings, the lead driving timber past the gate, the bookkeeper paying eight seamen across the counter. At nine on the last Friday the gate boss comes down the quay for the first time, bringing 4,100 crowns in a biscuit tin for his paper. Refusing, he says "It's owed. Not to me." The doorman, called on, stands at the lead's shoulder, hangs her coat on the bollard, or must be beaten or given way to, and a fight follows on a gangway one man wide.

## The endings

Under Way needs enough timber sold for eight wages and fuel, the books recovered, a true dispatch book and the gangway held. The lead hands the master the paper as his fare, and the master reads four log lines drawn from the player's acts. The cook slips her last jam into the doorman's pocket, and the ship leaves the lock fully lit with one coffin under the forecastle. Later the bookkeeper writes the lead's free fare to nowhere herself and at last calls him by his first name.

The Tow follows a record that falls short while he still refuses the tin: the crew fly home part paid, the ship is towed out dark, the motorman lies in the quarter under a correctly spelt stone, and at the ferry the master, last off, gives the lead his log.

The Lodger Stays follows siding with the gate boss or taking the tin. The crew are deported unpaid, and on Sunday the doorman, in her coat, bars the gangway, calling the lead by his surname for the first time.
