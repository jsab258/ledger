## What it is about

A port in an invented northern republic, autumn 1991. Two summers ago a law abolished the harbour labour register, which for forty years kept dock work for registered men, and a short strike failed. Most of the quarter's stevedores have since taken a severance cheque of up to 35,000 crowns, offered until early next year, and gone inland. Casual hiring is back, and thirty-year register men wait in a pen at the gate for a foreman's tap.

The lead, a stranger, comes off the ferry to take over his dead uncle's radio-taxi office and finds a burial club behind the counter: for thirty-seven years members have had a card stamped each Friday against a promise of a hearse, two cars, six bearers and a stone. The box holds 212 cards; the tin holds forty-one crowns. The story asks what a stranger owes a dead man's promise and the men who hold it.

The quarter empties in counts. The Friday queue falls from thirty-one to nineteen to eleven. Six bearers of a height take an afternoon to find in week one; by week five only eight men can lift. An old stevedore behind every coffin says how many walked, then gives a mark out of ten. One funeral a week for seven weeks is the spine, with driving throughout: infirmary fetches, corteges, fares and night jobs paying for stones.

## The lead

The lead wants to owe nobody. When a widow lays a card on his counter, his first words are "I'm not him." Seeing the book is a promise and the tin nearly empty, he wants the decent stranger's way out: find whoever emptied the fund, repay every man, shut the book and run cabs.

The player speaks for him freely; his fixed lines are few and flat. He returns a widow's fare in the same coins. He logs each funeral in the dispatch book, open by law to the police, as a free journey with the dead man's nickname as passenger, until the bookkeeper stops objecting. Being too tall, he wears a dead man's overcoat padded by the old neighbour until a coffin sits level on him. He unbolts the roof sign before every funeral. At the pen he will not tap a shoulder, and asks each man's name in front of the foreman.

He owns no black, so the quarter lends him a tie, a short-sleeved overcoat and small shoes; he rises in chapel half a second late. Each joke at his expense is a fault he mends with his hands, never twice.

If the player treats funerals as bills, the marks shrink to bare numbers, his notebook of willing bearers empties, and the doorman moves him to the back of the coffin.

## The people who matter

The doorman, fifty-eight, a stevedore until the register ended, now minds the door and the rank. He has carried sixty coffins, and at front left quietly tells each dead man where they are by what the buildings once were. A doctor has told him to sit down; he will not. He is making a bearer of the only material to hand.

The old mourner, seventy-four, founded the club and was its first collector; nobody knows his real name. At each graveside he says a mark out of ten to nobody, and gravediggers carry it downhill by teatime. He marks the funeral, never the man, has never given a ten and holds no card. Riding back in the lead's front seat he explains each mark, teaching the trade.

The bookkeeper, fifty-three, thirty-one years at the counter, speaks in four words and never says what is not so. She minds the telephone during every club funeral, never attending, then stamps the card buried, and watches what the heir does when a card looks short.

The crew boss, thirty, drove for the dead owner until sacked for fares kept out of the book, and now lives by a trade nobody names. His father, on oxygen at a window above the undertaker's parlour, turned up for forty years only when the work was done, and the quarter has not forgiven it. The son means to buy him a turnout: first the hearse, then the bearers, then the cards.

Around them are the priest, who counts the cars for each widow; the dead owner's daughter, a night nurse whose three o'clock calls mean a death; the gate boss, who gives night work and whose husband's funeral got the only nine; the undertaker, unpaid and selling up; the old neighbour, counting cars from her window; the detective; and the young hustler, an inch too short.

## The opening and the scene players retell

On Monday afternoon the lead walks in behind a widow who lays a soft card of purple squares on the counter. The radio has no driver, and the bookkeeper looks at him until he puts his suitcase down. He drives the widow by the dispatcher's landmarks, takes her fare in old and new coins of equal value, and next day returns them, exact or a crown over; her household reads him by it. He collects five small debts owed to his uncle, one ending in a fight on the quay, and by Thursday the street knows whether he squeezed.

That day the doorman, a bearer short, puts him on the back right corner. The player keeps step to the doorman's count and holds the box level, the lead's shoulder riding high. On the step the coffin tips and something inside knocks; six men stop, the doorman apologises to the dead man by name, and the coffin goes up the street like a wardrobe on a staircase. The lead drives the second car with the roof sign lit. At the dock gate the cortege stops a minute and the men in the pen turn round. On the hill the old mourner says, "Forty walked. Five." Then he gives the lead, last there, his reasons, two off for advertising at a graveside, and explains the scale on the drive down.

On Friday thirty-one men queue to have cards stamped; one has nothing in his hand, and the bookkeeper says, "Stamp it." That evening she counts the tin once before him, beside the 212 cards, the week's gravest moment. On Sunday she asks whether he will close the club, keep it, or say nothing.

## The middle

Weeks two to four, about 325 of the main line's 720 minutes, repeat a night call, a mortuary fetch, bearers to find, a carry, and Friday at the counter, rent paid with one hand and cards stamped with the other.

In week two the undertaker sells up, leaving his hearse half silver in a paint shed, and the crew boss offers it free, himself driving, his name printed. By night the doorman drills six men under the office sideboard, the old neighbour asking from her window if they are burying it. Taking the offer sends that household to the crew boss; otherwise the player can take the hearse by night and drive a walking route hiding its silver side, or shoulder the coffin uphill for the best mark.

In week three the regular bearers turn out in the crew boss's grey coats, and at dawn the doorman stops at the pen rail while the player works the gate alone. A widow brings a card unstamped for fifteen months; the lead buries her husband, or he goes up at nine off the council's van with only the old mourner behind. Two sets of bearers fight in whispers over one coffin on the chapel step, straightening whenever the widow turns.

The club's book shows sums paid out under two letters that fit three people: the priest, who keeps the tin and has a new roof; the retiring undertaker; and the old mourner, whose hand starts the column. Nothing prompts an accusation, but break-ins and public questions are open, each costing a congregation, the undertaker, or the old man's explanations.

Among twenty-five optional stories, the mortuary hands over the wrong body; the lead swaps the coffins back by night, finds the spare man has nobody, and may bury him too. In another, the player forces the power house and holds down the lever to sound the dock hooter for a widow's husband; the foreman sent to stop it takes off his cap, and the hooter sounds for club funerals thereafter.

## The turn

On the Friday of week four sixteen men queue and none has money, because casuals worked the ship. The lead stamps every card at the bookkeeper's word, and she turns the other book round. The two letters mean black weeks: a week when a man is not picked, or is sick, is stamped all the same, and always was. There was never a fund, only his uncle, then the doorman, now the stamp in the lead's hand; nothing was taken, so nothing can be recovered.

The turn lands on what the player did. The priest brings a list of fees waived since the strike, the crew boss the undertaker's unpaid book for forty-one funerals. The turned-away widow's card comes from behind the divider, and fifteen months are stamped, loudly. A wrong is mended only by a deed, such as carrying the tin back.

That week a man who left with his cheque comes home off the ferry in a box, eleven walking behind. The crew boss rehearses his father's funeral with an empty coffin while the old man calls through the glass that it is too fast. On Saturday night the lead earns a stone with a tobacco run for the gate boss, or lifts one from the mason. At two in the morning, engine off, the doorman says the doctor gives him six weeks unless he sits down, which he will not, and once only asks whether the lead would carry him. Only a plain yes counts.

## The last act

In week five three members die in three nights, one buried at dawn by the cab's headlamps, and the crew boss will send no hearse unless given the box. On Thursday the doorman leaves his cap on the lead's shoulder and goes to talk the crew boss round. With no menu, the player stays with the coffin, goes with him, or sends him back. Staying, the lead calls the count at front left, and when the young hustler appears cap in hand at the cemetery gate, the player must first pay the strap out to the bottom. On every road the doorman dies that day: on the parlour step, after a fight in the yard, on a wall after carrying a man short, or alone fetching back the box given away. Asked by the detective for next of kin, the lead names the firm.

In week six the player walks the quarter for six bearers and pencils them by height on the doorframe; the bookkeeper takes the lowest mark herself. The player can give up, beat, kill or leave the man who pushed the doorman, and may climb to the crew boss's father, who saw it. The doorman's card comes back naming six bearers for his own coffin, the lead first: "Mind his height." The crew boss's father dies.

In the last week the lead tells the crew boss to leave his father's card, and on Thursday he rides out at dawn to watch. The bookkeeper locks the ringing office, and the lead carries the doorman up the hill. At noon the silver hearse arrives with four hired men in step, and the crew boss stands holding the card: "He's paid up." The lead may take the coffin, stand still while the quarter turns its back, or strike him.

## The endings

No screen offers an ending. The record is read twice: on Thursday in which households come out for the doorman, those whose funerals were marked seven or better or put right in person, and on Friday in whether men bring their cards. The father's card sits between the readings.

If the quarter came, the bearers knew the doorman and the father was carried, the old mourner stands at the hill gate and says nothing, which is his ten. On Friday eleven men queue, some bringing back cards given to the crew boss, and the bookkeeper writes and stamps a card in the lead's name. Last in line, the old mourner asks for a card under his nickname and gets into the front seat. The doorman and the dock stay lost, and nobody stands behind the lead.

If the turnout was partial, or the lead let the father go up through turned backs, the club closes and the old mourner gives the doorman a seven. The lead returns each card door to door with the union's slip, and some doors shut on him; the bookkeeper gives notice. The last image is the empty box and the lead at the doorman's wooden marker, writing that one stone is owing.

If the quarter stayed in, the box was given away, or there was a fight at the gate, the old mourner will not mark it. Nobody queues, and the stamp goes into the bookkeeper's handbag. Weeks later a silver crew funeral leaves the street with the lead driving the second car, sign unbolted, the doorman's card beside him; it stops a minute at the dock gate, though nobody paid for that.
