# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 6: a player's review

I read this as if I were going to buy it. This is what I would feel with the pad in my hands.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8.

1. Tom: 8. A silent man who will not take a cup of tea for free, a packed suitcase under the bed, and a page headed "Asked" is a lead I could play, and the change from never asking to asking Sheila is real. He is still mostly defined by other people's lines about him, which is right for the frame but keeps him a notch below Arthur.

2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Ada, Pauline and Terry are people I would talk about afterwards; Ron in the passenger seat naming buildings by what they used to be is the best thing here. Too many of the rest are one line deep, and I would lose track of who Winifred, Albert, Edith and Shaun are.

3. The antagonists: 6. The Widow is interesting and I can see her point, but she never lies, never threatens and keeps every deal, so I never fear her; Agar is mostly letters and a speech; Danny is the only one who actually hurts anybody, once.

4. The central mystery: 8. A truth found with a mileometer and a stuck seat, that changes what "Every fare goes in the book" and "New management" meant, and pays off with her driving it for an officer: that is a proper mystery. It gives itself away too early in one scene (see below).

5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The carry at minute nine with a constable who can turn round, Ada's alley line the next morning, and the bollard in lesson one are strong. The first week is heavy with sums (£23.50, £156, £38, £22, £19) and a lot of people saying one line each, and I would feel I was being briefed.

6. Structure and pace: 7. Three acts with a midpoint on Bonfire Night and a clock (Agar's thirty-first, Reese's Friday) is sound. Act III crams two weeks of letters, a hearing-like inspection, a kitchen, a phone box, a gate and a drive into two and a half hours, and almost all of it is talk.

7. The middle: 7. Every week has its own fare, job and finding, which is better than most open worlds manage, and the Empty Seat is the emotional core. But the investigation part of it is reading books side by side at a counter, and I have seen that make players put the pad down.

8. Surprise against cliché: 7. Ron not being bought, and the car going and coming back, surprised me. The shape underneath is familiar: dying uncle's inheritance, landlord who wants the yard by a deadline, and "the quiet one who never lies did it".

9. The endings: 8. Every ending has a last image I would remember: "Ron drove", "Nowak!" on the quay, Sheila in the back of the cab paying £2. What decides them is partly hidden measures the player never sees, and the best one hangs on a single unprompted press.

10. The side stories: 7. The watch and Ivy Leadley, mother and daughter each protecting the other from a thing both know, are whole stories that touch the main one. The ferry clerk is a sketch, and four grown stories is thin for a twenty-hour game.

11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline on the radio, Terry's fifth word, Kevin's knees and the crabs would get a laugh out of me. Most of the humour is lines rather than set pieces, and the lessons are the only real comic set piece.

12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The handbrake, the dial, the seat, the take-or-pay press and Ron in whichever seat as the verdict are story told through the controls, which is rare. Typed answers and endings driven by unseen meters pull the other way.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. Act III is a talking act in a game that says it is not one.
What is wrong: Agar's speech in the rain, Reese's speeches at the counter and on Wednesday, Sheila's kitchen, the Widow at the gate, and Ellis's long verdict on Friday evening are all people explaining the plot to a silent man. The frame says few short scenes and no long cutscenes.
Where: section 8, the week of the letter and the week of the inspector; the Ellis paragraph after Friday.
Why I would feel it: after nine hours of driving and carrying, the climax turns into standing at a counter being told things. Shiner's confession in scene 13 and the Widow volunteering the watchman in scene 16 have the same problem earlier.
Fix: cut every speech to two lines and move the rest into things done. Agar's confession is the hat left on the seat and one sentence. Reese's terms go on a single page he hands over. Ellis's decision is shown at the counter on Saturday with the charge sheets, not said.

2. Scene 10 gives the answer away four hours early.
What is wrong: in one scene the player gets the seat jammed forward for a year, the repair struck off twice "with the initials S.D.", Sheila going on writing, the old books behind her chair, and June saying Sheila learned to drive and never drove again. Anyone who has played a mystery game knows it is her by the end of that scene.
Where: section 7, second movement, scene 10.
Why I would feel it: the bible counts on the player believing it was Ron until scene 15. I would not. Scene 15, which should be the best scene in the game, would land as confirmation, not a turn.
Fix: strike the repair in plain ink with no initials, so the hand is only recognised in the kitchen when the 1989 book is on the oilcloth. Move June's line about who never drove again to after scene 15. Let scene 10 point at Ron: the coat with no sleeves and the tally (a docker's) are enough.

3. The main opponent is too decent to fear.
What is wrong: the Widow's rule is that she never breaks a deal, never lies, never holds anything over anyone. Her one menace is standing in a road. Her single cruelty, never picking Frank Cammack because he once talked to a customs man, is only explained on the last Thursday.
Where: section 5, the Widow; scenes 2, 5, 14 and 16; the Thursday gate scene.
Why I would feel it: the GTA V complaint, antagonists thinner than the leads. I would admire her and never once feel pressure from her, and Agar would feel like paperwork.
Fix: let the cost of crossing her land on a named person mid-game where the player sees it: when Tom refuses her, Julie or Terry loses the cold-store shift and says so on the radio. Show Frank's exclusion as her doing at the gate in week two, so the player knows what she is capable of. Give Agar one face-to-face scene before December.

4. The best ending hangs on a press the game never suggests, and on meters the player cannot see.
What is wrong: the better Kingdom ending needs Tom to open the "Asked" page in Sheila's kitchen, which nothing prompts. Endings also turn on things like whether "the street does not hold his rackets as fact". And the Widow's offer is priced on an answer the player types in his own words.
Where: section 3, the question; scene 2; section 8, Wednesday night; section 9.
Why I would feel it: most players will miss the best ending and never know why, and a story that feels like it was decided behind my back is the thing BAR says gets punished. Free typed text in a game also rarely works; the game will either ignore it or misread it.
Fix: give picked answers, not typed ones. Show the "Asked" page with names and blanks, so the player can see who he has never asked. Have Ron or Ada say it once, plainly, before the kitchen: "You've never asked her, have you." Show the street's verdict on Tom somewhere visible, through Pauline or the Tangs' queue.

5. Too many people and too many branches for the hours.
What is wrong: twenty-one who matter plus six regulars plus a crowd of named one-liners; two men of sixty with ruined hands (Frank Cammack in the line, the Bishop in gloves) who I would mix up; and a whole alternative middle movement if Ron never left.
Where: section 4; scenes 2, 9 and 11; "If Ron never left" in section 7; section 11's road.
Why I would feel it: in sixteen and a half hours I would not keep most of these straight, and the alternative branches will get less care than the main path and feel thinner.
Fix: cut or merge the one-line people (Darren and Keith could share a job), give the Bishop and Frank clearly different marks, and make the never-left branch one strong scene with its own peak rather than three.

## 3. Would I single this story out to a friend?

No, not yet, but it is close. I would tell a friend there is a game where your best mate rides in the passenger seat, you teach him to drive on Sunday mornings, and the mystery is solved with a mileometer, and they would want to hear more. But I would have to warn them about a last act of talk at a counter, an opponent who never comes for you, and a twist they would guess at hour eight. Fix those and it is a yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron in the passenger seat, the three lessons and the handbrake. "I go, you stop", the empty seat after the Fifth, and the handbrake left alone at the bollard as the last input of the game is the Henry and Hans of this game.

2. The seat and the eleven miles. A stuck seat that is a joke for nine hours, a tally in the runner, and a player driving the route twice to find it is seven, all done with a steering wheel and a dial. That is the mystery told through play and it is the most original thing here.

3. Sheila driving it herself. Her four sentences, "To have it read", the drive to the bollard with an officer writing, and Ron saying "You come back, though" after a year of good mornings. That is the ending that is the sum of the people, and I would fight for it.
