# LEDGER's story: a critic's reading

Read on 20 November 2026: STORY-TREATMENT.md in full, against the praise and punish tables in design/story/05-players-basics.md (KCD2's figures only). The judgement is on the story as the treatment holds it today, not as it might become. Where something is named but not among the sources (the 32 side-story drafts, Mickey's two lines, the fire's draft), it is marked on what the treatment shows.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 4 out of 10.

**Characters worth caring about: 4.** The first week brings in more promising people per minute than most games manage in an hour: Sheila, Ron, Ada and Darren each get a sharp line and a want of their own. Then the treatment lets them go. Darren "has no story of his own" after week one, Ada's ends with the tea, June and Father Walsh have none, and Philip is a name; KCD2 earns the 32.2% of its reviews that praise story and characters by changing its people over forty hours, and here almost nobody is written past Thursday.

**Tom as a lead (want, fear, change): 2.** Tom has no want, no fear, no flaw and no change; the treatment says so itself, and every ending describes where he stands, not who he is. A player typing his words is a bold idea, but a silent lead only works when the story hands him a question of his own, and Tom's three "wants" are a rent bill, an errand and a question someone else asks; his one trait, the rule about who he will not hit, is a prototype's rule waiting on a ruling, not a character. Next to Arthur Morgan, whose whole game is a man reckoning with what he has been, or Henry growing out of a blacksmith's son, Tom is a coat on a hook.

**The people around him: 5.** Sheila is the best thing in the cast: a 53-year-old bookkeeper who calls him "new management" and withholds his name until he earns it is someone a player will work to win over, and Ada's single invitation ("I don't ask twice, mind") is the best-written beat in the document. Ron, the laid-off docker who respects "strength and cash, in that order", is a good shoulder with a hinted shadow. But both love interests are blank (Alison's two scenes unwritten, Philip "still to write" and given a trade the game cannot show), and the most loaded hook in the cast, Sheila seeing Ron argue with a stranger two nights before Mickey died, goes nowhere by ruling.

**The antagonists and the threat: 3.** The structure is clever: one rival goes for his people, one for his clean money, one for his cover, so each squeezes a different seam of the double life. The characters are not: a black widow who "buried a husband and kept his business", a courtly old-money villain who apologises before ruining you, and a loud young upstart are the three stock faces of the British gangster film since The Long Good Friday, and they come from July cards written for a different game, with no scene and no sit-down in words. The outfit Tom actually works for has no face at all, and DS Ellis, the one threat present from week one, wants nothing for herself.

**The opening hour and the scene a player would retell: 7.** The first twelve minutes are well paced: ferry, street, walk-round, first fare, first deed, first ask and the envelope run, all inside the time many games spend on their opening cutscenes, which answers the 10.7% of KCD2 reviews that punish a slow start. The carry is a real retold scene: three routes with honest trade-offs (lit and seen, dark and slow, quick with the firm's name on the roof), a constable who might turn round, Ada asking next morning what he was doing in her alley, and Darren's "Heard you did Mickey's run" proving the town noticed. The cost: a man of Polish family stepping off a boat to a relative's cab firm and a book of debts is the opening of GTA IV beat for beat, and every reviewer who played it will say so in the first paragraph.

**The shape of the three acts and the pace: 4.** On paper the shape is right: a tight scripted week, a long rise, a short week of reckoning. In practice the middle is eleven to fourteen hours, roughly three quarters of the game, with not one written scene, a guaranteed collision with nobody at the door and a closing sit-down with "one head"; there is no midpoint turn, no betrayal and no loss, so the rise has no fall in it. Friday to Sunday of week one are already blank, which suggests the act breaks sit where the writing stops rather than where the story turns.

**Surprise against predictability and cliché: 3.** Nothing in the treatment can surprise yet, because its only mysteries (the fire, the outfit, the real book) have no content to reveal. What is written leans on familiar furniture: the stranger off the boat, the reporter whose editor wants the story dropped, the bookkeeper who knows where the real book is, the priest who holds the dead man's secrets, the widow at the window. Worse, it plants murder (Ron found the body, Sheila saw him argue with a stranger, a locked office, a hidden book) and then rules murder out; genre-literate players will suspect Ron for two months, and "it really was his heart" with nothing else behind it will read as a cheat.

**Whether it feels thin or rushed: 3.** Thin rather than rushed, and thin almost everywhere after the Thursday of week one: the treatment's own gap list runs to fourteen items and takes in the lead, the founding mystery, the whole second act, the antagonists, the third act's inspector and every ending's last scene. Two to three hours for the reckoning is a fair length; a reckoning with an unnamed inspector, unlisted items and unwritten calls is not a reckoning yet. This is the commonest story complaint in the KCD2 data (8.3% of reviews), and as written LEDGER would earn it.

**The endings, and whether they would land: 4.** The ideas are good: endings as states the town is in, every cost shown by a sign first, no single beat that fails the run, the town left open to walk afterwards; that guards well against "choices that change nothing". A VAT inspection as the last reckoning, with the fare book he has lied in since day one finally read, is a fresh climax for a crime game. But no ending has a last scene, Tom's change is never said, "who is where, who calls him what" then the credits will feel abrupt, and the July balance run found the inspection is scenery for anyone who never built a business.

**The side stories as whole stories: 4.** Fares with a wrinkle are the best idea in this section: a cab is the perfect excuse to put a stranger with a story in the back seat, and "somebody who matters getting in" or a rival holding a sit-down in the back of his cab are scenes worth writing. But three to five steps, "mostly light or grey", each ending "in something the town knows", describes vignettes, not the whole stories that 13.7% of KCD2 reviews praise; KCD2's best side quests run for hours, bring characters back and touch the main plot. The 32 drafts were not among the sources, so this mark is on the shape, and the shape is short.

**Tone and humour: 6.** The voice that exists is right: dry, northern, period, people who say "mind" and "boss" and very little else ("Not today"); Agar's and Garbutt's lines have wit, and the five debtors (paid in news, in goods, in fists, not at all, three ways in) mix pathos and comedy well. Humour is not designed anywhere, though, and KCD2's praise for humour (4.7% of reviews) comes from set pieces, not lines. The content rule rightly removes the pub, the natural stage of British crime comedy, so the office and the radio must do that work, and Pauline on the radio is the obvious comic voice, still unwritten.

**How well story and play feed each other: 8.** This is where LEDGER is ahead of most crime games, GTA included. The fare book is the laundering mechanic, the laundering is graded by the climax, the climax is named in the title, the street's talk is both story and threat, the debts teach the five ways to play, and Tom's rise is shown by what people call him. KCD2 and RDR2 are both praised for exactly this marriage; the risk is only that the systems are carrying weight the writing should share.

## 2. Verdict

At the bar of KCD2 and RDR2, LEDGER today is a superb first four days and a clever machine for a story wrapped round an empty middle: no lead with a want, no written mystery, no antagonist with a scene, no final scene. Reviewers would praise the envelope run, Ada's window and a tax inspection as the last reckoning, then punish it under the commonest story complaint there is, thin and predictable, with "GTA IV's opening in a duffel coat" as the line they quote. Praise is within reach, because the parts hardest to invent (the premise, the setting, a structure that ties the books to the climax) are already right; what is missing is writing, not design.

## 3. The five weakest points, most important first

### 1. Tom has no want, no fear and no change

Players praise characters worth caring about above everything else, and the lead is the character they spend the game with. Options:

- a) Use the letter he arrives with. It is in the treatment's first sentence and never mentioned again. Make it Mickey's, written the week before he died to a nephew he had never met: come, and do one thing for me (finish something, or end it). Tom's want becomes "why did Mickey choose me, and what did he want done"; his past stays unsaid, because the letter is about Mickey, not Tom, and the real book answers it in Act III.
- b) Give him a present fear the town can see without telling his past: being back to one suitcase and nothing. The £23.50 and Friday's rent set it up; the Ledger's reading returns to it whenever the money is at risk ("Back to one suitcase."), and the Straight Life ending can show him choosing it freely. If the owner allows, a second option: one letter arrives in week three from wherever he came from, and he never opens it; the past stays unsaid but is felt.
- c) Make the change legible. Turn the "will not lay hands on" rule into a choice the player makes once in week one, so it becomes Tom's code and can be broken at a price; and end every ending with one line from whoever still counts him that names who he became (the man who struck out Julie's debt; the man who sent Lee back to the docks), drawn from what he did.

### 2. The warehouse fire and the outfit are blank, and everything hangs from them

Alison's question, Ellis's case, the real book, Iveson's goods and Walsh's secret all depend on a fire with no date, no owner, no victim and no truth, run by an outfit with no face. Write the truth, the street's version and who knows which before any other story writing. Options:

- a) The insurance fire: the warehouse's owner (Agar, through a front) had it burned to clear the site for the property boom; the outfit did the job; a night watchman the street knew was hurt or killed; Mickey carried the payment, and the envelopes Tom now carries are still paying for the silence, so every carry makes Tom part of it.
- b) The outfit's own fire (recommended, as it uses pieces already in the treatment): the old firm burned its own warehouse to clear the evidence of its dock trade once the labour scheme ended; Iveson bought what was "saved"; Mickey wanted out afterwards, and the stranger Ron argued with in the yard was the landing man telling Mickey he could not stop. The heart ruling holds (he died of his heart, under that strain), Sheila's hidden sighting becomes a payoff instead of a loose end, and the "last of an old firm" proposal gets its reason.
- c) Cammack's fire: the new crew's first job, done to announce itself, which the old firm let pass; Ellis's case then points at a rival, and giving her Cammack in Act III becomes the trade with real weight.

In every option, name the landing man by week three and give him one scene in which he says what Mickey's arrangement was.

### 3. The second act is a blank template

Three quarters of the game has no scene, three rivals climb the same five rungs with the same three answers, and nothing is lost. That is the "same loop" complaint (6.8% of KCD2 reviews) waiting to happen. Options:

- a) Write four to six fixed scenes tied to the firm's milestones, each about a person rather than a rung: the third car (Kevin Needham overheard on the open radio by the Widow's people, and Tom must decide what to do about him); five cars (the guaranteed collision, named: Alison at the flat for supper when Ron comes up the stairs with Terry bleeding, so she sees both lives at once); the first front (Agar's solicitor at the licensing committee in the town hall, a public scene with an audience); and a midpoint loss, someone Tom relied on poached, hurt or turned. Ron poached by the Widow, as the outline first had it, hurts far more than "his least loyal man".
- b) Break the identical ladders so each rival climbs in character: the Widow keeps every deal, so her ladder is a run of offers; Agar's is letters and hearings, slow and certain; Cammack skips rungs, because recklessness is his whole character, so the rule "never skipping a rung" should break for him alone.
- c) Give the player who winds the firm down an act of his own: the rivals still want the rank, the radio, the cars and the licence of an honest cab firm, so the straight life is a siege, not an absence, and June's arc (warming as the business shrinks) lives here. Alongside any of these, consider cutting Act II to six to eight hours around a named midpoint.

### 4. Stock antagonists, and an opening a GTA reviewer has already played

Familiar types are forgivable when they are personal and written; these are neither yet, and the opening hands reviewers the comparison. Options:

- a) Tie each rival to Mickey or Tom's people by something personal, so the pressure is history, not turf: the Widow and Sheila were girls together on the fish dock; Agar owns the freehold of the Quay Street office, so Friday's rent goes to him; Cammack is the stranger in the yard, or Darren's cousin. Then write each sit-down in words, with what each rival wants for himself or herself: Agar a marina on the cleared docks, very 1990; the Widow a clean sale and a way out before the young crews take it; Cammack the respect of a port that remembers his father.
- b) Give DS Ellis a want and a price: she is the one officer who will not let the fire be closed, under a superior who wants it closed, or someone she knew was hurt in it. Decide whether she can be bought (recommended: not bought, but open to a trade), because a detective whose price is undecided is not a character yet.
- c) Move the first image away from GTA IV: use the approved 29 September opening, Sheila meeting him at the office, first image the fare book and the locked door, and save the ferry for the landing at night, where it means something. Or keep the ferry for a few seconds only and open properly on Quay Street.

### 5. No ending has a last scene, and the reckoning is scenery for half the players

KCD2 players rarely punish its ending, but they do praise one that lands, and LEDGER's close is currently "who is where" and the credits. Options:

- a) Write one closing scene per shown ending, each with a named person and one line that says who Tom became: the Kingdom ends with Sheila closing the fare book and calling him "Mr Nowak" again, or "Tom" if she still counts him; the Straight Life with the keys handed over and the ferry leaving, the opening played backwards, one suitcase; Burn Both with Ellis at the door, or a constable at the gangway. Bookending the ferry is cheap and lands.
- b) Make the inspection reach back into Mickey's years, so every player faces it: the inspector's first item is from 1988, Mickey's lies are now Tom's, and Mickey's real book is the answer either way. That ends "scenery for the player who never built a business" and pays off the real book for everyone.
- c) Give the inspector a name, a manner and one scene, the two of them across the fare book on the last morning, and write the last day's calls as scenes: whoever picks up says one line that sums up the campaign he played.

## 4. The three strongest things to keep

1. The ledger as the spine. The fare book from the first walk-round, the nightly count with Sheila, how much the book can hide, and a courteous letter from Customs and Excise grading every lie in the last week: title, mechanic and climax are one idea, rooted in real history (the dock labour scheme's end in 1989), and nothing in GTA ends this way. Keep it, and make it reach every player (weakness 5b).
2. The carry and the night of two lives. The envelope run with its three routes and the constable, Ada's tea at nine against the landing that closes at one, her window seeing which he chose, and the street saying it back to his face the next day in words that fit what he did. This is the town reacting, the thing KCD2's critics praise most, built into the story's first night rather than bolted on.
3. Sheila and the ladder of names. "New management", then the new owner, Nowak, Tom and Tommy to two or three, with Sheila alone withholding his name until she trusts him: progress shown by how people speak to him, never by numbers. It is the best character device in the treatment, and it is the natural place to show who Tom has become.
