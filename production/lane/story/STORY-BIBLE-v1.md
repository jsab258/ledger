# LEDGER: the story bible, version 1

20 November 2026. Written by the story loop's writer from canon.md, RULINGS.md, the approved outline, the first-week designs, DESIGN.md and its addendum, the thirty regulars, the side-story drafts, the treatment and its three readers. Canon outranks this file. Nothing here is built. Where the story needs something canon or a ruling does not hold, the story is written with the change and the change is listed first, for his ruling; canon.md and RULINGS.md are untouched.

How to read it: sections 1 to 6 say what the story is and what is true. Sections 7 to 10 are the story as scenes, in order. Sections 11 to 15 are the side stories, the tone, the pay-offs and the rules of play the story keeps. Spoken lines are samples of the voice, not a script.

## 0. Changes to canon or rulings, for his ruling

Each has the reason and the plain alternative.

1. Ada is the head of the old arrangement. Canon has Ada as a widow at her window in the day-life ring; the first-ask note left "whose outfit" for him. Here Ada is Ada Jensen, widow of the man who founded the Hook's old dockside club, and the envelope is made up in her front room. Why: the outfit needed a face, the readers asked for one the player already knows by day, and the game's own idea is that the town is watching. The watcher at the window being the firm is that idea made into a person, and it pays off the tea, the alley and the window. It also changes one ruling (1 October): Ada never goes to the police. Stood up for her tea, she talks at the grocer's, and her talk is what brings Ellis. Plain alternative: Ada stays an innocent neighbour and the tallyman at the landing (change 4) is the last of the old firm on his own. The story still works; the midpoint loses its picture.
2. Whose outfit. Canon says Mickey left Tom "a half-dead criminal outfit". Here the outfit was never Mickey's alone and never a gang with a boss. It was a club with three seats: the gate, the row and the office. Mickey held the office. Tom inherits that seat, the book and what the book owes. Why: it lets canon's line and the first-ask note both be true, and it makes the inheritance an obligation, which gives Tom something to want. Plain alternative: the outfit is somebody else's and Mickey was its courier, as the first-ask note reads it.
3. Maureen Jensen is Ada's daughter-in-law. Her dead husband, Alan Jensen, was Ada's son and the club's man at the dock gate. Why: it gives the Widow a history with the people Tom inherits, and gives the last act a scene nobody has seen: two widows in the back of a minicab. Plain alternative: no relation; the Widow simply took the gate when the dock scheme ended.
4. The man at the landing gets a name by the end of week one: Cyril Blenkin, the tallyman. The regulars' sheet keeps him unnamed. Why: the readers asked for a face, and he tells Tom what the arrangement is. Plain alternative: he stays "the tallyman" and never gives a surname.
5. The warehouse fire is kept as ruled on 30 September (a Saturday night last November, the importer's warehouse, nobody hurt, the watchman away, the street blaming the owner, the outfit burning him out over unpaid rent, Mickey finding the men, the page in the real book). Added: the date, Saturday 18 November 1989; Mickey drove the two men himself in car two and wrote the fare in the fare book; the word was Ada's; Geoffrey Agar knew beforehand and bought the burnt plot. Why: the fire had to reach the fare book, the climax and one rival. Plain alternative: the fire as ruled and no more, with the page in the real book its only trace.
6. Mickey's heart stands as ruled: he died of it, at his desk, early, Ron found him, and nobody thinks otherwise. Added: he wrote Tom's letter the night before; Sheila found it on the desk and posted it. The stranger Ron argued with in the yard was one of the two fire-setters, back for money. Why: the yard argument is in Sheila's card and every player will ask; it now has a cause and no murder. Plain alternative: cut the yard argument from her card.
7. More people speak of Mickey than the ruling of 30 September allows (only regulars, in two approved lines). Why: he is the dead centre of the story. Plain alternative: Mickey is described only through his book and his letter.
8. Sheila's name for him has two steps: "Mr Nowak" when she trusts him with the real book, and "Tom" only later, once, for an act of his she finds out for herself. Canon says she withholds his name until she trusts him. Why: the readers called her first "Tom" the biggest moment in the game; under the built rule a careful player hears it on day three. Plain alternative: one step, as built.
9. Tom's own line (QUESTIONS 2, open): he lays hands on no woman, nobody of sixty or over, and not the priest. Adopted as recommended there. It makes Ada, the Widow and Agar untouchable by force, and the story is built knowing it.
10. Tom's letter is given words, and Keith Garbutt is the man who found Tom for Mickey. Canon leaves Tom's life before the Hook unsaid: nobody asks and he never tells. Both stay true here. The letter speaks only of Mickey and the office. Garbutt knows where he found Tom, says so once, and never says where. Plain alternative: the letter is a solicitor's and Garbutt knows nothing.
11. DS Ellis cannot be bought (her sheet leaves it open). Her mother's name is in the club's book. One constable under her can be bought, as the addendum already has it.
12. June Suddaby came to the funeral and stayed (the outline) and is a night sister at the infirmary (the July card). Philip Danby is kept small and whole: the last master of the closed school, minding an empty building. Nothing he says touches pupils.
13. The inspection has causes and reaches back. Customs and Excise come because the name on the registration changed when Mickey died; what they look hardest at depends on Tom's own wash and on a tip from whichever rival he hurt most. The officer, Mr Reese, reads Mickey's books for 1988 to 1990 as well as Tom's.
14. Burn Both is shown as two closes: the Reckoning (the man who went for both and lost them) and the Suitcase (the man who let it drift). Three endings shown, five states beneath, as DESIGN proposes (QUESTIONS 1, open).
15. Small things minted: Agar's agents hold the freehold of the parade and Mickey owed Agar £4,000; Friday is wages as well as rent; the council's licensing panel sits at the Harbour Board for anything touching the port; the names Walter and Alan Jensen, Les and Ray Coultas, Frank Cammack, Ivy Leadley and T. A. Reese.

## 1. The spine in a paragraph

In October 1990 Tom Nowak, a stranger with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter from an uncle he never met, takes over a minicab office in a port whose docks have died. The letter says: people are owed. The office turns out to be one seat in an old dockers' club that has paid forty-one families a little money every week for thirty-seven years, by means that were always crooked and ended, last November, in a warehouse fire. The woman who runs what is left of it watches him from the window across the street and asks him to tea. For two months Tom decides what a debt like that is worth: he can keep the forty-one paid and become the port's man, one racket at a time, while a widow, a bankrupt gentleman and a local lad each try to take the street from him; or he can shut the book and try to keep an honest firm and a few people alive without it. Then a mild man from Customs and Excise opens the fare book, in which Mickey wrote down every fare, including the one he drove on the night of the fire. What the books hold, what the street believes and whether one person still says his name with any warmth decide whether Tom ends as the keeper of the Hook, a man with a name and a life, or a stranger with a suitcase again.

## 2. The theme

Every fare goes in the book. LEDGER is about what a place owes its people once the official books have written them off, and about who gets to keep the account.

- The dock labour scheme ended in July 1989 and a government ledger closed on the Hook. Nobody in the game explains why. Everybody lives after it.
- The club's book is the old answer: forty-one names, paid weekly, out of theft, rent taken by fear, and one fire.
- Each rival is a different answer for the same port. The Widow: work for those who can, nothing for those who cannot. Agar: sell the quay to people with boats. Cammack: take it while it is going.
- The town keeps its own account of Tom, and reads it back to him as a name: the new owner, Nowak, Tom, Tommy. A name is the account other people keep of you. To be known is to be owed and to owe.
- The last act is a man from the state asking to see the books. It asks the outline's third question, which life was real, as: which book was true.

The story never says which answer is right. It makes every answer cost a named person something the player can see.

## 3. What is true

Nothing in this section is the street's. The street's version comes after it.

### The club

In 1953 Walter Jensen, a tally clerk on the Meridian quay, started a sick club with a tin and a notebook: every gang paid in, and a man who was hurt, or his widow, got a few shillings a week. Under the dock labour scheme a registered man had work for life, so the club lasted. It grew a second purse. Cargo was short-tallied at the gate, held in a warehouse on the row, moved in cabs and sold. Every business on the row and the quay paid a weekly "rent" so that nothing of theirs went missing. Most of that money went back out in envelopes. Some of it stuck to the people who handled it. It was never called a firm. Old people call it the club. Ron calls it Mickey's arrangement. Only outsiders, the detective and the reporter, say "the outfit".

It had three seats. The gate: Walter, then his son Alan, who decided what fell off a ship. The row: Walter's wife Ada, who kept the stores and, from her front window on Quay Street, the watch. The office: Mickey Suddaby from 1962, whose cabs moved the goods and the money, and who kept the book. Mickey's real book is the club's book: every name, every week, in his hand.

Walter died in 1979. Alan was a hard and greedy man who took more than his share. In March 1986 he went into the dock between a ship and the quay on a night shift. His wife Maureen was on the quay. She did not push him. She did not throw the ring. Gordon Leckenby, the ferryman, pulled him out too late and saw her standing there. Maureen took the gate and ran it better.

In July 1989 the scheme ended. Half the men went. Maureen turned the gate into her own business, hiring by the day and bringing in what pays, and stopped paying into the club: she pays men who work. The row's trade died and the rent with it. Forty-one names were still in the book. Ada and Mickey kept paying them: about £4 a name a week, £164 in all, half on each bank of the water. The Hook's half goes round by hand on Fridays. The far bank's half goes over in an envelope, to Cyril Blenkin, the club's last tally clerk, who meets it at the ferry landing after ten and brings back what the far bank's few payers still give. A tenth of what comes back was the office's. That is the envelope Ron hands Tom on his first night.

### The fire

By autumn 1989 the biggest payer left on the row, an importer of tinned goods and machine parts, stopped paying. In the fare book his rent had always been written as account work, six runs a week that no car ever drove; the account stops in October. Ada said the row needed a lesson or the forty-one would go without. Mickey argued, lost, and did it. He asked Keith Garbutt for two men from across the water, the brothers Les and Ray Coultas. On Saturday 18 November 1989 Mickey met them off the last ferry himself, drove them to the warehouse row in car two, waited and drove them back. Norman Dent, the watchman, had slipped off home on his break, by nobody's doing, and swore afterwards he had walked his rounds. Nobody was hurt. The warehouse burned from two places.

Mickey wrote the run in the fare book, because every fare goes in the book: the date, twenty to midnight, car two, landing to warehouse row and return, two fares, wait, no charge, his initial. In the real book, that week: "The warehouse is settled. The fire settled what the rent couldn't."

That sentence has a second meaning. For a year Mickey had been making up the club's shortfall with money borrowed from Geoffrey Agar, £4,000 in the end, against the lease of the yard behind the office. Agar's family had paid the club for labour peace since the fifties; that was the "understanding". Agar is buying the row plot by plot for a marina. Mickey told him the importer's plot would come free. Agar bought it from the importer in the spring for a third of its worth, and let Mickey's interest ride that quarter. Agar did not order the fire. He knew, he said nothing, and he profited.

The lesson failed. The row emptied faster. The fire brought a detective and a reporter. Mickey confessed it to Father Walsh, who can never repeat it. He told Ada it was the last thing of that kind he would do.

### Mickey's last year

The Coultas brothers came back for more. Mickey paid them twice. On Saturday 22 September 1990 Les Coultas came to the yard a third time and Ron put him out with his hands; Sheila saw it from the back window and did not know the man. The next night Mickey sat late at his desk and wrote to his sister's son, a man he had never met, whom Garbutt had found for him in the summer. He died at that desk before six on the Monday, of the heart his doctor had warned him about. Ron found him. Sheila found the letter, sealed and stamped, by his hand. She does not know whether he would have posted it in the morning. She posted it.

Why the nephew and not the daughter: June left at twenty-three because of the business, and Mickey knew she would shut the book the day she got it. He could not bring himself to shut it and could not bear to hand it to her. In the real book, in August: "The sister's lad. Garbutt says nothing keeps him where he is. He'll come. God forgive me, that's why."

### Who knows what

- Ada and Cyril Blenkin: all of it, except Mickey's borrowing from Agar.
- Agar: the borrowing, that the plot would come free, and that Mickey kept a book.
- Garbutt: that he supplied the brothers and found the nephew. Not why.
- Ron: the envelopes, the Friday round, the man in the yard. He was not at the fire and never asked.
- Sheila: the real book's place, not its fire page; she keeps the books, not Mickey's own pages. She has read the fare book's line for 18 November many times and never asked him about it.
- Father Walsh: Mickey's confession, sealed.
- Norman Dent: that he was not there. Darren knows that Norman was not there.
- Maureen: that the club still pays, and roughly whom. She believes the fire was Ada's doing and cannot prove it.
- Ellis: a Mickey's cab was seen waiting at the landing late that night. The watchman's times do not fit the brigade's.
- Alison: the importer had stopped paying somebody.
- June: that there is a second book and what kind of thing is in it.

### The street's version

The importer burned his own warehouse for the insurance, his trade being dead, and went south in the spring. That is a street fact: people say it as known. A second, quieter version says the docks' old lot did it. Nobody on the street connects the fire with the envelopes, and nobody who takes the club's money asks where it comes from.

## 4. Tom Nowak

Tom is 32, the son of a Polish post-war settler and Mickey's sister. He has never been to the Hook. His life before it is unsaid: nobody asks and he never tells. He is not voiced; his lines are typed or picked. Everything below is carried by what he holds, what he does and what people call him, never by a speech about his past.

The letter. It is in his hand in the first shot and on the first page of the Ledger, in Mickey's writing:

"Tom. You don't know me. I was your mother's brother. I have a cab office on Quay Street in Meridian, two cars, and a good woman who keeps the books. It is yours when I am gone, and the doctor says that is sooner than I would like. There is more to it than cabs. There is a book. People are owed, and I have not always paid them the right way. Come and look before you say yes. Ask Sheila for the book when she is ready to give it. If you cannot stomach it, shut the door, and nobody will blame you, least of all me. Every fare goes in the book. M. Suddaby. P.S. Don't let them call you Mickey's nephew for ever."

What he wants. A place that is his, where his own name is said by people who mean it. The letter gives the want its shape in each act. Week one: make Friday, and find out what "people are owed" means. Act II: do right by the forty-one, by keeping them paid or by finding an honest way to stop, and stop being "Mickey's nephew". Act III: keep the book from drowning everyone whose name is in it.

What he needs. To learn that a name is a debt. The people who come to know him will count on him, and a man can be known to everyone and loved by nobody. He has to stop living in other people's books (Mickey's, Ada's, the town's) and write a line of his own.

His line. He lays hands on no woman, nobody of sixty or over, and not the priest. The game never offers the blow. It is tested twice: when the Widow stands between him and Ron at the dock gate, and when Ada, seventy-four, is the one person whose word could finish him. Each time the only ways through are to pay, to walk, or to out-count her.

What he fears. Two things, both shown and neither spoken. The suitcase: being nobody again, moving on with what he can carry. It stays packed under the bed in Mickey's flat until the player unpacks it, one press, whenever he likes; Sheila notices the day he does ("You've unpacked.") and the flat stops being Mickey's. And being known for more than he has shown: Keith Garbutt found him for Mickey and says so once ("It took me two days to find you, which tells me something, and I'll not say what"). Garbutt never says more and nobody else ever learns it. The past stays unsaid because Tom keeps it so, and the player feels that he is keeping it.

How he changes, and what each ending says he became. Each close gives one line, from the person whose judgement the player earned, that names him by what he did.

- The Kingdom: he becomes the keeper. He holds the book, the street and the forty-one, as Mickey and Ada did between them, and he has done at least one thing of the kind Mickey swore was his last. The landing asks for "Nowak's". Plain turn: everyone calls him Mr Nowak. Better turn: one person still says Tom.
- The Straight Life: he becomes a man with a name and no kingdom. He shut the book or made it lawful, and the name is used by a friend, in daylight, for nothing.
- Burn Both: he becomes what he was on the gangway. In the Reckoning his name is shouted on the quay by a constable. In the Suitcase nobody uses it at all.

## 5. The people who matter

Each has a want, a secret and an arc that pays off.

Sheila Dunn, 53, the bookkeeper, at Mickey's since she was 22. Kept exactly as written: "New management", the walk-round, the fare-book rule, the locked door ("Not today"), the Sunday question. Want: that the office outlives Mickey and that the fare book stays true; it is the one clean thing in thirty-one years. Secret: she posted the letter and does not know whether Mickey meant it sent; and she has never struck or questioned the line for 18 November. She also saw Ron and the stranger in the yard. Arc: she sizes Tom up as she promised. With a clean week she trusts him: he becomes "Mr Nowak", and on Sunday the real book is on the counter. The nightly count is where they live: every pound washed past what the fares can carry she says aloud before she writes it, and thinks less of him. "Tom" comes once, later, and only for something she finds out herself in the books: that he paid someone on the list out of his own tin in a short week and did not write it up. "It wasn't a fare," he can say. "No. Goodnight, Tom." In Act III, if she trusts him, she tells him where the lie in the books holds, and on the Thursday she tells him about the letter. Every ending's last image is a line in her hand.

Ron Kirby, 58, a docker for thirty years, on the door and the rank since 1989. Want: to be a foreman of something again; failing that, to be paid and not pitied. Secret: he put Les Coultas out of the yard two nights before Mickey died and believes the row brought the heart on. He pawned Mickey's watch to pay for the funeral flowers. Arc: he brings the asks, walks the Friday round, stands at Tom's shoulder. He respects strength and cash, in that order, and the Widow offers both: his old gang work her gate and she wants him as foreman. Whether he goes turns on small things the player did or left: the watch, the Friday supper, his cut, whether Tom stood beside him when two men waited by car two. If he stays he is the natural successor for a firm kept the old way. If he goes he is the man at the gate in Act III who can open it or shut it.

Ada Jensen, 74, the widow at her window. The first week keeps her exactly as written: the window, the single invitation, "I don't ask twice, mind", the tea against the landing. Want: that the forty-one are paid until the last of them is buried, and that somebody is fit to take the watch. Secret: the club is hers, the fire was her word, the envelope is made up on her table, and the tea is an interview. She let the room upstairs to the reporter on purpose. Arc: she is lonely and she is the last head of the old firm, and both are true in every scene. The player learns it at the midpoint by seeing the tallyman at her window, or earlier by following Ron at dusk on an ask night to her back door. After that she is the most dangerous person in the game and the only one who never raises her voice. She pays off three ways: in the Kingdom she gives up the window to him; on the straight road she is the last and hardest opponent of an honest club; in Burn Both she is the one who watches him go.

Cyril Blenkin, 63, the tallyman at the landing. "Mickey's?" "Right. Wednesday, same again." He counts an envelope with his thumb without looking down. Want: to hand the tally on before his eyes fail; he reads the ferry's lights better than a face. Secret: he kept a tally of what Alan Jensen stole from the club, and gave it to Ada the week before Alan drowned. Arc: the last man who counts. On night seven he rides in Tom's cab to the burnt plot and says what the arrangement is. His question at the landing changes with the ending.

June Suddaby, 34, Mickey's daughter, a night sister at the infirmary, back for the funeral and still in town, "wanting nothing from the office". Want: the book shut, and not by her. Secret: she nurses half the forty-one and knows what four pounds does in a cold week; she has never been able to square that with what the office did to her father. Mickey wrote to her for years at an address she had left; the letters came back and wait at the landing. Arc: she is the mirror. She warms as the business shrinks and goes cold when Tom wears Mickey's coat. People from his night life arrive on her ward. Agar's solicitor offers to fund her claim on the estate; her answer depends on what Tom has done. On the straight road the idea that saves the club is hers: make it lawful.

Alison Sedman, 31, the paper's port reporter, lodging above Ada's since August. Want: the fire, right, under her name. Her editor wants it loud, soon and safe: the importer did it. She will not print what she cannot stand up. Secret: she chose the room for the view of Mickey's and has no idea whose ceiling she sleeps over. Arc: she is the dangerous choice. She never burns a source: true things confided are safe with her and cost her something; a lie she finds out goes in the story drawer. Three scenes are hers (sections 8 and 9). Her piece runs on the Thursday of inspection week, and what it says is the sum of what Tom told her.

Philip Danby, 36, the last master of the closed school on the hill end of the Hook, kept on to mind the building and list its library before the building is sold. Want: the building kept, for anything. Secret: he has already been offered a post inland and has not answered. Arc: a quiet, funny man with a chessboard and a paraffin heater, the other person Tom can be honest for, as a friend or more. Agar's company wants the school for the marina's car park. Ten thousand books are the best place in Meridian to hide one. He can speak for Tom at the licence hearing.

Father Brendan Walsh, the parish priest, Irish-born. Want: that the arrangement does not eat another generation. Secret: none he can ever use. Arc: he buried Mickey and counted who came. On Remembrance Sunday he reads the port's dead. Outside the confessional, at his kitchen table, he tells Tom the one thing he may: Mickey asked him whether a man can repent of a thing and go on paying for it out of the profits. "I told him no. He kept paying. I've not decided which of us was right." On the straight road he is the club's lawful trustee.

Darren Milner, 25, who sells nothing anyone can name. Want: to be somebody's man; to be on the landing. Secret: he knows the watchman was not on the row on the night of the fire, and has sold that to nobody because nobody has offered enough. Arc: the first to say Tom's night to his face. If Tom carries the envelope, Darren is the boy outside the door, jealous and for sale, and somebody buys him. If Tom says no, Ada gives the envelope to Darren, who is proud and bad at it, and at the midpoint he is the one who pays. Either way, what the player did with a man who sleeps in the spare car comes back.

DS Carol Ellis, 44, born in the Hook. Want: to close the fire properly before her inspector closes it for her at the year's end as the owner's own work. Secret: her mother, Ivy Leadley, is one of the forty-one, and Ellis has never let herself ask where the four pounds comes from. Arc: she asks while the case is answerable and tells once it is not. She cannot be bought. Putting her mother's name to her is possible and ugly, and she answers it by offering to hand the case to strangers who know nobody in the Hook. Her trade in Act III is honest: a name and a page for her silence on the rest.

Keith Garbutt, the Fixer. Moustache, car coat, percentages. Want: both pans paid. Secret: he supplied the Coultas brothers and found Tom. That is why he was at the funeral: to see who would be paying. Arc: he sells true reads, a meeting and one truce. In Act III the brothers become his problem too, and for once he is the one who needs a favour.

Pauline Kitching, 44, the radio. The game's dry running voice and its chorus: she says on air, to every cab, what the office thinks of the day. Kevin Needham, 36, the day driver, wants his own plate and is promised one by the Widow. Terry Hodgson, 52, the night driver, says four words a shift and is the one who is hurt.

Mr T. A. Reese, 57, Officer of Customs and Excise. A flask of tea, a bad knee, a propelling pencil. His father drove a cab. He likes cabs and he likes books that add up, and he is sorry, truly, when one does not. He is not an enemy. He is the first person in the story who only wants to know what happened.

## 6. The rivals

Three kinds of threat, kept: the Widow goes for his people, Agar for his clean money, Cammack for his cover. New: each wants something of his own, each is in Mickey's real book, and each climbs differently. The rule that no rung is skipped unseen holds for the Widow and Agar. Cammack breaks it, because breaking it is who he is, and his sign is always shown at least a game hour before it lands.

The peaks come in turn, so the middle has a shape: the street first (Cammack, up to the midpoint), then his people (the Widow, the weeks after it), then paper (Agar, last), then the books.

### Danny Cammack: the street, by noise

26, born three streets from the office, and unwilling to say so. Loud on purpose; quicker than he lets on. His crew sell what moves faster than a shift change; the game never shows what.

- Want: the Hook, and the end of the old lot. A stranger was handed the street Danny was born on and could never earn. "You've got the keys. I've got the accent. One of us is in the wrong house."
- Why it is personal: his father, Frank, a registered docker, had his hand crushed in 1981 and was struck off the club's book in 1984 on Alan Jensen's say-so, as a man who had talked. Danny grew up watching his father wait for an envelope that had stopped.
- In the book: "F. Cammack struck off, A.J.'s word. Wrong man. Paid him since from my own." Mickey paid Frank quietly for six years. Frank hid it from his son out of pride. Danny's whole grudge stands on something false, and the page proves it.
- His climb: he skips rungs. Noticed: the first Saturday, match day, he buys chips at the Tangs' and talks to Tom like an old friend in front of the whole street. A visit: he takes Tom's cab, has him drive round the estate he grew up on, and pays an 80p fare with a fifty-pound note the float cannot change. "Keep it. Call it rent." Then, with no pressure rung between, the threat is the deed: on Bonfire Night his crew burn car two.
- How it can end: with fists, in the open (the only head Tom can fight); in the ring at the boxing club behind the chandler's, gloves on, nothing staked, the whole Hook watching, which Danny proposes himself "like our dads would have"; with the page, shown to him or to Frank; with Ada's answer, a third fire; or with his name given to Ellis.

### Maureen Jensen, the Widow: his people, by offers

50, broad-shouldered, dressed like a harbour master. She took the gate when her husband drowned and ran it better. Since the scheme ended, sixty men stand at the dock gate at six each morning and she picks twenty.

- Want: every job at the gate through her hands, and the club's book closed. "A pension for men who'll never work is a tax on men who do." The eleven men on the list who could still work will not sign with her while Ada's four pounds comes. She also wants Mickey's as the gate's cab firm: cars that move her men and her goods with a fare written for each.
- Why it is personal: Ada is her mother-in-law and they have not spoken since the funeral in 1986. "I buried a husband and kept his business. His mother buried a business and kept her window. Ask her which she's still crying over."
- In the book: the gate's payments in, stopping July 1989; and Cyril's tally of what Alan took.
- Her climb: she keeps every deal, so her ladder is a run of offers, each fair by her arithmetic, each worse than the last, each put once. She never threatens. She prices. Noticed: a voice that is not Pauline's on the open radio one morning: "Tell Mickey's nephew the gate sends its regards." Kevin's short fares are how she knew. A visit: she books a car herself, has Tom drive her to the dock gate before six and keep the engine running while she picks her twenty. "That's the scheme now. Me." First offer: fifty pence on every fare through the gate. Second: she takes the list off his hands, work for the eleven, the parish for the rest. Third: Ron, as her foreman. Last: Mickey's becomes the gate's firm; he keeps the name on the roof and a wage.
- How it can end: accept at any rung (and the forty-one become eleven); defy (his drivers are stopped at the gate, the cold-store contract goes, a man leaves); or counter with what he knows: what Gordon Leckenby saw in March 1986, which is a threat and makes her an enemy for good, or what the list is worth to her, which is a deal.

### Geoffrey Agar: his clean money, by the calendar

61, silver, unhurried. Third generation of the family that owns paper on half the port. He has borrowed against a marina on the dead docks at fifteen per cent, and his bank wants its money on 31 December. He is ruined if the row and the old basin are not his to sell by then. The courtesy is all he has left to spend.

- Want: the yard behind Mickey's, which is the only road to the old basin; the lease Mickey pledged; and one page of Mickey's book with his own name on it.
- Why it is personal: "Mickey and I had an understanding once. It died before he did." He lent Mickey £4,000. He is Tom's landlord: the £38 rent on the office and flat goes to his agents every Friday.
- His climb: letters, each with a date in it, so the player always knows what is coming and when. Slow and certain. Noticed: the agents' letter, a rent review, "with regret". A visit: he does not visit Quay Street, so he gets into Tom's cab outside the Harbour Board and is driven along the dead docks while he describes four hundred berths and a hotel where the cold store stands. "They will call it Meridian Quays. I voted against the name." He apologises, and offers to forgive Mickey's debt for the yard. Pressure: the private-hire licence, called in before the licensing panel on Thursday 22 November, on the ground that Tom has driven fares on a dead man's badge since his first afternoon, which is true. A threat: his solicitor offers June the money to contest the will. The last rung is a telephone call to Customs and Excise.
- How it can end: sell him the yard (the debt is gone and so are the yard's two escapes); beat him on paper, at the hearing and over probate, with the people who know forms (Edith Stockill, Christine Pickering, Philip); or counter with the page, which makes him an accessory to the fire. A cornered man with a title is the most dangerous of the three, and he is sixty-one: Tom cannot touch him.

### The three together

They know each other. The Widow despises Cammack and buys from him. Agar needs the Widow's men off the basin and will not be seen with her. Cammack wants what both have. Keith Garbutt carries messages between all three and sells Tom true reads of each. From the midpoint on they move against one another as well as against Tom, and what he knows from the book is worth more to each about the other two than about himself. That is the playing-off the player does with his own hands.

## 8. Act II: The Rise and the Squeeze

Monday 22 October to Sunday 2 December. Eleven to fourteen hours. Beats fire on states, never on sequence, with two exceptions the town itself names: the calendar's days (Bonfire Night, Remembrance Sunday, the fire's anniversary) and dates that arrive in letters (the hearing). Those days happen whether Tom is ready or not, so a slow player is pulled forward rather than left with fewer scenes. Between the scenes below runs the game as designed: fares, jobs, the count, the board, recruits, fronts, side stories.

The act has one question in each half. Before the midpoint: can he make it his? After it: whose book is it?

Two roads run through the same days and the same set pieces. The keeper's road is for the player who took it over or would not say. The straight road is for the player who wound it down. Each scene says what differs.

### First half: the firm is his

Scene 1. Monday's wages (week two). Pauline, at the counter, before the first call: "Am I still wanted, then?" Who: Pauline, Kevin, Terry, Sheila listening. At stake: the firm's people learning what Sunday's answer means for them. Choice: what he tells them, and whether it is true. Comes back: Kevin's ear at the gate carries it to the Widow word for word.

Scene 2. The third car and the first recruit. A car bought from a man selling one; Julie Kettlewell, twelve years on the laundry's presses and twelve weeks' notice, who can drive. Meet her need (slow, holds) or use the £9 she owed (quick, brittle). Comes back: Julie is the successor for an honest firm.

Scene 3. The long way round. Kevin's day fares come in short against his mileage. Sheila sees it in the book before anyone says it. Followed, Kevin is running parcels for the gate in Mickey's car, for a promise: the Widow will get him a hackney plate of his own. Choice: sack him on the rank, turn him (he tells the gate what Tom wants it to hear), take a cut, or say nothing and pay the gap. Comes back: the radio leak is now Tom's weapon or the Widow's.

Scene 4. Alison, first scene: the row at low tide. A Sunday. She asks him to walk the warehouse row with her, because a man with Mickey's name on his roof can stand there without being stared at. They look over the burnt plot together: casing, with a guide. Norman Dent's torch comes along the row and she steps behind Tom. She quotes back, exactly, what he said in the cab in week one. Then she asks him one true thing about Mickey's nights. At stake: which drawer he goes in. Choice: a true thing (safe with her, and it costs her to keep), a lie (she will find it), or nothing. She takes a photograph of the plot with him in the edge of the frame. "I'll crop you out. Probably." For the player whose someone is Philip, the same afternoon is chess in the school library by the paraffin heater, among crates of books marked for pulping, and the true thing is asked across the board.

Scene 5. The gate at six. The Widow's visit, as section 6: the dawn fare to the dock gate, the engine running, sixty men and twenty picked. Lee Drewery is not picked and sees whose cab she came in. Her first offer, put once.

Scene 6. A ride for the sergeant. Rain, night, Ellis's car dead by the fish market. If Tom drives her himself it is the first long talk with the law, in his own cab, with the radio on. She gives an address on Tannery Row. It is the door Ron said to knock soft at. She does not explain and Tom does not ask.

Scene 7. Fifty pounds. Cammack's fare round the estate and the note the float cannot change. Whatever Tom does with the note (banks it, sends it back, gives it to the Tangs for the window Danny's lads shout at) is on the street by evening.

Scene 8. The rent review. Agar's first letter, and his drive along the dead docks. On the keeper's road he offers to forgive the debt for the yard. On the straight road he offers to buy the whole firm for what Mickey owed him. Dated: the hearing, Thursday 22 November.

On the keeper's road the asks grow through these weeks: the far bank pays less each time, the packet that comes back is thinner, and the forty-one still need £164. Cyril: "It doesn't add. It hasn't added since the gate stopped." The rise is driven by that sum. A first front, a first round, the cold-store contract: each is how the list gets paid this month, and each makes the club less of a club.

On the straight road the envelope still goes down the quay, carried by Darren in a coat too big for him, proud, and seen by everyone. Twice it goes short. Winifred has chrysanthemums again. June rides to her shift in Tom's cab and talks to him for the first time. The rivals come sooner, because a firm with no arrangement behind it is only a rank, a radio and a yard.

### The midpoint: the Fifth

Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990. If the firm has reached rung 2 by then, it is staged there; if not, on the first evening after that it has. The display on the quay is for the town's grown people: a fire in a brazier the size of a van, fireworks off the old coal jetty, the one night the quay is full after dark. Noise covers everything.

1. The book. That afternoon, on the keeper's road, Sheila opens Mickey's office if she trusts him, or because Ellis's people have been asking for it: "I'd sooner you saw it before she does." He reads the forty-one in Mickey's hand and knows half the names from the round. He reads the page: "The warehouse is settled. The fire settled what the rent couldn't." He reads the line from August about himself: "Nothing keeps him where he is. He'll come. God forgive me, that's why." At stake: what he now knows he was sent for. On the straight road the office stays shut and he reads none of it yet.
2. The supper. The first evening his someone (Alison, or Philip) comes up to the flat above the office. If the suitcase is still packed under the bed, the guest sees it. The windows flash. This is the day life, and it is going well.
3. The door. Ron on the stairs, blood on his sleeve that is not his. "Boss. They've done car two." Under the fireworks Cammack's crew have burned it where it stood, by the kerb under Ada's window. Terry Hodgson was asleep across the back seat between fares. Ron pulled him out. Terry's hands are burned and he will not drive again. The guest is standing behind Tom and sees and hears all of it. On the straight road the knock is Darren, beaten, the far bank's envelope gone: Danny's lads took it off him on the quay in the crowd. Car two burns as well.
4. The window. Down in the street the car is still alight and half the Hook has turned from the fireworks to watch. Ada is at her window, lit by it, and she is not afraid. The tallyman is standing in her front room beside her chair, where the landing man has no business to be. A window is a watcher only when somebody is at it; tonight there are two. That is the reveal, and nobody says a word of it. Then her front door opens. "You'd better come in, Tom."
5. The second tea. Ada's front room, the pot, the table with forty-one small envelopes laid out in rows. She tells it plainly: Walter and the tin in fifty-three; her son, who was no good; the gate that stopped paying; the fire. "Mickey found the men. I found the reason. He never forgave me and he never stopped carrying." On the keeper's road she gives the old firm's first real order: Danny Cammack is to be answered tonight, his lock-up on the row, the same way. "Mickey wouldn't, after. You'll want to decide what you are." On the straight road she asks one thing only: "You said no to Mickey's arrangement and I let that stand. Now a lad's been hurt carrying what you wouldn't. Fetch it back."

What the player can choose:
- Ada's answer: burn the lock-up. A night job, loud. The escalation dial moves; Danny's men begin to carry; Ada counts him as hers.
- His own answer: front Danny himself, tonight or in the ring; or take back only what was taken (a break-in at the lock-up for the envelope, on either road); or go to Garbutt for a meeting.
- The law: give Ellis the names. The street learns who talks.
- Refuse. "Then I'll ask Darren." She will.

What is lost, whatever he chooses: Terry, for good. Car two. The guest's innocence: Alison has seen a story and Philip has seen the man. And the idea that the envelope was ever Mickey's alone.

What comes back: Terry is on June's ward. Hal Metcalfe was filming the fireworks from his shop door with the only camcorder in town. Ellis has a burned car on Quay Street and a reason to stay. And from tonight Ada says "Tom".

Scene 9. Remembrance Sunday, 11 November. The chapel. Father Walsh reads the port's dead: the wars, and by the Hook's custom the men the docks have taken since. Alan Jensen. This year, Michael Suddaby. Everybody is in one room for the only time in the game: Ada, out of her house on the one day she leaves it; Maureen across the aisle from her; Agar with the Harbour Board's wreath; Frank Cammack with his ruined hand, and Danny standing at the back; Ellis beside her mother; Sheila; June. Two minutes of silence in which the player can only choose where Tom looks, and who looks back. Nothing happens. It is a day's truce and the whole board in one frame. Afterwards, at the priest's kitchen table, Father Walsh says the one thing he may.

Scene 10. The Widow's offers, and Ron. Through weeks five and six her second and third offers arrive. Ron's old gang stand him a breakfast at the caff. Then a Friday comes when Ron is late for the round. Who: Ron, the Widow, Lee, the men at the gate. At stake: the midpoint took a driver; this can take the door. Choice: match her (make Ron foreman of something real: the night desk, the round, the yard), let him go with a handshake, or hold him by what he did in the yard two nights before Mickey died, which holds him and loses him. If Ron holds, she takes the cold-store contract and Kevin instead. Something is always lost here. Tom's line is tested: she stands between him and Ron at the gate, and there is nothing to do with his hands.

Scene 11. The anniversary, Sunday 18 November. A year to the day. Alison's paper wants "One year on". Norman Dent, who misses nothing on the row and was not there, has begun to sleep badly. Darren offers to sell Tom what he knows about the watchman. Ellis sends notice: she will search the office tomorrow evening. That is the sign, and it gives one night.

Scene 12. The night of the book. The real book cannot be in the office when she comes. A carry across the Hook under watch, with the heaviest thing he has ever had under the coat. Where he takes it is whom he trusts: Ada's table (she will keep it, and it is hers again); the school library, among ten thousand others (Philip will not open it: "I taught history. I know what a first-hand account does to a man"); the room above Ada's (Alison will read every page and print none of it without him, and will never be the same about him); the priest's house (Father Walsh refuses, kindly); or the yard shed, and hope. Or he leaves it on the counter and lets Ellis find it, which is one way to begin her trade early. On the straight road the book is still locked in and the question is whether to let Sheila open the door before the police do. Comes back: whoever holds the book in Act III.

Scene 13. The hearing, Thursday 22 November. The licensing panel sits in the Harbour Board's boardroom, three members and a clerk, with a public bench. Agar's solicitor puts the dead man's badge, every arrest, every caution and every telling he has been able to collect. Then the chair asks whether anyone will speak for the firm, and the town's memory becomes testimony. Whoever Tom has dealt with fairly can stand: Edith Stockill with her fountain pen, Christine Pickering with the forms in order, Grace Rinaldi, Brenda Laverack, Philip, Father Walsh, Lily Tang. Whoever he has wronged can stand too. It is the game's ledger of him read aloud, and he mostly listens. Outcomes: the licence kept; kept with conditions; or suspended, after which every fare is a risk and every stop is a search. None ends the game.

Scene 14. June and the will. Agar's solicitor has written to her. She comes to the office for the first time, stands in the doorway of the room where her father died, and asks what she has never asked: to see the book. Then she tells him what she was offered. What she does turns on what he has done: the letters, the coat, Terry on her ward, the forty-one. On the straight road this is where she says it: "It doesn't have to be shut. It has to be lawful. - The Widow, in the back of his own cab, driving the dock road in the dark with the meter running. "I'll pay what's on the clock. I always do." Her last offer, his counter, and the price of each.
- Agar, in the cab outside the Harbour Board, apologising. The yard, the debt, the page. "I don't ask what Mickey wrote. I ask where it is kept."
- Cammack, on the quay edge where the bonfire was, no cab, no coat. "Say it, then. Say I'm from here and you're not."
Accept, defy, or counter with what he knows. Each closes one rival's story and opens the last week, because whoever is left hurt and standing makes a telephone call.

### The straight road through the second half

The same scenes with different stakes. Ada does not accept being wound up: "You don't close a club by not turning up." She is this road's fourth and hardest pressure, and she is seventy-four and right about the forty-one. The firm has to earn £164 a week clean, in the open, to pay people the law does not say it owes: the cold-store contract, the Harbour Board's late men, the laundry's hampers. Edith does the forms. The society meets in the school hall. The hearing matters more, because a suspended licence ends the money. The Widow wants the list closed, Agar wants the firm for the price of Mickey's debt, Cammack wants the street, and Tom has no arrangement behind him and no night money in the tin. It is the hardest way through the game and it has the same number of scenes.

## 9. Act III: The Ledger Comes Due

Monday 3 to Saturday 15 December. Two to three hours.

### Why the inspector comes

Three causes, and the player can read all three.

1. The name on the form changed. Mickey was the registered trader; when a trader dies Customs and Excise look at the business before they register the next one. Sheila said so at minute two. This cause reaches every player, whatever he built.
2. Tom's own wash. Every week the books were "off" is a week Mr Reese reads twice. Takings that a firm this size could not have earned are not a tax he is owed; they are a reason to check fuel and mileage against the book and, if they do not fit, to pass a note along the corridor.
3. A telephone call. Whichever rival came out of Act II hurt and standing has rung Customs with one particular thing to look at. Agar says the 1989 accounts. The Widow says the drivers' sheets, which Kevin copied. Cammack says a cab office that pays forty-one pensioners. In the first two mornings Reese's questions show which, and the Ledger says so.

The letter comes on Monday 3 December and names Monday the 10th. It is courteous. It is signed T. A. Reese.

It reaches back. Reese reads three years. Mickey's lies are Tom's now: the estate is liable for tax on the account work that was never driven, and Tom is the estate. For the man who wound the firm down and washed nothing, the danger is a bill that closes an honest firm and the lawful club with it. Nobody sits this week out.

### The week before

The town smells it. The Widow bids openly for his drivers. Agar's agents serve notice on the yard. Cammack, if he is still standing, promises noise on an inspection morning. Les Coultas is seen at the landing: he has read that the paper is running the fire again and wants paying to stay across the water. Garbutt, for the first time, asks Tom for a meeting. Ada sends for him: she wants the real book back on her table before any man from the government sits in that office.

### The five mornings and the five nights

The days are Reese at the counter. The nights are Tom's hands.

Monday. The item: the fare book since October, with the drivers' sheets. This is Tom's own wash, read aloud by a mild man with a pencil. If Kevin's sheets are with the Widow they are missing. Night: get them back from the gate hut. A break-in on the docks, or Ron's word if Ron is at the gate, or a deal.

Tuesday. The item: fuel and mileage. "I like to ride a route or two. Mileage is a truthful thing." Reese rides in car one all morning with his flask, and Tom drives. Whatever the street does that morning it does in front of a Customs officer: Cammack's noise if he promised it, a fare who talks, a constable who nods or does not. Night: Ada asks to be driven. She has not been in a car since 1986. "The dock gate." Maureen gets in at the gate. Two widows in the back of a minicab, the meter running, Tom's eyes in the mirror, and thirty-seven years of the club settled or not between a woman who will not stop paying the forty-one and a woman who will not start. He drives; he can speak once. What he says, and what he has already done with the list, decides whether they make a peace, a war, or a sale. Tom's line holds: there is nothing here his hands can do.

Wednesday. The item: the account customers, 1988 to 1990. The importer's six runs a week, stopping in October 1989. Reese says nothing and makes a small mark. Night: Ellis, at the burnt plot. Her trade, as the outline has it: give her a rival and a page, and she closes the fire and leaves the rest; the price is that the street learns who talks. The page gives her Agar. The lock-up and Terry's hands give her Cammack. The gate gives her the Widow. He can refuse, and still reach the best ending through what the town believes. He can put her mother's name to her; she answers by offering to hand the file up to people who know nobody. Later that night Alison knocks. She has the proof of tomorrow's page and has broken her own rule to bring it. She asks him one question. A true answer, a lie or silence each print differently.

Thursday. The paper is on every counter by eight. The item: "The book for the quarter to December 1989, if you'd be so kind." Sheila fetches it. Before she hands it over she tells him about the letter: "He wrote it. I posted it. I've asked myself every night since whether he'd have torn it up by morning." Then Reese turns to Saturday 18 November and reads, in Mickey's hand, a run to the warehouse row at twenty to midnight, two fares, wait, no charge. "Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak." He asks to take the books away overnight. Night: the last job. The book is in Reese's lodging at Mrs Rennison's boarding house until morning, and one line in it ties the fire to the office. The choices:
- Leave it. Mickey wrote it; let it stand. What follows depends on everything else.
- Alter it. Sheila will not, and will not sit beside a book that has been scratched. A break-in at the boarding house, a pen, and Reese is a man who notices a different ink.
- Give it to Ellis first, as the trade.
- Burn the book. A second fire. After it the books cannot hold.
Les Coultas is in the Hook that night too, wanting his money, and is the only living man who can say who paid him.

Friday. Reese has the office from nine and the telephone in it is the one everybody hears. Tom has the phone box on the corner and the coins in his pocket: five calls. He counted coins on the first day as well. He can ring anyone he has dealt with, to ask for one thing: a word to the panel, a door held, a statement changed, a car moved, a debt called. Who picks up, and what they say before he has finished asking, is the campaign he played. Then he walks back in, and Reese closes his notebook and says one of two things: "That all seems to be in order, Mr Nowak." Or: "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak."

Signs before every cost, as designed: Reese's manner, Sheila's words, Ellis asking and then telling, the remark that stops as he passes, the friend's unasked act, the successor's offer and its withdrawal. Tom's reading in the Ledger is built only from these.

## 10. The endings

States, never a menu. Three shown, five beneath, in the built order. Each has a last scene with named people, a line that says who he became, the fate of everyone who worked for him, and a last image. The last image of every ending is a line being written in the fare book.

### The Kingdom

Reached when the business stands and the books hold.

The last scene. Saturday 15 December, after ten, the ferry landing under the customs lamp. A carrier in a dark coat that does not fit him (Darren, or Lee) walks the envelope down the lit quay past the constable, who looks at the coat and looks away. The tallyman steps up and asks, for the first time: "Nowak's?" Across Quay Street Ada's window is dark and her chair is gone from it. The camera climbs to the flat above the office. There is a figure at the glass. Tom has the watch.

Below, Sheila locks the office. On the plain turn she says, "Goodnight, Mr Nowak," and she has given her notice for the end of the quarter. Ron has the gate or the door. June has left the Hook. The forty-one are paid, by rounds and fronts that have nothing of a club left in them. Ada, from her back room: "Mickey kept the book. My Walter kept the street. You'll do for both." That is who he became.

On the better turn (the built Both: one friend counts him, the street does not hold his rackets as fact, Ellis's case is answerable, the books hold and make sense), Sheila stops at the door. "Night, Tom." It is the same kingdom, with one person in it. It can be reached by a careful first player; it is hard, not hidden.

The last image: the fare book on the counter, her last line of the day. "22.10. Landing. Account." And under it tomorrow's page, ruled and empty.

What it leaves: the fire-night line stands in a book the inspector passed. Agar is ruined or bought off. The Widow has the gate and a treaty. Danny is gone, or his.

### The Straight Life

Reached when there is no business of his at the close and one friendship holds.

The better turn (the built Quiet: handed on to someone who can hold it, never while hunted for a killing). The last scene. Friday, the chapel hall. The Quay Street Friendly Society pays out for the first time, in the open. Edith Stockill has the pen. "Sign there, dear. And there." Forty-one signatures in a book anybody may read. Father Walsh and June are the trustees. Tom is not on the committee and stands at the back. Then three days of watching, as built: Julie Kettlewell has the firm, or Ron does, and the board, the rank and the radio either hold without him or do not. On the last evening Tom gets into the back of car one. "Where to?" "Just round." Pauline, on air, to every cab: "Car one's got the old gaffer. Be nice." Back at the office Sheila writes the fare herself and says, "Mind how you go, Tom." For many players it is the first time. That is who he became: a name in the book as a fare, not as the owner.

The last image: her line. "Nowak, T. Round the Hook. 80p. Paid."

The plain turn (never built, or sold up). Shaun Hewson paints out the board. Sheila clears a desk of thirty-one years and gives him the last fare book. "It's a full one. I'd not throw it out." The forty-one are the parish's now, or the Widow's eleven. He stands at the landing in daylight with the suitcase, and the friend who held is beside him, and the ferry goes out without him. Somebody passing says, "Morning, Tom." The last image: the book under his arm, open at its last written line, "Closed", in her hand.

### Burn Both

Reached when nothing else fits. Shown as two closes.

The Reckoning, for the man who went for both and lost them. "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak." Ellis has stopped asking. The last scene is the first night again: the quay after ten, the lamp, no envelope. The constable turns round. He does not call "You there." He calls, "Nowak!" It is the top of the name ladder from the wrong side: everyone knows who he is. The player has the same two inputs as on night one, stand or run, and the close cuts on the press. Afterwards the walkable town is the Hook on the morning he is bailed: the office shut, Customs' seal on the door, nobody hunting him because he has been had. The last image: Sheila closes the fare book without writing in it and puts it in the drawer with Mickey's.

The Suitcase, for the man who let it drift. No scene of judgement, because nobody thinks enough of him to hold one. A grey afternoon. He walks down the gangway he walked up, with what he can carry. Darren asks him for a pound. Ada is at her window and watches him the whole way, as she did when he came. The last image: Sheila's line. "Ferry landing. One. No charge." It is the same charge Mickey wrote on the night of the fire.

### What the endings settle

Every ending says where the fire page is, who holds the forty-one, who still has a job (Sheila, Ron, Pauline, the drivers), where each rival stands, and what Ada is doing at ten at night. None offers redemption for what was done. Reloading is never stopped.

## 11. Side stories that are whole stories

The thirty-two drafts of 8 November stay as the short ones, found on foot. Eight of them are grown here into whole stories: a person with a want, a turn the player does not see coming, and an end the town keeps. Each runs across acts, brings its people back, and touches the main story without being needed by it. Most of the forty-one are met this way first.

1. The watch (Ron). Found: Mickey's watch in Rita's window the week he died. Want: Ron wants it back and cannot say so. Steps: the ticket; Rita, who will not say who pawned it until it is redeemed; Hal, who knows; the florist's bill. Turn: Ron pawned it himself, for the funeral flowers, because the office was frozen and he would not ask Sheila. He wanted Mickey to have had a proper send-off from somebody. Ends: redeemed and handed back without a word; asked about, and he is ashamed; or left in the window for the street to see. The town keeps: what kind of boss Tom is to a proud man. It decides more of scene 10 than money does.

2. Nothing moves but me (Norman Dent). Found: the watchman on the row, glad of company and a flask of tea. Want: somebody to tell, and never to be asked. Steps, over three acts: his round; the lights in an empty warehouse that are Cammack's crates; the anniversary, when he stops sleeping; Darren's price for what he knows. Turn: Norman did nothing. He went home on his break because his wife was ill and frightened, and he has let a detective believe for a year that he was paid, because the truth costs him the job and the tied cottage. Ends: Tom takes him to Ellis and stands beside him (her case loses its false trail and she owes Tom the knowledge); Tom buys Darren's silence and Norman keeps his row; or Tom uses it, and Norman says what Tom needs said. The town keeps: whether the new owner spends an old man.

3. One way (Karen Grummitt). Light. Found: the ferry's ticket clerk wants a car at five on Thursday, with her case, and not a word to Mrs Rennison, whom she owes a fortnight. Steps: the case turns out to be a case, a wardrobe and a budgerigar. A cab with a wardrobe roped to the roof has to pass Sylvia Rennison's front step, where Sylvia sits in her slippers from four. Turn: Karen does not get on the boat. She stands at the rail she sells tickets for and cannot do it. Ends: he drives it all back, past the step again, and Sylvia says only "Friday means Friday"; or he pays the fortnight and Karen goes; or he tells Sylvia. The town keeps: the sight of the wardrobe, twice, and a postcard that either comes to the ticket window or does not.

4. Two of number nine (the Tangs). Found: Lily Tang remembers Mickey's Friday order. Want: Peter says the shouting at closing time is nothing, as he always does; Lily wants it stopped without a fight on her doorstep. Steps: be at the counter at closing, three nights; the young men are Danny's hangers-on. Turn: Danny Cammack stops it himself, in a sentence, the night he sees Tom standing there, and then tells the street he did. "My dad ate here before you could find it on a map." Ends: Tom lets him have the credit; or says who stood there first; or it was Ron who stood there. The town keeps: who the takeaway thanks, which is who the Hook thinks protects it. Racism here is a fact about the men, never voiced, never rewarded.

5. Lilies (Winifred Duggleby). Found: the chapel flowers. Want: lilies for her husband's anniversary; "nobody gives lilies any more". Steps: the market in Copper Row by a car from the rank; the altar; Sunday. Turn: in a short week she tells him, not knowing who he is in it, that "the bit from the club" did not come, and that she has gone without before and will not make a song of it. Ends: he makes it up from his own tin (Sheila finds it in the count; this is one of the acts that can earn her "Tom"); he does not; or he tells her whose envelope it is. The town keeps: a kindness told after the service, or a woman who now knows where her coal money comes from.

6. The camcorder (Hal Metcalfe). Found: Hal filmed the fireworks on the Fifth from his shop door. Want: Hal wants company on Mondays and has never in his life wanted to be important. Steps: the tape shows Cammack's lads at car two. Thirty seconds later, as the camera swings, it shows the tallyman in Ada's window. Hal has watched the first part and not the second. Turn: Danny, Ellis, Alison and Ada each want the tape, for different halves of it, and each sends Tom or tries to get there before him. Ends: bought, taken, copied, given, or wiped by Hal himself in a panic with Tom standing there. The town keeps: a picture, the one witness that does not forget, in somebody's hands.

7. The master and the building (Philip Danby). Found: a light in the closed school at night. Want: the building kept, for anything at all. Steps: the library list; Agar's surveyor measuring the yard; the hall lent for a meeting; chess. Turn: Philip has had a post inland in his pocket for a month. He is not staying for the building. He is staying because leaving feels like agreeing. Ends: the hall becomes the society's and he stays; the building is sold and he goes, leaving Tom the chess set; or Tom tells him to take the post. The town keeps: whether one more person who could leave the Hook did.

8. Mickey's mooring (Gary Mawer, the Widow, Agar). Found: a mooring at the yard in Mickey's name, three months unpaid, with no boat on it. Want: Gary wants the bill paid; the Widow's man asks about the berth the same week; so does Agar's surveyor. Steps: the yard; the harbour office's forms (Christine Pickering); a night look from the water's edge. Turn: there was never a boat. The mooring is the one berth with a right of way across Mickey's yard to the old basin. Mickey rented it so that nobody else could. It is why the yard matters to everyone. Ends: keep it, sell it to one or the other, or let it lapse and watch them race. The town keeps: which of them now thinks Tom is in the boat trade.

## 12. The scene in the first half hour a player retells

The carry, minute nine, kept as written and finished. Three routes with honest costs: the lit quay, quick and seen; the yard and the lane behind the warehouses, dark and slow; the cab, quickest, with the firm's name on the roof. The constable on his beat can turn round. Next morning the woman at the window asks what he was doing in her alley, and Darren says it to his face.

What was missing is now written: what happens if the constable takes the envelope. He calls out; Tom stands; the constable opens it. The player sees the constable's face, not the contents, then a glimpse: small packets, a name pencilled on each. The constable reads them for longer than a search needs. He keeps the envelope and takes it to the station, because he is honest; Ellis has it by morning. Nobody comes for Tom with a hammer. Sheila, at the count: "Twenty people over the water are short this week. You don't know what that means yet." Ron: "It'll want making good, boss." And in Mickey's debt book, under the five names, Sheila writes a sixth in her own hand: "Nowak, T. £82." The new owner's first week ends with his own name among the debtors, to be cleared the way the others are. A failed carry is the fastest way in the game to learn what the envelope is. Nothing about it is a dead end.

The second retold scene is the tea, minute thirty-nine, kept exactly. On a second playthrough it is a different scene, because the player knows who is pouring.

## 13. Humour and the mix of tones

Grounded noir carries the main line. The comedy is dry, local and attached to people, with seaside-postcard cheek used sparingly. Never satire of the American kind. With no drink in the game the pub's jobs are shared out.

- The radio is the bar. Pauline's commentary goes to every cab at once and is the game's running joke and its chorus. "Car one, the chapel, and the lady says not the way you went last time." "Kevin, wherever you are, you are not in Copper Row, because I can hear the gulls." "Two real minutes, love."
- The caff is the snug. Grace Rinaldi bars, forgives and feeds. Gary Mawer is anybody's for a bacon sandwich. Brenda Laverack does the postcard lines, about fish. "It's not the size of the haddock, love, it's what you do with the batter."
- Darren is the clown with a cost. Forty transistor radios off the back of a lorry, every one of which will only pick up the pirate station, which is playing the same record all week. By Thursday the whole parade can hum it. They were Cammack's.
- Three set pieces built for laughing: Karen's wardrobe (side story 3); the morning Tom takes the radio for Pauline's one hour off and sends a wedding car full of dressed-up grown people to a funeral tea and the hearse party to the registry office steps; and Mr Reese's Tuesday ride-along, in which the most alarming things that happen to a Customs officer in a minicab are all perfectly legal.
- The dry line is everybody's. Sheila: "Sit down, you're making the place look untidy." Agar apologising before every blow. The tallyman's exactness: "You're four minutes early. I'll not hold it against you twice." The Widow paying what is on the meter to the penny. Reese: "I've never found a cab firm that kept a tidy mileage book. I live in hope."

The mix is managed by the evening's own shape: an easy day, a tightening dusk, the job, the relief, the count, and next morning the sting or the laugh. The darkest scenes (the second tea, the two widows, Thursday night) are played quiet and never joked across. The lightest sit in the daytime, in the weeks before the midpoint and again between the hearing and the letter, so that the game has somewhere to fall from.

## 15. How play carries it

- Hands first. Every scene above is a fare, a carry, a round, a tail, a break-in, a fight, a drive or a count, with talk where the consequence lands. The climax week is five nights of jobs behind five mornings of paper.
- Nothing is on rails. Scenes fire on states; the calendar's days and the letters' dates are the town's, not the plot's. Every reveal can be found early by a player who follows the right person.
- No game over. A lost envelope is a debt. A lost fight is Grace Rinaldi's kitchen. A suspended licence is a harder game. A burned book is an ending, shown coming.
- Tom cannot die. A car hurts and never kills. Nobody in this story is killed by the story: a killing is the player's doing, rare and permanent, and the town holds it for ever.
- Guns are rare and late, and come only through people: the dial first moves if Tom takes Ada's answer on the Fifth.
- His line is never offered as a blow.
- Talk decides nothing alone. Every answer that cannot be undone is asked twice.
- No drink, no gambling, no children, anywhere. The bonfire, the wedding car and the bout are grown people only, and nothing is staked on anything.
- What the systems must carry that they do not yet: the Friday round as a carry with twenty doors; Ron's dusk walk as a thing that can be followed; the hearing as testimony drawn from the town's memory; the ride-along; two passengers who talk to each other in the back; five calls. Each is a use of a system the design already has.
