# Contest reading: the player

20 November 2026. A fresh Opus reader (a player of GTA V, RDR2 and KCD2 who plays for story), given BAR.md and the three treatments under shuffled labels and nothing else. For this reader: X was CONTEST-C (The Passenger), Y was CONTEST-A (Twice), Z was CONTEST-B (No Charge). The reader never saw the letters A, B, C. The text below is the reader's own.

## 1. Ranking

- First: X, The Passenger. It is the only one built on a friendship I would actually miss. Its turn makes me replay every driving lesson in my head, and its midpoint loss lands in the chest.
- Second: Z, No Charge. It has the cleverest reversal of the three: the feared outfit is a bookkeeper doing sums, and that rewrites my own first night. But it stacks a second twist on top and ends on a familiar crowd-pleaser.
- Third: Y, Twice. The idea of handing me the crime as an ordinary fare is bold. But the game warns me so loudly beforehand that the Fifth plays like a test I was coached for, and the opponent is too thin to fight.

## 3. Each treatment

X, The Passenger

Best things:
- Ron, the docker who cannot drive. The Sunday lessons at eleven miles an hour are funny when you play them and gutting when you remember them. They pay off in "I can drive, boss. Near enough", and that one line turns the bollard, "Mind that wing", "Not down there, boss" and every unfinished story into something else. That is the Henry and Hans thing BAR asks for. Two men who never ask for anything, eighteen inches apart, is a real relationship, not a buddy routine.
- The Fifth. Ron in the Widow's foreman's coat at the dockers' fire, watching a MICKEY'S effigy burn with his hands in her pockets. Then Pauline on the radio: "Car one. You on your own?" Then Ada using Tom's name for the first time. Those are three images in a row and I would remember all of them. Using Tom's unsaid past as the third name for the box, "T. Nowak", is the smartest use of the frame in any of the three.

Worst faults:
- The gate hut book (section 8, scene 12). It spells out the motive in writing: "Consideration: his silence and mine, 18.11.89, car two". A woman this careful would not write her blackmail down in a hut a man can break into. The biggest reveal of the game arrives as a convenient note to the player.
- The second half's loop and the last input (scene 8, and the final drive in Act III). "Ask each person what they want" turns into a round of NPCs, each with one want to tick off. Pulling the handbrake or leaving it off at the end is meant to be the last word, but the treatment never says what either choice means. As a player I would press it and shrug. Danny and Agar are also thin next to the Widow.

Z, No Charge

Best things:
- There is no outfit. The envelope I carried past the constable was my own takings, and the nineteen pounds of "carrier's money" was Sheila's. The sixty-eight unopened wage packets in a biscuit tin are a proper "go back and look again" turn, and all the clues are fair. Agar as the victim of a fire that never happened, fighting a rumour, is the best antagonist idea in the pile.
- Two scenes. Ada and the Widow in the back of the cab, both looking at the back of Tom's head. And the Friday round driven backwards, with the man who never takes anything knocking on forty-one doors to ask. That is Tom's flaw paid off with my own hands. Ada's line "I'll not be kept by a bookkeeper's wages" gives the whole street the flaw as well.

Worst faults:
- Two twists stacked (section 8, scene 13). Straight after the no-outfit reveal, the warehouse fire turns out to be a watchman's paraffin heater, with a scorched wall behind a calendar. The first twist was earned. The second deflates it, because now nothing anyone feared was ever real, and the stakes start to feel weightless.
- The midpoint and the climax. At the midpoint (section 7, "the count") the big discovery is me reading a fare book at a counter for an insurance assessor. The burnt car is the drama, the book is the reveal, and the reveal is paperwork. The Friday climax (Act III) is the town passing the hat at five to five to save the firm. Strip off the 1990 coat and that is It's a Wonderful Life. I saw it coming once Reese named the sum.

Y, Twice

Best things:
- The wait on the row. Three minutes in the cab, fireworks overhead, Pauline cheerful on the radio, then a low glow in the mirror that is not a firework. That is the strongest single played moment in the three treatments, and it is pure hands, no cutscene. Sheila writing my fare into the book in the morning, word for word the line she wrote for Mickey, is a fine echo.
- Consequence that stays. Darren, bandaged and cheerful, on the ward: "I thought it was you, come to run me home." Danny's line, "Then you're the same as my lock-up. Something of theirs that got used." And Agar: "Your uncle's friends light them for nothing, and I buy what is left." Those are the lines of the whole game.

Worst faults:
- Over-signposted (Act I Sunday, scenes 6 and 7). The tallyman explains last year's fire in week one. Darren tells me he sleeps in the lock-up. Ron says "Just don't take it." After all that, the treatment's claim that burning Darren is "the common first playthrough" does not hold. The midpoint is a deed I was briefed for, not one that ambushes me. The opening paragraph even announces that it ends with the same fare booked a third time.
- The opponent (section 4 and the Act III finale). Cyril is a quiet sixty-three-year-old who does nothing himself and is beaten by having "nothing left to count". There is no mystery that changes earlier scenes, because the treatment drops the mystery on purpose. Ron is a narrator here, not a friend, and the tone runs grim with little that is funny.

## 4. What I would tell a friend to play

X, but only just, and I would say so honestly. "There's a docker in your passenger seat you'll still be thinking about a week later, and a Bonfire Night that hurts." It is the only one where the story is a person rather than a mechanism.

What X still lacks before I would give it a 9:
- a reveal I dig up rather than read in someone's diary;
- a second half with an antagonist who comes at me in more than one way;
- a last input I understand.

## 5. What X could take from the other two without losing its shape

- From Z: Ada and the Widow in the back of the cab. X already has the Widow claiming the front seat. Put Ada in the back with her in the empty-seat stretch, and the two widows look at the back of Tom's head. It costs nothing and is the best scene in Z.
- From Z: the first line in the book. Sheila's "15.10. Ferry landing. One. No charge" entered on the day he came, shown on the Thursday night in her kitchen. X's kitchen scene would be warmer for it, and it sets up the Suitcase ending's last line.
- From Z: the Friday errands as doors. Make X's Friday errands a short round driven backwards, knocking on doors, rather than phone calls. It is the same mechanic but played with the hands, and it hits the "never asks" flaw harder.
- From Y: a better Agar. Give X's Agar Y's line about buying what fires leave, instead of the solicitor gift alone. One speech makes him an antagonist with a motive.
- From Y: Danny's "something of theirs that got used". Give Danny one scene with it in the empty-seat stretch, after the window. X's Danny is a placeholder.
- From Y: the radio in the same voice. If the Bonfire fare to the gate came over the radio in Pauline's ordinary voice, the Fifth would feel even more like an ordinary night going wrong.
- To replace the gate hut note: replace the Widow's written confession with something nearer Y's ward scene. The Bishop's gloved hands are already in X, so let the tally under the seat lead there, and let the Widow say the rest to his face in the passenger seat. Found by play, told by a person, and no convenient diary.
