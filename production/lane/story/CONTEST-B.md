# No Charge

A treatment for LEDGER's story. Spoken lines are samples.

## 1. The shape in a paragraph

Tom Nowak inherits a minicab office that the whole port is afraid of. Mickey's is the last seat of an old dockers' outfit; everybody knows it burned a warehouse last year to teach a tenant a lesson; people pay debts they would not pay anyone else, and three rivals circle a power they take to be real. For three weeks the player lives on that name, and it works. Then, on Bonfire Night, a rival burns one of his cars with a driver asleep in it, and the Hook waits for the old firm's answer. The insurance claim puts the fare book in the player's own hands, and in it he finds fares he drove and was paid for, written in the bookkeeper's small upright hand as "Account. No charge." He follows her. There is no outfit. There has not been one since the dock scheme ended. The envelope he carried past a constable on his first night was his own firm's takings; the forty-one families have been paid for sixteen months by a bookkeeper writing paid fares as free ones, by her own unopened wage packets, and by an old man who drove nights until his heart stopped. The second half of the game is a man with nothing behind him but a name, holding off three rivals who believe in it, while a mild inspector from Customs and Excise adds up twenty-three thousand miles driven for nothing. It ends on a Friday afternoon with the Friday round driven backwards: the man who never took anything from anyone going door to door to ask.

Nobody in this story is secretly the boss, because there is nothing to be the boss of.

## 2. The hours

Act I, the Inheritance (15 to 21 October): 2 hours. Act II, first half, the Name (22 October to 4 November): 4.5 hours. The midpoint, the Fifth and the count: 45 minutes. Act II, second half, the Coat (6 November to 2 December): 5 hours. Act III, the Ledger Comes Due (3 to 14 December): 2.5 hours. Side stories: 2 hours. About 17 hours in all, with fourteen played scenes after week one.

## 3. Tom

His want. Mickey's postscript: "Don't let them call you Mickey's nephew for ever." His own name, said by people who mean it.

His flaw. He lets nobody give him anything. He pays for tea that was on the house and carries alone what two could carry. A man who owes nothing can pick up his suitcase. The offers run all game as one press: take it, or pay for it.

The load the flaw cannot carry. At the midpoint he learns he has been taking since the gangway without knowing it: his rent was made up out of a bookkeeper's purse, his name was lent him by a dead man's reputation, and a woman who will not use that name has gone without her wages for a year so that he could meet his. And he has been giving without knowing it: every night fare he drove has gone into forty-one envelopes. He never chose either. The rest of the game is the choosing. At its end the only way to save the person who did this for him is to do the one thing he cannot do: ask.

His change. Three choices only he can make: what to do with a name he knows is empty (wear it, fill it, or take it off); whether to put his own signature under hers; and whether to knock on forty-one doors with his hand out.

## 4. The people

Eighteen who matter.

- Sheila Dunn, 53, the bookkeeper. As written: "New management", the walk-round, the fare-book rule, the locked door, the withheld name. On Fridays she will not take her wage packet until the others are paid. The player sees her put it in her bag. He does not see where it goes after.
- Ron Kirby, 58, the passenger seat. For twenty years he has fetched the envelope on Sunday nights from the poor box at the old Seamen's Mission and walked the Friday round. "I only fetch it, boss. The gate's old lads fill it. Don't ask me which, I never have."
- Cyril Blenkin, 63, the tallyman at the landing, who takes the far bank's half over the water. He thinks the money comes from Mickey's rackets. "Tell your side it's been light since July."
- Maureen Jensen, the Widow, the port's labour contractor, registered and lawful since the scheme ended. She is the only person in the Hook who knows for a fact that one part of the old outfit pays nothing, because it is hers.
- Ada, 74, the widow of Walter Jensen, who started the dockers' sick club in 1953. Exactly what she seems: on the list, at her window, the best witness on Quay Street. Her tea is tea.
- Geoffrey Agar, 61, the main opponent. Silver, unhurried, apologises before every blow. He owned the warehouse that burned. His insurers have never paid, because the file says "undetermined" and the street says arson; his tenants on the row either pay a weekly "watch" to gangsters or give notice; his loan on the old basin falls due on the thirty-first of December. He believes, sincerely and on good evidence, that Mickey Suddaby's firm burned his building, and he is fighting it the only way a man like him knows: letters, a licence objection, and a telephone call to Customs and Excise. He is right about everything except the facts.
- Danny Cammack, 26. Hook Cars, two yellow cars, £1.50 a ride. Loud on purpose and quicker than he lets on. He has been frightened of the landing for as long as he can remember and has decided to find out what happens if he is not.
- Pauline Kitching, 44, the radio. Terry Hodgson, 52, nights, four words a shift. Kevin Needham, days.
- June Suddaby, 34, Mickey's daughter, night sister at the infirmary.
- Father Brendan Walsh, who buried Mickey and counted who came.
- Norman Dent, the night watchman on the warehouse row, who has a cottage that goes with the job.
- DS Carol Ellis, 44, whose file on the fire was closed as undetermined.
- Alison Sedman, 31, the paper's port reporter, the someone worth being honest for. She has the fire brigade's times for last year and one question: where did an hour go?
- Mr T. A. Reese of Customs and Excise.

## 5. Act I: the Inheritance (2 hours)

The approved first week stands. In order, with what this version plants.

1. Monday. The ferry and the letter ("There is more to it than cabs. There is a book. People are owed"). Sheila's walk-round. The first fare, Father Walsh: "Forty-one envelopes on the plate, and every one of them empty. They wanted him to know they'd come." The debt book: five names, £156. Darren's £22, paid at once, with a look over Tom's shoulder at the office: "I'm not having what that importer had." The first time the player hears what the name is worth.
2. Monday night. Ron, the coat, the envelope, the carry to the landing with three routes and a constable. Cyril's thumb: "Right. Wednesday, same again." Ron counts £19 out of his own coat: "Carrier's money." It is, to the pound, the gap between an honest week's takings and Friday's rent. If the constable takes the envelope, Sheila writes "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book next morning. She knows the sum without being told.
3. Tuesday to Thursday. The debts, the fares, Grace's tea (take it or pay), Terry's flask. Each night the count with Sheila: the tin on one side, the fare book on the other. Ada's question about her alley; her invitation; Wednesday's tea, at which she tells him what the envelope is. "Walter's club. Four pounds. I get mine like the rest. The gate fills it and the row fills it and your uncle moved it. Don't ask me more, I never did." Thursday: Dennis Iveson pays £80 he has owed for two years, in full, the moment he hears the word "row".
4. Friday. Rent to Agar's agents. Wages by hand: Pauline, Kevin, Terry, Ron. Sheila's packet goes into her bag. The round with Ron, twenty doors. On the quay wall, two of number nine. Ron pays.
5. Saturday. Danny on Quay Street, buying chips. "Is it right you've a man comes over the water with a can? Only I've a lock-up." He is laughing, and watching.
6. Sunday. The first lesson and the bollard. Sheila's question: "So which is it going to be?" Wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say. She puts the real book back behind the locked door either way. That night Ron goes to the Mission for Monday's envelope, as he has for twenty years, and if the player rides with him he sees a man put his arm into a poor box up to the elbow in an empty building and come out with a packet. "Told you. I only fetch it."

What the player believes at the end of week one: Mickey's is one seat of an outfit with men over the water, men at the gate and a fire to its name. It feeds forty-one families and frightens everybody else. He is its heir, and so far it has cost him nothing but a walk down a quay.

## 6. Act II, first half: the Name (4.5 hours)

Two weeks. The spine: what a feared name does for a man who has done nothing to earn it. Every scene is somebody giving him something because of the fire. Each is one press, take it or pay, and none looks like an offer.

Scene 1. Lily Tang. Monday. She puts a five-pound note on the counter with his order. "For the watch." Nobody asked. The chandler does the same on Tuesday. Take it, and by Friday six shops are paying a watch that nobody set; Ron shrugs: "They paid Mickey's row seat for years. They think it's you now." Push it back, and Lily says, "Then who do I pay?" and means it. If he took the firm over, this is his first racket, and it walked in on its own legs. If he wound it down, the notes keep coming all the same.

Scene 2. The landing. Cyril, counting with his thumb: "Light again. Twenty this week, not twenty-one. Tell your side it's been light since July of last year." Tom asks Ron who his side is. Ron: "The gate's, I always thought." The Ledger writes an open line: "Who fills the box?"

Scene 3. The gate at six. The Widow books a car and sits in front. Sixty men, twenty picked. Her offer, put once: fifty pence on every fare through the gate. And, getting out: "The gate's put nothing in that box since the scheme went. Sixteen months. Somebody does. When you find out who, come and see me, because I'd like to shake his hand and then I'd like to buy him."

Scene 4. Danny. His number is on the freephone in the ferry terminal. He rides in a Mickey's cab for the pleasure of it and pays a two-pound fare with a fifty: "Keep it. Call it rent." Then two of his lads put the office window in at night and wait a week to see what comes over the water. Nothing does. The player can answer it with his own hands, and the street reads that as the old firm; or not, and Danny notes it.

Scene 5. The hearing, Tuesday 30 October. The licensing sub-committee, referred by a councillor who owes Agar. Agar's solicitor does not mention the dead man's badge. He reads, with regret, what the town says about the warehouse row, and asks whether the applicant's firm is a fit and proper one. Then the references: only people Tom has let do him a turn have written. The name that pays his debts is, in this room, the thing that may sink him. Licensed, licensed with conditions, or refused; none ends the game. In the corridor Agar stops. "I do apologise. It is nothing personal. Your uncle burned my building."

Scene 6. Alison. Her fare to the warehouse row; the umbrella left on the seat. "The brigade were called at ten past eleven. The watchman swore he was on his rounds. Nobody was on that row for the best part of an hour before. I'd like to know where an hour went." He is the heir of the people who did it, as far as either knows, and she is pleasant to him anyway: "I've decided you weren't here."

Scene 7. The second lesson. Sunday 4 November. L-plates, eleven miles an hour, and Ron asks to go round again.

A keen player can end the first half early: sit outside the Mission on a Sunday and see who fills the box before Ron comes. Nothing prompts it. Everything permits it.

What the player believes going into the Fifth: he has the most frightening name in the Hook, three people are testing it, and one question is open in his notebook that nobody he asks can answer.

## 7. The midpoint: the Fifth, and the count

Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990. The quay full of the town's grown people, fireworks off the old coal jetty, the busiest night of the cab year. Tom drives until his hands ache: the best run of fares in the game, among them Mrs Rennison, who books a car to go forty yards with a ham and pays two pounds for it.

The door. A little before eleven, Ron, with blood on his sleeve that is not his. "Boss. They've done car two." It was burned on the rank, under the fireworks. Terry Hodgson was asleep across the back seat between fares. Ron pulled him out. Terry's hands are burned and he will not drive again. There is no mystery about it: Danny's lads did it, Danny says so in the Tangs' queue before midnight ("That's what's coming to the old lot"), and stops saying it when he hears there was a man in the car.

The window. Ada is on her step. She saw them: two lads and a can, and she can name them. "You'd better come in, Tom." Tea. She will say it to the sergeant or she will not, as he asks. Then: "Mickey would have had it answered by Sunday. I'm not telling you to."

The wait. For two days the Hook holds its breath for the old firm's answer. Cyril, at Wednesday's landing: "It wants answering. Send for whoever your uncle sent for." Tom has nobody to send for. Ron, asked: "I never knew who he rang, boss. I only stood on the door."

The count. On Thursday the insurer's assessor wants three months' takings for car two before he will pay a penny. Sheila is at the infirmary with Terry. For the first time the player is alone with the fare book and his own Ledger, at the counter, turning pages. It is played with the hands: find the night, read the line, set it against the notebook. Bonfire Night. Fish quay to Quay Street. In his Ledger: "The ham had a seat. £2." In the fare book, in a small upright hand: "Rennison. Account. No charge." He turns back. Night after night, his own fares, paid in coins he remembers counting, written as free. Every fourth line of his. The tin has been short every night by what the book says he never took.

What the player believes: his bookkeeper is robbing him, on the week his driver lost his hands.

The kitchen. He can face her at the counter, and she will tell him; or say nothing and follow her home on Sunday afternoon, a tail through streets she has walked for thirty-one years. Through the window, or from her own kitchen chair if she lets him in: forty-one small envelopes in rows on an oilcloth, and Sheila filling them with pound coins from the office tin. On the dresser a biscuit tin with the lid off, and in it sixty-eight wage packets with "S. Dunn" on them in her own hand, unopened. At six she puts on her coat and walks to the Seamen's Mission with the far bank's packet and Friday's bag, and puts them in the poor box, and goes home. At eight Ron comes for them.

She tells it once, without turning round from the sink, and it is short. The gate stopped in July of last year. The row's watch brings twenty-six pounds in a good week. Forty-one at four pounds is a hundred and sixty-four. "He drove nights for it. Six nights a week at sixty-three, and I wrote every fare he took as account so the money could go out and nobody would ask where it came from. Then he died at that desk, and I had forty-one people and a nephew I'd never seen. So I gave Ron nineteen pounds to give you for carrying your own takings down a quay, because you'd not have stayed a week if you'd known there was nobody to be frightened of. Nobody would. It's how it has always been paid."

The player's first night was a con, worked on him by the one person in the Hook he wanted to think well of him. The carrier's money was her own. The outfit is a bookkeeper, a tin, and two old men who each think the other is the firm.

## 8. Act II, second half: the Coat (5 hours)

Four weeks. The spine: a name with nothing in it, and three people who must not find out or must be told. Every scene asks the same thing in a new way: wear the coat, fill it, or take it off. One car fewer means the envelopes cannot be filled by fares alone from now on, so the question is never idle.

Scene 8. The ward. Visiting hour. Terry, hands bound, uses five words for the first time in the game: "Tell Pauline it don't count." June is the night sister. "He drove nights at sixty-three. I used to see car one go past the infirmary at four in the morning and think, what is he frightened of. I'd check what you're getting, I said. I didn't know what it was either." She did not know. She does now, if he tells her.

Scene 9. Telling Ron. The player chooses whether, and where. Told, Ron says nothing and asks for the car. It is the third lesson and it is not funny: he drives the length of the dock road without a word, stops, handbrake on. "Twenty year I've put my arm in that box. Who've I been frightened of, then, boss?" And, after a while: "Who've I been frightening?" Not told, he goes on fetching from the Mission, and the player watches him do it.

Scene 10. The answer. The one job in the game planned like a job, on the board in the office, with a week to plan it. The Hook expects men from over the water. There are none. Three keys.

- Stage it. Tapper and Shiner, two of Ron's old gang off the fish dock, go over on the morning boat in borrowed car coats and come back on the last ferry as strangers with a holdall. Car one meets them with its light off and drives them, slowly, down Quay Street, past the Tangs' queue, to the dead end of the warehouse row, where they stand and look at Danny's lock-up for five minutes and are driven back to the landing. Nothing burns. The planning is who sees what: the ticket clerk must not be on, the route must pass the right windows, and Shiner must not speak, because half the Hook knows his voice. Done well, Danny empties his lock-up that night. Done badly, the ticket clerk tells him by Friday who the two strangers were.
- Make it true. Burn the lock-up with his own hands, or send Ron's gang with a can. Then the fire the firm was feared for is, for the first time, a fire it lit.
- Do neither. Take Ada to the sergeant. Then the street learns that Mickey's nephew answers arson with a statement, and every note left on a counter "for the watch" stops.

Scene 11. Remembrance Sunday, 11 November. The dockside memorial. Father Walsh reads the names: the wars, and the men the docks have taken since; this year, Michael Suddaby. Everybody is in one place for the only time: Ada, Cyril with his cap off, the Widow, Danny at the back, Ellis, June, Sheila, and Agar laying the Harbour Board's wreath. Two minutes of silence in which the player chooses only where Tom looks. If he looks at Agar, Agar is looking at him, with the face of a decent man looking at the people who burned his property.

Scene 12. Two widows. Ada asks to be driven. She has not been in a car in four years. "The dock gate." She means to tell Maureen Jensen that the gate is paying short into Walter's club. Ron is in the front. The Widow gets in at the gate, and the two of them sit in the back of a minicab on the dock road in the dark with the meter running and Tom's eyes in the mirror. Ada says her piece. Maureen: "I've not put a penny in that box for sixteen months." A long quiet. Ada: "Then who has?" Both of them look at the back of Tom's head. He may speak once. If Ada learns the truth, here or later, she says the thing the straight road has to climb over: "A club is a thing you're in. A charity is a thing done to you. I'll not be kept by a bookkeeper's wages." And the next Friday she hands her envelope back on the step. By Sunday eleven more have. The forty-one will take money stolen from the docks by men they knew. They will not take a gift. The flaw is not only Tom's. It is the Hook's.

Scene 13. Where an hour went. Sunday 18 November, a year to the day. The player has two things that have never been set side by side. Alison's times: brigade called at ten past eleven. And the 1989 fare book, on the shelf behind Sheila's chair since the walk-round, where he can find, in the small upright hand, squeezed in under the day's last fare: "18.11. 23.40. Car two. Office to warehouse row and return. One. Wait. No charge." Mickey drove out to the row half an hour after the alarm, stood, and drove back. He went to look. The line that the street would call a noose is the firm's alibi, and Sheila wrote it down because Mickey would not: "He said, let them think it. The parade paid on the Monday without being asked. I said, every fare goes in the book." The hour itself is found on the row: a night watching the watchman from the parked cab. Norman Dent goes home to his cottage for an hour on his break, as he did that night; and in his hut there is a new paraffin heater, and behind the calendar a scorched wall. He will say it once, to a man who has sat a night with him: his old heater, his hour away, his job and his cottage. Nobody burned the warehouse. An old man let the street blame gangsters, the gangsters let it, and a landlord has spent a year at war with a rumour.

So the player holds the truth about the fire, and it is a weapon that cuts three ways. Given to Agar's insurers, it pays Agar, ends his war and costs Norman his cottage. Given to Alison, it is a worse story for her than the one her editor wants, and she must choose whether to print the dull truth; she asks Tom, as the one person whose answer she will take, what he wants said about his uncle. Kept, it leaves the name frightening, the watch paid and Agar coming.

Scene 14. Danny finds out. However the answer went, Danny is the one who works it out, because he is the only one who ever tested it. He gets into car one. "Nobody came, did they. There's nowt there. There's you, and her with the book, and an old lad on a door." He can be bought, fought or told the rest. Fought: Saturday 1 December, the old hall behind the chandler's, gloves on, the Hook on benches. Told the rest, including whose envelopes Tom's night fares fill, he is quiet. His own father is on the list. "My dad's had four quid a week off a woman doing sums. I've been at war with a pencil."

Scene 15. Agar in the rain. Geoffrey Agar walks down Quay Street for the first time in his life, without a hat, and knocks at the office. He does not threaten. The bank has named the day. He asks the one thing he has wanted to know for a year: "Did your uncle burn my building? I should like to hear it from one of you before I am finished." The player knows the answer. Tell him, and show him the line; lie, and keep him frightened; or let him go back up the street not knowing. Whatever is said, Agar has already made a telephone call.

The act ends on a letter: courteous, dated, signed T. A. Reese.

## 9. Act III: the Ledger Comes Due (2.5 hours)

Why he comes: a registered trader has died and the name on the form has changed; and a gentleman has rung to say the firm's takings are not what they seem. He is right about that too.

Monday 10 December. Reese at the counter with the book since October. "A fare at no charge is either a kindness or a hole, Mr Nowak. I'm paid to ask which. You have rather a lot of them."

Tuesday. The ride-along. Reese in car one with his flask, watching what a no-charge fare looks like. Winifred to the chapel, free, and he sees Tom carry her bag. Brenda and the crabs: "Are those declared, Mrs Laverack?" "They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in." Then a man who pays, and Reese watches whether it is written.

Wednesday. Fuel and mileage, 1988 to 1990. "Mileage is a truthful thing. Your cars earn fourteen pence a mile on one line and nothing on the next, and it is always the night car." Sheila stands the whole time.

Thursday. He closes the book and says it without looking at either of them. "Twenty-three thousand miles at no charge since July of last year. Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak." Then the sum: tax of £1,304 on takings that were never declared. "If that, and a penalty I may set myself, is on this counter by five tomorrow, nineteen hundred pounds in all, I am able to call it carelessness. If it is not, it is suppression, and it goes along the corridor with the book and the name of the hand it is in." He does not look at Sheila. He is sorry, truly.

Thursday night. Sheila's kitchen. She is writing a statement in the small upright hand: "I, Sheila Dunn, kept the fare book of M. Suddaby." She means to take all of it. Then she turns the 1990 book to its first page under the new owner and shows him one line he has never read: "15.10. Ferry landing. One. No charge." She entered him the day he came. "You were the last thing he sent for. It wanted writing down that somebody came, and it wasn't for money. Nobody reads a fare book." There is a pen on the table. He can let her sign alone; sign under her as the proprietor, so that it is his; or tear it up and find nineteen hundred pounds.

Friday. The phone box on the corner, five coins, and until five. He can ring only people he once let give him something. There are three keys to nineteen hundred pounds.

- Ask. Each answered call is a street of the Friday round, driven backwards. He knocks on the doors he has carried envelopes to and asks old dockers' families for money, to their faces, for the woman who has been paying them. Whether they open depends on everything: what he did about the watch, what he told Ada in the cab, whether he ever let Winifred give him a flower. Ron walks it with him or does not. The constable from the first night stops him on the lit quay with a bag: "What's in it, Nowak?" This time he opens it himself.
- Sell. Agar will write a cheque for the yard, for exactly the sum; the Widow will buy the firm and leave his name on the roof; Danny will lend it, with a hook.
- Pay it from the tin, if after the midpoint he filled the coat and has rackets that earn. Reese: "I shan't ask where a cab firm found this in a week, Mr Nowak. Somebody else will."

At five to five Agar comes down Quay Street in daylight to see the old firm finished, and stands in the office door. What is on the counter decides what he sees. If the player asked, and the Hook answered: forty-one envelopes in rows, none of them empty, and more behind them; Father Walsh with the plate; Terry holding one between his wrists; Ada, who has brought her own back. Reese counts it with his propelling pencil. He will not take the cup of tea Sheila has made him every day that week: "I'm not permitted, Mrs Dunn." When he has finished counting, he takes it. "That all seems to be in order, Mr Nowak." Agar, who can now be told or shown what he has spent a year fighting, stands in the rain a second time.

## 10. The central turn, and why it is fair

The turn is that the outfit does not exist and the bookkeeper is its keeper. What it changes: the first night's carry, which was his own money and her test; the nineteen pounds; "New management", said by the only management there has been; the withheld name, kept back by a woman who could not afford to like him; the priest's forty-one empty envelopes; Mickey dead at a desk at ten to six with the kettle on; "People are owed", which never once meant her; and every note left on a counter for a watch nobody set.

Why it surprises. A reader of crime stories is watching the widow at the window, the tallyman and the dead uncle for the hidden hand. All three are exactly what they seem, and the firm's one famous crime never happened. The landlord is not the arsonist; he is the victim. The power everyone fears is a woman doing sums.

Why it is fair. Sheila's wage packet goes in her bag every Friday on screen. The carrier's money is the rent gap to the pound and comes out of Ron's coat. She knows the sum in a lost envelope without being told. Ron only fetches; Cyril says "your side"; the Widow says the gate pays nothing; the Ledger has asked "Who fills the box?" since the second week. The tin has been short every night by an amount the player could have added up. And the ham had a seat, and paid two pounds.

## 11. The endings

States, never a menu. Each has a last scene, a line Sheila writes in the fare book and a line Tom writes in his Ledger.

The Kingdom. He filled the coat. The lock-up burned by his word, the watch is collected and not left on counters, there are men who really do come over the water now, and the books hold because a front does the work Sheila's pencil did. Last scene: the landing after ten. A carrier in a coat that does not fit him walks the envelope down the lit quay; the constable looks at the coat and looks away. The tallyman asks, for the first time, "Nowak's?" Now there is an outfit, built by a stranger out of a rumour. Above the office, a figure at the flat window. Ron gets out of the passenger side of a cab on the rank and stands by the door. Sheila takes her wage packet on Fridays now, like the others, and says "Goodnight, Mr Nowak." On the better turn (one friend counts him, the street does not hold his rackets as fact, nobody was in a building he burned) she stays past the quarter. Fare book: "Landing. Account. No charge." Ledger, in a hand that has started to look like Mickey's: "41 out. T.N."

The Straight Life. He took the coat off. The better turn: he asked, the counter filled, and the club is a club again. Friday, the chapel hall: the Quay Street Friendly Society, members paying in fifty pence and drawing four pounds, a book anybody may read, Edith with the pen, Ada's signature first because it is a thing she is in. The firm declares what it gives. Julie Kettlewell has the cars; Terry has the night desk and the radio, wearing a headset he cannot put on himself. On the last evening Tom gets into the back of car one. Julie drives. Ron is in the front passenger seat, by habit. "Where to?" "Just round." At the office Sheila writes the fare herself, and takes his two pounds, and puts it in the tin. "Mind how you go, Tom." Fare book: "Nowak, T. Round the Hook. £2. Paid." Ledger: "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four." The plain turn: he sold to pay, the board is painted out or reads somebody else's name, Sheila clears a desk of thirty-one years, and somebody passing says, "Morning, Tom." Fare book: "Closed." Ledger: "Stayed."

Burn Both. The Reckoning, for the man who let her sign alone or found nothing by five: "It goes along the corridor, Mr Nowak. I am sorry." Sheila is asked for her keys by a constable half her age and hands them over without looking at Tom. Saturday night is the first night again, the quay, the lamp, and the constable does not call "You there." He calls, "Nowak!" Stand or run; the close cuts on the press. Fare book: closed unwritten. Ledger: the last page cut out. The Suitcase, for the man who let it drift: a grey afternoon, the gangway he came up, the gate's van passing with Ron in the passenger seat, not looking across. The fare book's last line, in her hand, is the line she wrote the day he came, written again: "Ferry landing. One. No charge." Ledger: a date, and nothing under it.
