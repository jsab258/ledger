# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 7: a player's review

I have finished GTA V, Red Dead 2 and KCD2, and I play for the story. I am judging this as fifteen to twenty hours with a pad in my hands, not as a document.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7 out of 10. It has one great friendship, one very good opponent and a mystery I would solve with a steering wheel instead of a menu. But the middle is thin in places, the last act is mostly paperwork, and the twist can be guessed.

1. Tom: 7. The flaw of never asking and never taking is clever because I play it: every free cup of tea is a press, and so is the "Asked" page. But he is mostly a man who says no, and his change shows up as Ledger lines more than as moments where I feel him turn.

2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Ada, Pauline and Terry are people I would talk about afterwards. The Bishop's one speech at Ropery Lane is worth the trip. Kevin and Darren are useful but you have met them before.

3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is the best thing on this list after Ron. She keeps every deal, never lies, stands in the road with her hands in her pockets, and sits in my friend's seat. Danny is a noisy nuisance until his father turns up, and Agar works mostly through letters and a file he leaves on a car seat.

4. The central mystery: 8. A seat that sticks, a tally in the runner, and a route I drive myself that comes to seven miles when it should be eleven. That is a mystery I solve with my hands, and it changes the first scene, the fare book rule and every "good morning". It loses a point because the key finds are optional and nothing catches a player who misses them.

5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The ferry, the letter, the walk-round, the priest's forty-one empty envelopes and the carry past a constable make a strong first evening. Lesson one at the bollard is the scene people will retell. The first week is busy with numbers (£23.50, £156, £38, £19) and could lose one or two.

6. Structure and pace: 6. The three movements and the Bonfire Night midpoint are well shaped. But Act II runs nine and a half hours against a two-and-a-half-hour Act III that is crammed, and a lot of the turns depend on optional scenes the player may never trigger.

7. The middle: 6. The weekly table is a good idea, and the falling round (twenty doors, then nineteen, then eighteen) is lovely. Too many of the dusk jobs are vague, though ("a rent review: who pays, and who is helped" is not something I do with my hands), and "take it or pay" happens so often that it turns into a tic.

8. Surprise against cliché: 7. Sheila at the wheel is fair and well hidden in plain sight. But "the one person you trusted most did it" is a known shape, and Ada names Sheila among the people Mickey taught to drive on Bonfire Night, which is when a sharp player will clock it.

9. The endings: 7. Ending with which seat Ron sits in is a real idea, and "You never asked" in the Straight Life is the best line in the bible. Ellis charges everybody in every ending, so even the best one puts Sheila and Ron in the dock. The Reckoning cuts back to the first night's stand-or-run, which is a fine echo.

10. The side stories: 7. The sergeant's mother and the chapel money is a proper story with a turn on both sides, and the watch has a sting after scene 15. The umbrella is good. One Way is a sketch.

11. Tone and humour: 6. The radio as the bar is a good call, and Terry's fifth word and the crabs made me smile. Apart from the lessons and the gate lifted off its hinges, most of the humour is dry lines, not set pieces I would laugh at with the pad down.

12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The dial, the handbrake that has meant "somebody else goes, and I stop them" all game, the seat repair, the phone box that only friends answer, and the hearing filled by people I let do me a turn are all story carried by play. It falls short where the last act becomes reading folders.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The middle drifts into admin and repetition. Where: section 7, the weekly table, and the offers in section 3. The 19 November dusk job is a rent review, and the eleven who will not sign is a conversation. On top of that, every other scene ends in "take it or pay". Why I would feel it: this is most of the game, and the KCD2 crowd who complain about the same loop do so at exactly this point. By week four I would be pressing "pay" without reading, the same way I skim the twentieth fetch quest. Fix: make every dusk job a verb for the hands. In Agar's week, I clear a tenant's stock out of a shop before his agents lock it, at night, against the clock. Cut the counted offers to about a dozen named ones and make each come back visibly in a later scene, so the press stays heavy.

2. The mystery can be guessed, and it is fragile. Where: the Fifth (Ada's list includes "Sheila Dunn"), scene 10 (the seat only comes out if I choose to mend it), and scene 12 (the eleven miles only gets driven if I think of it). Why I would feel it: once Ada names her I am watching Sheila for six hours, and the "everyone suspects Ron" misdirection weakens. And a player who never mends the seat reaches Sheila's kitchen, where the bible says "the player already knows" when he does not. Fix: take Sheila off Ada's list and leave her only in June's count in scene 15, where it lands as confirmation. Then add a backstop. In scene 17 Ellis has car two's seat out herself, looking at the wing, so every player has the tally in his hand before Reese arrives.

3. The climax is a desk and a dashboard. Where: Act III, Monday to Friday of the inspector's week. Why I would feel it: after GTA heists and Arthur's last ride, my big finale is a VAT officer reading two lines aloud, a kitchen, a phone box and a slow drive with a clipboard in the back. The drive itself is beautiful. Everything around it is talk, and the last fight with Danny at Ropery Lane feels bolted on. Fix: put the hands on the Friday route. Danny's yellow cars block the dock road on the way to the bollard. Who I kept decides whether the road is cleared, and in what state the car arrives. That makes the last drive the last fight, and Thursday becomes quieter.

4. Danny and Agar are thinner than the Widow. Where: section 5, scene 6, scene 17, and the week of the letter. Agar comes at me by post and then leaves his evidence on my back seat with a speech. Danny's father at the gate ties things up too neatly, and his worst act happens to Julie offscreen. Why I would feel it: this is the GTA V complaint again, with villains far thinner than the people they press on. A rival who hands you the key file is not a rival. Fix: make Agar stand in the rain at the yard with his own padlock, apologising, and make the file something I take, by lifting it from the surveyor's office in a break-in, not something he gives. Let me be driving car three when Danny crowds it into the wall, so the harm happens in my hands.

5. The road of the player who refuses the crime is a table. Where: section 11. Six one-line weeks of names, a pay-out, a hall, an audit, more asking and Ada. Why I would feel it: the bar says no stretch may be thin, including this road. As written, a straight player gets the best scenes second-hand and a middle of forms, a registrar and Edith Stockill's finger. Fix: grow two of the weeks into played scenes the size of the carry. The pay-out on foot with Danny's lads behind should get its own routes and costs. The hall on Bonfire Night should be held with sixty stacked chairs as a real set piece, ending with Ada walking half the room out. Edith's "What's this, dear?" should start the same seat and dial hunt as the crooked road.

## 3. Would I single it out to a friend?

No. Not yet. I would tell a friend about Ron learning to drive at eleven miles an hour with his knees at his ears, and about the moment he brakes by himself at the mouth of the row and says "Still where she left it". That is a Henry and Hans level of memory. But I would not tell him to play the game for the story, because I would have to warn him about the middle weeks and an ending that plays out over a counter. At the moment it is a very good spine with a soft belly. Fix the middle and the climax and it could reach yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron in the passenger seat, and the three Sunday lessons. Naming every building by what it was, the stories that stop one sentence later each time, "I go, you stop", and his last-act lie told at eleven miles an hour for the woman who left him. This is the heart of the game.

2. The eleven miles on a dial. A mystery solved by driving a route and reading a mileometer, set up by "every fare goes in the book" and a seat that sticks, paid off by a VAT officer who says "Mileage is a truthful thing". I have not seen this anywhere else, and it is pure LEDGER.

3. The Widow and her rule. She never threatens, keeps every deal and never lies when asked straight: "Yes. You didn't ask before." She stands in the road, I cannot touch her, and she takes my firm apart by asking people what they want. A villain who is partly right and never cheats is rare, and she has to stay exactly that clean.
