# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 3: a critic's review

I measure game stories against two. Kingdom Come: Deliverance II made me care about a friendship over sixty hours. Red Dead Redemption 2 made a whole ending out of the sum of one man. This bible has one idea worth putting beside them: a passenger seat, and who sits in it, as the verdict on a friendship. Most of the rest is good work that I would admire and then forget.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7. A careful, honest, often lovely story whose central secret shows its hand in the first two hours. Its best surprise is quiet, and the climax rests on a kind man with a pencil.

1. Tom: 8. The flaw is played rather than stated: the offers, the question, the packed suitcase. That is the cleverest thing in the document. He changes from a man who asks nothing to a man who asks, and the player does the changing. He is still mostly a gap the others talk into, and in the climax his agency comes down to which name goes on a form.

2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Ada, Pauline and Terry are people, and each has a want and a line I would quote. Shiner, who cannot stop talking and whose talking is the plot, is a fine piece of design. Kevin and Julie are thinner. Twenty-one who matter is a lot to keep alive in sixteen hours.

3. The antagonists: 7. Maureen Jensen is a rare thing, an opponent who wins by asking. Agar's single scene with the hat is the best-written page in the bible. But the Widow never changes and never loses anything she cares about. In Act III all three are reduced to obstacles: a lane full of yellow cars, a padlock, a standing offer. That is the very GTA V complaint the bar names.

4. The central mystery: 7. The clueing is fair and physical, and driving seven miles where the book says eleven is the best detective scene I have read in a game bible. But the first answer, that Ron drove, is plain by the end of week one. The deepest truth, that Mickey planned the theft, has no scene where the player finds it.

5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The carry is a real retold scene, and Sheila writing "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book is a superb sting. The first lesson with the bollard is a second retold scene, and better than most games' first hours. Monday alone has nine beats, though, and Ada's tea is a lecture on the club.

6. Structure and pace: 7. The three movements of Act II are well shaped and the Fifth is a proper midpoint. Firing scenes on game states, with clues that depend on each other (scene 10 before 11 before 12), will be hard to keep in order without quietly putting the story on rails.

7. The middle: 7. The weekly table is the right answer to "the same loop over and over", and the Empty Seat movement has real ache. Six weeks of fare, dusk job and count is still one loop with different props. The bible names who comes back each week, not what changes in the player's hands.

8. Surprise against cliché: 6. The bible says it is fair, and it is. But "the friend who can't drive did the driving" will be guessed by anyone who has played a crime story. "He drove away and came back" is a moral nuance, not a turn, and the blackmail the player supplies for himself only works if he supplies it. The inspector who owns the night is the real surprise, and it lands late.

9. The endings: 8. Every close has a last image and a seat, and The Suitcase, with the ferry line written again, is the equal of anything in KCD2's finale. Reese recommending that nothing be done about a fire in a bonded store is too kind to believe, and it lets the best ending off lightly.

10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and her daughter, each shielding the other from a thing both know, is a whole story with a real turn. Alison's is good. The watch is a find, a return and a footnote. Karen's wardrobe is a sketch.

11. Tone and humour: 7. "The ham had a seat." "They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in." Terry's fifth word. These are actually funny, and they come from situations, not quips. The six set pieces are well spread. The noir side is quieter than it is gripping.

12. Story and play feeding each other: 9. This is the story's reason to exist. The mileometer as evidence, the handbrake as trust, take-or-pay as character, a hearing filled only by people you let help you, the dockers lifting a gate off its hinges. Nothing here is a cutscene pretending to be a game.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The answer is visible in hour one. What is wrong: Ron "cannot drive", Sheila stares at car two's wing on the walk-round, the bollard carries the firm's paint, and Ada mentions the coat with no sleeves, all in week one. Where: section 6, scenes 3 and 7, and the first Sunday's lesson. Why a player feels it: a player who guesses the driver at hour two then spends nine hours watching the game catch up, which is exactly the "ending guessed in the first hour" the bar punishes. Fix: hold back the sleeves until Ada's tea on the night of the Fifth, where she is already turning Ron over in her mind. Cut Sheila's look at the wing and let Shaun's "second time" in scene 10 be the first sign. Give Kevin a real unexplained night in the 1989 books, so the suspicion has a second place to go and does not fall straight on Ron.

2. Mickey's guilt is never found. What is wrong: section 10.2 says Mickey planned the theft and meant to drive. That is the truth that turns the letter ("people are owed") from a kindness into a confession, and no played scene uncovers it. Where: sections 8 and 10. Why a player feels it: the dead uncle stays a saint in play, the letter never changes meaning, and the bar's "truth that changes what earlier scenes meant" is only half met. Fix: on the Thursday of the last act, the Bishop's account includes it ("Your uncle was to drive. He'd the route on a fag packet"), and the packet is in the flat for a player who looks: Mickey's hand, three names, twenty minutes. It costs one line and one prop.

3. Ron's reason for the coat does not hold up. What is wrong: Shiner has already said it in the Tangs' queue. Moving him to the night gate cannot unsay it, and Ron could simply have told him to shut up. Where: section 7, scene 13 and section 10.7. Why a player feels it: the second big turn is the emotional heart of the story, and a thinking player will ask "why not just talk to him?" at the moment he should be moved. Fix: make it a gift and a gag at once. The Widow was about to drop Shiner from the gate for his mouth, and he is past the age anyone else would hire him. A foreman can keep him in work and away from listeners. Ron asks for the coat to save his friend's living and his own secret in one stroke, which is also him paying something off.

4. The last input punishes a habit the game trained. What is wrong: three lessons teach the player that his job is to pull the handbrake. On the Friday, pulling it means he does not trust Ron. Where: section 8, Friday, and the closing paragraph on the handbrake. Why a player feels it: a player who pulls by reflex gets the lesser reading of the best scene in the game and will call it a trick, not a verdict. Fix: Ron changes the arrangement as he gets in, aloud, for the first time: "I go, I stop, boss." The player hears that the rule has changed, and leaving the brake alone becomes a choice he makes rather than a test he fails.

5. The wind-down road is a sketch. What is wrong: the player who refuses the crime gets "the same eighteen scenes, read differently" plus three jobs and Edith's fountain pen. Where: section 11. Why a player feels it: the bar demands that this road be as strong as the other, and "read differently" is a promise, not scenes. Without the carry and the round, the first movement loses its hands. Fix: give the road its own retold scene to match the carry. The society's first pay-out, the holdall on foot with Danny's lads behind him, should be fully staged with routes and costs. Write two of the eighteen scenes outright for this road: the hearing, where the references come from the society's names, and Ada's "means-tested" refusal turning to her signature.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review

No. Not yet. I would single out the passenger seat and the mileometer as the smartest marriage of story and driving I have seen planned. But a review that sends people to a game for its story needs a mystery they cannot see through and an ending that costs what it should. Here the first is guessed early and the second is softened by an officer's mercy. Fix the first two weak points above and I would think again.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron and the seat. The buildings named by what they were, the stories that stop one sentence later each time, and the last image in every ending being which seat he is in. This is the Henry and Hans of the piece, and it is told with the hands.

2. The eleven miles. The fare book's two lines, the dial reading seven, the four missing miles, Reese in the back seat with his flask. It is the fare book as alibi and noose, paid off on the road rather than at a desk.

3. Take or pay. The offers mechanic, with nothing to mark a gift from a hook, and the hearing filled only by those Tom let help him. It turns a hero's flaw into a system the player lives in, and it is the reason the dockers on the public bench will make people put the pad down.
