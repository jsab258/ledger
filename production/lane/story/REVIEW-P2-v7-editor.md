# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 7: a script editor's note

A serious piece of work. A mileometer that tells the truth about a night nobody will talk about is the best idea I have seen in a crime game in years, and Ron Kirby is a man people will talk about afterwards. But the last turn is told when it should be found, Act III is overloaded, one opponent runs on a stock deadline, and one branch breaks the scenes after it.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7.

1. Tom: 7. His want (the postscript), his flaw (he will not take and will not ask), his fear (the packed suitcase) and his change are all played, which is rare. Even so he is mostly a camera, and at the climax his right move is to do nothing. That is elegant on paper and risks feeling hollow in the hand.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Ada, Pauline and Terry each have a voice and something they are carrying. Ivy and Ellis, each protecting the other from a thing both of them know, is the finest small relationship in the bible.
3. The antagonists: 6. The Widow is a real opponent with a rule, a body and a price, and she comes at him four ways. Danny's father earns him a late depth, but Agar is a landlord with a bank deadline and letters, and his Act III motive is muddled.
4. The central mystery: 8. It is fair. The seat, the tally, the struck repairs and the dial are all planted early and all physical. It falls short of 9 because the last step, who drove, comes out of Ron's mouth and June's fingers and not out of play.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 7. The ferry, the walk-round, the first fare with forty-one empty envelopes and the carry at minute nine are strong. Act I is still two hours of "approved beats", heavy on talk, and the second retold scene (lesson one) needs the quick-or-slow handbrake to land as written.
6. Structure and pace: 6. The three movements of Act II are well shaped and the midpoint is a true reversal. Act III jams two weeks, Agar's whole subplot, the kitchen, two doors, a fight, the drive and the sergeant into two and a half hours.
7. The middle: 7. The week table is the right answer to the same-loop complaint: a returning fare, a dusk job, a count, and a round that shrinks. The Empty Seat leans on the count and on witnesses explaining things, so it is close to desk play for long stretches.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. The VAT man, the bonfire effigy, the driving lessons and the eleven miles are fresh. Against them sit a cold-case sergeant who "has not stopped", a reporter with one question, a landlord with a loan due on the thirty-first, and the trusted figure who did it.
9. The endings: 8. Ron's seat as the verdict is a superb organising image, every close has a last scene and a written line, and the better Kingdom is moving without being sweet. Agar goes missing from all of them, and the plain turns are thin beside the better ones.
10. The side stories: 7. The watch and the sergeant's ride have real turns and they feed the main line. One Way is a sketch, and the Umbrella is a love interest dressed as a theme.
11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline, Terry's fifth word, the crabs and the gate off its hinges are funny because they come from people. There is too little of it in Act III, and the dockers barely swear.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The offers, the "Asked" page, the handbrake, the seat and the dial are story made of inputs. Talk is where consequences come back. At its best this is exactly the brief.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The final turn is told, not found. In Act II, scene 15, Ron says "Still where she left it" and June counts on her fingers. In section 10 the bible boasts that the truth is uncovered by play, and for the car going and coming back it is. Who drove, though, is given to the player by two people talking. It is the revelation the game rests on, and the bar asks for a truth uncovered by play. The fix: one more physical clue within reach before scene 15, so Ron's word confirms rather than reveals. Mickey's flat (Act I, Monday, beat 5) could hold his old daybook: "S.D. passed. First go. 1961." Or the "Asked" page lets the player ask Sheila who drove car two that night, and she, who never says what is not so, goes on writing.

2. The asked-first branch breaks the scenes after it. The midpoint's alternative and "If Ron never left" both say everything after plays as written. It cannot. Scene 13 depends on Shiner spending nine nights on the night gate because Ron put him there. Scene 14 depends on Maureen saying Ron asked for the coat. Scene 15 opens with Tom holding out the keys at the gate. The player who did the right thing early meets broken scenes. The fix: write the three variants. Shiner confesses when Kevin's name is in the queue; Maureen says Ron turned her down twice; the third lesson begins from the passenger seat.

3. Act III is overloaded and talks too much. In section 8 the reader gets Agar's fare, the yard, Reese's counter scene, the ride-along, the old books, the kitchen, two doors with a fight, the eleven miles and Ellis's verdict. Reese makes four speeches of three or four sentences each. The bar punishes a hurried last act and long talk, and LEDGER is not story-first. The fix: move Agar's fare and the yard into the week of 26 November, so Act III is the inspector's week alone; cut Reese to two lines a speech and let the drive carry the rest.

4. Agar is thin and his last move does not add up. In section 5 he has a bank deadline, and in Act III he informs Customs, then hands Tom the watchman's statement and offers him a solicitor as "a gift". Once he has told the Crown, the file is no leverage; an opponent acting against his own interest is thin. The fix: the letter is a dated, unsent draft and the telephone call was anonymous; the price is the option on the yard for the letter going in the grate. Then say where he ends in every close.

5. Reese's power is a convenience. In section 8, Wednesday, he says he "may close a bond night myself, on my own report". A VAT control officer would not hold that power over an excise matter, and the line "the department that was robbed" overstates it, since nothing left the bond. This matters because a period crime drama lives on its procedure being believed, and the climax turns on this man's discretion. The fix: make Reese a former warehouse officer who knew that bond, now on VAT. Keep his recommendation as only a recommendation, which the Friday scene already half does. Change "robbed" to "the department whose lock it was".

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Pauline has "nine years without a Saturday off" yet "had gone off at ten, as she did every Saturday". Fix: she finishes at ten on Saturdays and has never had one off.
2. A driving licence from 1961, never used, would have lapsed. Licences before 1976 ran for three years. Fix: she renewed it by post every time out of habit, and after 1976 it ran to seventy.
3. Ron held no licence of any kind in November 1989, so the "R. Kirby" lie dies on one check with the licensing centre at Swansea. Fix: Reese says so aloud, and writes it down anyway.
4. While Sheila drove, nobody was on the radio or in the office, and Terry, coming back from the infirmary, would have found car two gone. Fix: Terry stayed on the ward with Mickey till morning, at Sheila's word on the radio.
5. The asked-first branch: scenes 13 to 15 assume Ron is at the gate. Fix: write the variants, as in point 2 above.
6. Section 9 promises that each ending says where the three opponents stand, and Agar is in none of them. Fix: one line for him in each.
7. Mickey's operator's licence lapsed when he died, so the firm itself is operating unlicensed, not only Tom's badge. Fix: the 30 October hearing covers both the operator's and the driver's licence.
8. A cabbie who died at his desk would not be read among the dead at a war memorial. Fix: Father Walsh names him in the prayers afterwards.
9. Under PACE in 1990 a custody officer bails, not the investigating sergeant. Fix: "the custody sergeant bails them on her word".
10. Severe burns treated at home by a ship's surgeon are not believable. Fix: a week later he went to a hospital over the water, "a stove", under his sister's married name.
11. Six-foot Kevin drives car two daily with the seat jammed for a year and never looks under it. Fix: in Act I, he tried with a bar and Sheila told him to leave Mickey's seat be.
12. Ada's "first time anyone has used his name" follows "Mr Nowak" from Sheila and the Widow. Fix: "his first name".
13. The thirty cut pages promise a past the frame forbids paying off. Fix: Ada notices them once and lets it go, so the player stops waiting.
14. Making Sheila the driver changes a character the frame says to keep as written. It is the right story, but it needs his ruling before anything is built, as section 0 says.

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review?

No, not yet. I would describe the eleven miles and Ron on L-plates, because nobody else has done anything like them, but I would not call the story a reason to buy the game while its last revelation is spoken rather than found, its final act hurries through talk, and the early asker meets broken scenes. Fix the first three weak points and it is a 9.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. The eleven miles. A truth found on a dial with a steering wheel, driven twice, then driven again with an officer writing in the back. It is the game's identity and its climax.
2. Ron and the seat. The lessons, the buildings named by what they were, the stories that stop one sentence later each time, the coat taken to keep a name out of a queue, and "You come back, though". The verdict of every ending is which seat he is in. This is the relationship people will remember.
3. The fare book and Sheila's one written truth. "Every fare goes in the book", said in minute two by the one person with a line in it, paid off by a mild man from Customs who reads two lines aloud and takes, at last, his Friday cup of tea.
