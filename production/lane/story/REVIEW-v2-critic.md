# LEDGER story bible, version 2: a critic's review

## 1. Scores

**Overall: 8**

1. **Tom: 7.** The Ledger gives an unvoiced man a real voice, and the flaw (he asks nobody for anything) is clear, playable and paid off by the phone box. But he spends most of the story reacting to other people's moves, and the climax is about what Mickey did, not about a choice only Tom could make.
2. **The cast: 8.** Sheila, Ron, Ada, Pauline and Reese are people a player would talk about afterwards. The crowd around them is far too big for twenty hours, and June and the "someone" (Alison or Philip) are thinner than the scenes that rest on them.
3. **The antagonists: 8.** Three different ways of coming at him (offers, paper and hired hands, noise), each with a personal reason and a scene of their own. Agar in the rain without a hat and Danny reading his father's page are the best villain beats here, though Les Coultas and Garbutt come in late and do a lot of the plot's work.
4. **The central mystery: 8.** A true mystery with layers, where the plainest book turns out to be the honest one, and the second tea means something different when you look back on it. It loses marks because a tax officer reads out the two biggest turns, and because a careful plotter writing his own crime into a book is a stretch the story only half answers.
5. **The opening and its retold scene: 8.** The first ninety minutes are tight and full of people: the priest's line about forty-one empty envelopes, the badge, the sum that bites, the carry. The failed carry ending with "Nowak, T. £82" in the debt book is excellent; the carry itself is good rather than something people will retell.
6. **Structure and pace: 7.** The calendar beats (Bonfire Night, Remembrance Sunday, the anniversary, the hearing) give Act II a shape and pull a slow player forward. Act III is far too full for two to three hours.
7. **The middle: 7.** The rivals' peaks taking turns is the right idea, and there is a cost every week. But the straight road gets thinner scenes, and the stretch between the hearing and the letter leans on the side stories to carry it.
8. **Surprise against cliché: 7.** Mickey's lie, Agar's complicity and the fare book as the true record are fresh. "The sweet old widow runs it all" and "the beloved dead uncle did the worst thing" are both shapes a seasoned player has seen, and Ada's twist is clearly signposted.
9. **The endings: 8.** Every close has a last scene and a last image in two books, and the echoes ("Nowak!", "No charge", "Stayed", "Ron drove. He didn't hit anything.") are earned. Ada's table in every ending is the best idea in the section; the Kingdom's two turns differ by a single line of goodbye, which is thin for the game's main crime ending.
10. **The side stories: 8.** The watch, the watchman, the wardrobe and the tape are whole stories with turns and with endings the town remembers. Philip's story is the weakest and the mooring is more of a plot device than a story.
11. **Tone and humour: 8.** Pauline on the radio is a great running joke and chorus, and the crabs on the Customs ride-along are properly funny. The rule that the dark scenes are never joked across is the right one.
12. **Story and play feeding each other: 7.** Lessons, rounds, carries, the hearing and the phone box are all things the systems can carry. The last week's mornings are watching a man read, and too many truths arrive as speeches.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The last week is crammed.** Section 8 puts three of the six layers of truth (Alan on Tuesday, Mickey on Wednesday and Thursday, the letter on Friday) into five game days and two to three hours. Alongside them come Ellis's trade, Les Coultas's confession, Garbutt's favour, whether to alter or burn the book, Agar's visit, Alison's front page, the two widows, the five calls, and then Ada's table and the ending. This is the "last act that hurries" BAR names from GTA V, made worse because these are the best scenes in the story. A player will reach the two widows in the cab, the darkest scene in the game, and then lose it under three more turns within a single in-game day. **Fix:** move layer four out of Act III. The two widows in the cab belongs in late Act II, on the night of scene 13 when Ron and the gate are already in play; Remembrance Sunday's six men looking at the floor sets it up a week before. Move Garbutt's favour and Les's first visit earlier as well, so that Act III holds one new truth (Mickey) and one danger (Reese). Give Act III three to four hours, or let Reese take two weeks with a weekend in between where the player acts.

**2. The inspector solves the mystery, not the player.** "A good payer, this one. Right up to the week of his fire" and "Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak" are the two lines the whole mystery depends on, and Reese reads both aloud from documents (section 8, Wednesday and Thursday). Section 15 says a player who follows the right person can find every turn early, but nothing in Act II gives him a reason to look at the importer's account or at November 1989 in the fare book. BAR asks for a truth "uncovered by play". A reviewer will write that the mystery was solved by an NPC with a pencil while the player waited. **Fix:** keep Reese as the danger and make him confirm what the player already suspects. Act II already hands him the clues: Alison's missing hour, Norman's free lifts, the box at Iveson's, Sheila's card about the stranger in the yard. Add one Act II job that sends Tom to the fare book for autumn 1989 for his own reasons (Alison asks where the cabs were that night; Sheila won't let the book leave the office, so he has to read it there after hours). Then Wednesday's dread is "he is going to find what I found", which is better drama than "he found it".

**3. The cast is too big, and the relationships that carry the midpoint are underbuilt.** Section 4 and the side stories name well over forty people. Some, like Brenda, Hal and Edith, are fine as faces in the crowd. The real problem is that the midpoint supper (section 7, the Fifth, beat 2) and "the guest's innocence" rest on a "someone" who is either Alison or Philip, fed by one Sunday walk or one game of chess. Without warmth beforehand the guest has no innocence to lose. June, Mickey's daughter, is the most obviously wronged person in the story but has three scenes and is used mostly to carry information. **Fix:** cut the Alison/Philip choice to one person (Alison, who is already part of the mystery) and give her three short beats before the Fifth that the player chooses to have. Fold Philip's job (the hall, the library, the chess) into June or Father Walsh. Then give June one scene with her own turn: the letters from Gordon's parcel, read in front of Tom, in Act II.

**4. The straight road is still the poorer road.** BAR says no stretch may be thin, "including the road of the player who refuses the crime". On the straight road the midpoint loses its first and best beat (section 7, the Fifth, beat 1: "On the straight road the room stays shut"), so he never reads "God forgive me, that's why". Scene 3 (S) and scene 8 (S) are a tail and a short Friday with no turn in them, while the keeper's versions both have one (Akers sells to the Widow; Lily stops saying "I remember"). Scene 12 (S), Ada paying with her own house and walking out of the hall, is the only straight scene as good as its keeper twin. **Fix:** give the straight player the book by another hand. On the Fifth, Sheila opens the room anyway: "You said no to it. You'd best know what you said no to." Give scene 3 (S) a turn (Darren arrives short because he has been paying Danny for safe passage) and scene 8 (S) a face (the person whose wage he shorts is Pauline, and the radio goes quiet for a day).

**5. Ada's twist is signposted, and the story teases a past it may not pay off.** Ada is "first name in the book" (Friday round), lets her upstairs room to the reporter, stands at her window over the burned car, and "knows there is no cat". A player who has seen any crime drama will suspect her by the end of week one, and Tom's own Ledger nearly says so. Meanwhile, Garbutt's "It took me two days to find you for him, which tells me something, and I'll not say what" (section 3) is a hook the frame forbids from ever being pulled. It is a promise the player will expect kept, and when it isn't, it reads as a writer's trick. **Fix:** misdirect towards Cyril harder and earlier. Let the player see Cyril give an order on the far bank in week two and pay Darren from his own pocket, so that the man in Ada's front room on the Fifth is the surprise: the boss is standing like a servant. Cut Garbutt's line, or point it at the present: "It took me two days to find you. Nobody was looking for you. That's why he wanted you." That matches Mickey's August entry and needs no past at all.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review

No. Not as written, though it is close. The pieces I would quote are here: Ron learning to drive at eleven miles an hour and then the third lesson when he does not talk; Frank Cammack's "Go on, Tommy"; Agar in the rain asking for one line to be left alone so his wife can keep believing; a tax officer with a flask turning out to be the most dangerous man in the game. A story with those beats is better than most in the genre. But a reviewer judges what the player lives through. Here that is a strong first week, a well-shaped but busy middle in which the honest road is visibly the poorer one, and then a last week where the best truths and the hardest choices arrive in a heap and are mostly read aloud to him. That is how an 8 is played, not a 9. Fix the first two weak points and I would very likely say yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. **Ron and Tom.** The passenger seat, the map as a list of things that shut, the three lessons, number nine on the quay wall, and Ron driving something in every ending. It is the one relationship here that can stand next to Henry and Hans, and it is built from play, not from talk.
2. **The fare book as the true book, paid off by Reese.** Four books, a mild man from the state asking which is true, and the answer being the dullest one in the office. "Every fare goes in the book" works as the theme, as a clue and as the last image of every ending. Keep Reese exactly as he is.
3. **The Fifth and the second tea.** The burning car, two people at a window that should hold one, "You'd better come in, Tom", and forty-one envelopes in rows on her table. It turns the first tea into an interview and gives the game its middle. Keep it, even if its signposts are made quieter.
