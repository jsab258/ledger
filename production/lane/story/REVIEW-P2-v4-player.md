# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 4: a player's review

I read this the way I would play it: as a man with a pad who wants to care about somebody, and who has been lied to by trailers before. Short version: there is a real friendship here, a twist that plays fair, and a last drive I would remember. There are also places where I would see the trick coming, a last act that tries to do too much in too little time, and a refuser's road that is a sketch.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8 out of 10.

1. Tom: 7. The fear (the packed suitcase) and the flaw (takes nothing, asks nothing) are clear and you play them, which is rare. But what he feels mostly arrives through take-or-pay presses and one-line Ledger entries, and over sixteen hours that could feel like a meter more than a man.

2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Ada, Pauline and Terry are people I would talk about afterwards; Terry's four words and Pauline's tally are the kind of small thing a player quotes. A few (Julie, Kevin, Darren) are functions until something happens to them.

3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is the best idea in the game, an enemy who only ever asks what you want, and her blockade on the cold-store road uses Tom's line properly. But her own rules make her untouchable, Agar is mostly letters and one good ride, and Danny's turn to violence comes late.

4. The central mystery: 8. A truth found with a mileometer, that changes what minute two meant, and pays off at the climax on the same road and the same handbrake. It loses a point because the clues to Sheila are lit up brighter than the writers seem to think.

5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The ferry, the walk-round, the carry with a constable who can turn round, and a sixth name in the debt book if you fail. Lesson one with the bollard is a strong second. Night one is a lot of beats in one evening, though.

6. Structure and pace: 7. Three acts with a real midpoint, a weekly table so the middle has shape, and a clock (Agar's thirty-first, the inspection). Act III at two and a half hours carries six moving parts and four signature branches and will feel hurried.

7. The middle: 7. The Fifth to scene 15 is the best stretch, and the round shrinking from twenty doors to eighteen is a lovely way to show time. Between scenes, though, it is still fares, a dusk job and a count, and I have played that loop before at hour twelve.

8. Surprise against cliché: 7. The dead uncle with a secret, the inherited business, the inspector at the end: all familiar. What is new is that the secret belongs to the bookkeeper and the friend's betrayal is a sacrifice. That is good, if I do not guess it.

9. The endings: 8. Every ending is a seat in a car, and that image works. "Ron drove." and "Nowak!" on the quay are both endings I would feel. The Reese and Ellis outcomes are too gentle for a burned bonded warehouse.

10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and her daughter protecting each other from the same truth is the best of them and feeds the last act. The watch has a strong second turn. One Way and the umbrella are fine but small.

11. Tone and humour: 7. The crabs, Shiner under orders not to speak, Agar's hat, Terry's fifth word. Dry and earned, kept away from the dark scenes. I would smile more than laugh; nothing here is a set piece people clip and share.

12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. Driving the eleven miles yourself, a handbrake that means "somebody goes, you stop" all game and then the right input is none, references only from people you let help you. That is the game and the story being the same thing. The question mechanic needs more thought before I trust it.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The twist is signposted. What: Sheila is five foot one and fetches the steps; car two's seat is stuck forward "for a year"; Ron says "Mickey taught Sheila on this road, nineteen sixty-one, she's not driven since, far as I know", in the first lesson, in week one. Where: Act I Sunday (lesson one), section 4 Ron and Tom, scene 10. Why I would feel it: anyone who has played a mystery game hears "far as I know" as a flashing light. A short seat plus a short woman plus a man saying she can drive is the answer in hour two, and then nine hours of Ron as suspect feel like the game thinking I am slow. Fix: keep every clue but separate them. Move the 1961 line out of lesson one and into the old books on the top shelf (a learner's receipt, or Mickey's own note in a 1961 fare book) so it is found, not said. Cut "far as I know". And give the seat a second plausible owner: a short relief driver from 1989 who has since left, named in the count, so a careful player has two short drivers to choose between and Ron as the loud wrong answer.

2. Act III is crowded and rushed. What: in one week the player gets Reese's three visits, Sheila's kitchen, a phone box, Danny's blockade, the Widow's prices, Agar's lock with an option to sign, four ways to fill in a name, Ellis, and then the endings. Where: section 8, Thursday and Friday especially. Why: this is exactly where GTA V got punished. The best scene (Sheila driving the eleven miles) will arrive after a day of errands and I will be tired of running about. Fix: cut Thursday to two doors. Fold Agar's lock into Danny's (Danny is renting the yard from Agar's agents) so one obstacle carries both, and let the gate scene carry Frank Cammack. Give Friday's drive the room.

3. The soft landing. What: a theft from a bonded store, a burned warehouse, a badly burned man, and the outcome at best is a caution and a lost bookkeeping job, delivered by a kind Customs officer who has "never used" his one power. Where: section 8 Friday, section 9 The Kingdom better turn. Why: I would feel the writers sparing her. It undercuts the BAR's own rule that no ending makes it nothing, because this one nearly does. Fix: keep Reese mild but make the cost land on screen. Ellis cannot caution a fire this size; she charges Sheila, and the "better" ending is Sheila in front of the magistrates in January with Ron and Tom on the bench where the dockers sat for Tom in scene 4. The suspended sentence can still happen after the credits. The price stays seen.

4. The refuser's road is thin. What: the player who winds the club down on the first Sunday gets five bullet points and is told the two roads "run the same eighteen scenes, read differently". Where: section 11. Why: BAR names this road as one that must not be thin, and as written I cannot see how the mystery reaches him: why is he counting old books, why does Ron still need the coat, why does Shiner brag. A player who chose the honest road and then finds the story only half fits will feel punished for it. Fix: write out the three or four scenes that differ, and give the honest road its own reason to find the night: the friendly society's audit needs the 1989 books, so June or Edith Stockill does the count with him, and the eleven miles turn up as the society's first problem rather than the firm's.

5. The flaw as a button. What: offers are "one press: take it, or pay for it", and the question is typed or picked "in his own words". Where: section 3, the offers and the question; repeated through Acts I and II. Why: if it looks like a prompt, I will learn to farm it. Players spot the karma meter in an hour and then play it, not the man. And "a question asked is an answer owed" is never shown costing anything specific. Fix: never put the offer on a prompt; let it happen in the world (he drinks the tea or leaves coins by the cup; he lets Ron pay at the Tangs' or reaches past him). And give the question a visible price the first time: the Widow answers Tom straight, then asks him what he wants, and he cannot answer, which is the game telling him what his silence is.

## 3. Would I single it out to a friend?

No, not yet, and it is close. If you told me there was a game where I teach a fifty-eight-year-old docker to drive at eleven miles an hour, lose him to the enemy on Bonfire Night, find out he went to protect somebody, and end it with that somebody driving the same eleven miles while I keep my hand off the handbrake, I would want to play it. That is better than most of what I play. But I would guess who drove too early, the last act would rush me to that drive, and if I had refused the crime the story would hardly know I was there. Fix the first two and I would say yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron and the lessons. The passenger seat naming buildings by what they used to be, the stories that stop one sentence later each time, "I go, you stop", and his lie told at eleven miles an hour for the woman who left him. This is the Henry and Hans of the game.

2. The eleven miles on a dial. The mystery is solved by driving, not by a cutscene, and paid off by driving. Seven, and four, and "eleven and a tenth, I'll allow the tenth". Do not let anyone turn it into a menu.

3. Sheila as the one who never said a false thing. Every line she speaks being true on a second reading, "Nobody reads a fare book", and "Tom" said once for something she found out herself. Hide her better, but never change her.
