## For Jafar: the story on one page

What Tom wants. Tom Nowak steps off the ferry with £23.50 and a dead uncle's cab office. On his first night he tells Ron: by Christmas every night fare, every door and every quay on the Hook goes through Mickey's, and when people say Mickey's they will mean him. He wants to be somebody, fast.

How the empire grows. You never see a number. You see cars on the board, your men at their posts, shops that wash your money, and the name the town calls you: the new owner, Nowak, Tom, Tommy. You can fight the three gangs, pay them, side with one against another, turn their men, and take a beaten gang in.

The opponent. Danny Cammack, Mickey's old driver, who thinks the office should have been his. Quicker and funnier than Tom, he wants the same thing and can be your best earner or your loudest enemy. Every week you watch him gain something.

The seven weeks. One: nothing, nobody, rent due Friday. Two: cars, ranks, your first men. Three: the docks at dawn and the big contract. Four: the Landing, a planned night job on the Widow's ground; after it you are holding, not grabbing. Five: they come for your men, and for Ron. Six: they come for you. Seven: five things the tax inspector wants, each a job, then Danny.

The turn and the loss. On your first morning Sheila asks what goes in the fare book for the night before, and you choose the words. Five weeks later DS Ellis reads that line back and tells you to write it again. Ron walks in to take the blame. You can stop him, at a price: sign for it yourself and carry the charge into the last week, or a cheaper way if you have treated him right. Do nothing, and Ron goes inside in your place. He lives.

How it ends. On the last afternoon Danny is on the quay with the day book, sat in Mickey's own car: take the book back, or lose it. At five you decide whose name the line carries: Ron's, your own or Danny's. The town tells the ending in its own voices, from what you did.

## 1. The empire goal

### What Tom wants

Night one. Ron comes in off the rank with a dark coat over his arm and an envelope: Mickey's arrangement, down to the ferry landing after ten. Whatever Tom does about it, he first says this, a fixed scene:

> TOM: Thirty years, and that's the firm? An old man walking an envelope down to the water? Not for me. Every fare after dark, every door on this street, every quay with a rope on it: it goes through this office or it doesn't go. By Christmas, Ron. Mickey's runs the Hook. And when they say Mickey's, they'll mean me.
>
> RON: Aye. Well. Envelope still wants carrying.

"Mickey's runs the Hook" is what the town repeats, at first as a joke on him.

The want is something to get, and a little wrong in canon's register: class, not greed. A stranger of 32 with £18 of rent due on Friday wants to be somebody, quickly, in a town that did not come to the ferry to meet him. What he needs, and cannot see, is a place where somebody waits up for him. He has it on the first night (Ron on the rank, Sheila's kettle), and the empire is paid for in exactly that.

The question the game scores and the ending answers, which nobody ever says: what is a name on the Hook worth, and who pays for it? Two things keep the score in view: the name people call him, and, every Sunday, Sheila reading back the week's nights with the name that stands beside each.

## 2. The town's powers

### The peace that died with Mickey (PROPOSED)

Mickey's outfit was half dead (canon): Ron, Sheila, two drivers and the arrangement. What it ran was the room every gang needed (OPEN 5): a book of every night fare on the Hook and a radio nobody could help overhearing. Every other night a carbon of the night sheet went down to the landing in an envelope, to the old firm that worked it before the three rivals were anybody (OPEN 5). For that the Widow let his cars through the dock gate untaxed, Agar renewed his licence unasked, and Danny Cammack drove car two (OPEN 3). None of the three could take the Hook without the other two closing on it. The morning Ron found Mickey, that ended: in week one all three are looking at the office.

### Pacts, wars, truces and betrayals

PROPOSED, the frame's main new system; the dial, the ladders, the truce and the bought men are the blueprint's. All of it is engine, met only through people.

- A pact is made with one rival against another, at his visit, a sit-down or a meeting Garbutt brokers. Its terms are spoken and kept in the Ledger: which posts each holds, one joint job, whose ground each keeps off. While it holds, the ally's ladder does not climb against Tom.
- It breaks when Tom grows past what the ally can live with (the fourth rung makes every ally a rival), when his men do a deed against the ally's, when the ally is offered more, or when Tom breaks it.
- Betrayal is a beat the director may wake once those conditions turn, never without signs: the ally stops coming in person, asks for his share early, and his men are seen with another firm's. Its form is the ally's own: Danny talks and takes the rank, the Widow takes a man, Agar withdraws a paper.
- War is the dial (0 Fists, 1 Shown, 2 Fired, 3 War) and each rival's ladder (noticed, a visit, pressure, a threat, a sit-down). The dial moves only on what was seen or heard, and falls one step after about two quiet game weeks, never to 0.
- A truce is Garbutt's one a game, holding one rival at its rung for exactly two game weeks; or a pact. Paying a rival holds him two game weeks unless Tom does a deed against him.
- A rival's man whose loyalty to his head is low (paid short, shamed, his head lost a sit-down, Tom spared him or helped his family) can be turned by a need met or a hook spent. He brings a true read of his head's habits, and his old head may send for him. A whole firm can come over once its head has lost: Danny back on the board, under Tom.

## 3. The core six

Tom, and five people carried through every week. Everybody else is light.

### Tom Nowak, 32

- Wants: to be the Hook's name by Christmas (section 1). His bond is Ron.
- Flaw: pride, a stranger's need to be seen to win, and a temper that goes when he is treated as Mickey's lad.
- Good at the wheel, and at walking straight up to things; canon never says where he learned. Laughed at for giving everybody his whole name, like a man standing for the council, and for never asking the way.
- The one deed only he would do, in week one. Danny Cammack leaves his car across Mickey's rank with the keys in it, because nobody on the Hook would touch it, and sits on Tom's counter. Tom, in the suit he brought for a funeral he missed, drives it down Quay Street, parks it on the quay's edge with the front wheels over the water, walks back, hangs the keys on Mickey's board and chalks a third line: CAR 3. He risks his body (two of Danny's men are on the wall) and his standing (the parade is watching). Danny answers: he laughs, pays the fare, and a week later comes back with an offer. How far the player takes it is his (the corner, the edge, the water). The edge is where the two meet at the end.
- His second is offered in week five: his own name, signed, to keep Ron out (section 5).
- By path. With Danny, loudest and laziest: Danny does the running. With the Widow, safest and smallest. Alone, most at risk in his own body. Wound down, the same want by daylight.
- He can be hurt, arrested and held for hours. He cannot die.
- Fixed lines, at authored beats only; the rest of his talk is the player's:
  - "It's on my rank, so it's on my board. Car three. Two pound forty to the quay. Sheila, put it in the book."
  - "Nowak. N, O, W, A, K."

### Ron Kirby, 58: the bond

- Wants: to be a man who is sent for. A docker until 1989, and a docker is a man somebody wants at a gate at six. Mickey gave him a door to stand in.
- Flaw: he does not ask and does not tell. He will carry a thing for years before he says it is heavy.
- Code: you do not grass, you do not leave a man on the quay, you turn up.
- The one deed only he would do: the day the office is searched he puts on the coat he wore to Mickey's funeral, walks into the station, asks for DS Ellis by name and offers a statement before anybody has asked for one. He will sign it at nine, unless Tom has signed first.
- By path. With Danny: he loses the front seat, and they bicker down the dock road ("I've been overtaken by a hearse, Ron." "It were in a hurry."). With the Widow: back at her gate under the pact, and he may come out of the cells hers. Alone: hurt worst. Wound down: glad of it, and still the one the old years fall on.
- Voice:
  - "Coming up on the ice house. That's the ice house. Leaving the ice house."
  - "I've told her nowt that isn't so. I've just not told her you."

The bond is built in the hands, week by week:

- Week 1. The ask on the rank, where Tom says what he wants (talk; trust). Lee Drewery's £15: Ron fills the doorway (action). Car three: Ron at his shoulder (action).
- Week 2. Lesson one, the dock road: Ron drives; the player has the handbrake and one word, "Stop" (drive). The ferry rank, back to back before the boat queue (action).
- Week 3. The dawn convoy, Ron naming buildings a street late (drive). The gate: Tom has him stand against the men he stood among all his working life (action; the first strain, his want against Tom's).
- Week 4. The Landing: in the plan, and at the wheel when it goes wrong (action and drive; trust at its height). The cut, settled in the car going home: paid from the tin like a crewman, or kept on Mickey's wage (drive; strain).
- Week 5. The gate again: Ron cornered (action, or Tom's absence; strain). Lesson five: he stops the car properly for once, and asks what is in the book (drive; the breach: Tom answers, true or false). Clearing the office the night before the search (action). The cells, and the night between (Tom's deed, or Ron's; the loss, or its price).
- Week 6. Free: at Tom's back in the yard. Inside: a job he sends Tom on, by Father Walsh (action).
- Week 7. Free: he drives Tom down to the quay. Inside: the visit (drive, or talk). The last scene (a drive, or a hatch; the last word).

Sixteen beats, at least one a week, three strains before the breach; eleven are jobs or drives on any path. Extra lessons, Mickey's Friday and Mickey's watch are invitations that cost nothing if refused.

### Sheila Dunn, 53: the book

- Wants: one book. Thirty-one years she has kept two, and she would like to end with one she could leave open on the counter.
- Flaw: she writes what she is told. She has never told a man no; she withholds instead: his name, a key, her opinion.
- The one deed only she would do: she sits at her desk through the whole search with her handbag on her knee, Mickey's real book and her own true count inside it under her knitting, and offers DS Ellis a mint from the same bag. (The true count is PROPOSED: a third book, in her hand, of each night's money as it really was. He first sees her shut it in week three.)
- Bond: she makes him say what he did, each morning, in words she can write. She calls him new management until she trusts him; the day she says "Tom" is a rung of its own. She pulls against him at the first wash: "Same as your first night?"
- By path. Trusted: the firm's brain, who says where the lie will hold and hands him the pen at the end. Asked to put the books off three counts running: she leaves until he comes to her door after a clean week. Never trusted: she keeps the true count anyway, hers to hold out or hold back.

### Danny Cammack, 27: the opponent (OPEN 1, written as A)

- Wants what Tom wants: to be the Hook's name. For himself: his name back on Mickey's board, at the top.
- Flaw: he cannot wait and cannot be second. He spends what he has not yet earned, money and trust alike.
- The one deed only he would do (with OPEN 3): he never gave back his key to car two. He calls him "nephew" (PROPOSED: Danny's needle, outside the name ladder). He pulls against him the morning after the Landing, when the town says Cammack took it and Danny lets it.

- Wants: one case. For herself: the fire file closed in her own handwriting.
- Flaw: she moves only on paper. A thing is not so until somebody has signed it, which is why the street always knows more than she does.

## 5. The spine

Beats wake on states, never on a mission list. Dates appear only where a person names them or the town's calendar has them. No beat can fail the run.

### The early deed: the line in the book (decided by this lane; PROPOSED to him)

The morning after the first ask that reaches him, whatever he did with the envelope (carried it, refused it, opened it), Sheila turns the day book to that night.

> SHEILA: Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule, and it's mine now. So. What was last night?

The player answers in his own words; she reads them back; his plain yes puts them in ink. Three kinds of answer: a lie, which is a cover fare ("Ferry landing and back, waited, cash"); the truth ("An envelope to the landing, for Mickey's arrangement"); or blank, after which Sheila, who has never left a hole in a book, writes something of her own.

The line is stored word for word. It is ordinary play, the first job's paperwork, done in ten seconds and forgotten in an hour. It returns three times.

1. The habit (Act IIa). The first time night money must pass through the book, her pen is up: "Same as your first night?" His first answer has become the firm's way of writing, and every wash hangs from it. He can change it here. Then she writes a second line in a red book of her own, and shuts it.
2. The turn (Act IIb). In the searched office Ellis reads the line aloud and has him write it again underneath. The Core compares the kind of answer and its facts with what he said five weeks before; the model only classifies.
   - A lie: can he remember it the same way twice? If so, she has one false entry under the owner's name. If not, two.
   - The truth: he can write it without thinking. It is a confession with a date, and in the column for the man who brought the job in, the name is R. Kirby. The book has no column for owners.
   - Blank: there is a line there, in Sheila's hand, that he has never seen and cannot write. "Then these are Mrs Dunn's words."
3. The last line (Act III). The inspector keeps week one for his last morning. At five Sheila turns the book round, lays her pen on it and takes her hand away. What Tom writes, and under whose name, is the ending.

### Act I, The Inheritance

Opens on the ferry; closes on Sheila's Sunday question. Stakes: money. The most authored act; the nine first-week designs stand. Its beats: arrival, the ask and the want, the line, five debts, Ada's tea, Alison's fare to the warehouse row, Friday's rent, Sunday.

The rivals stay in Act II, as the approved outline and first hour have them, with one exception (PROPOSED, a change to Act I): Danny's car across the rank is his "noticed" rung, and the deed only Tom would do. The Widow and Agar are names overheard. Their first looks open Act IIa: the Widow's men tax Kevin at the dock gate (pay, defy or counter; if he pays, he asks for a receipt in front of the whole pen, and a day later gets one in her own hand, made out to Mr Nowack), and Agar's letter about the licence comes addressed to Mr Nowek.

By path. Take it over: the arrangement stands. Wind it down: it ends that night, and every rival starts a rung higher. Refuse to say: the street decides, once a third car, a first man or the ask taken twice in week two makes the firm his. Every road reaches the second rung. A player who stops here has had a small whole story: a stranger arrives with nothing, makes one enemy laugh and gets a name.

### Act IIa, The Rise

Opens at the second rung; closes when the midpoint wakes. Stakes: the firm's things and his own skin. Beats, in any order the states allow:

- The old firm's end. Kept: it hands over the landing, its men and its night custom. Ended: the landing stands empty until the highest rival fills it. Either way its people go out on the morning boat, in his cab or past it, all but one old hand who stays on at the landing.
- A first man, by need (slow, and it holds) or by a hook (quick, and brittle).
- Danny's visit: car two, six minutes, the offer. His men, his car on the board and his nerve, for a share of the nights; he opens at half. Take it, refuse it, or keep him waiting. Where his name is chalked is the player's to choose.
- The ferry rank: a car on it five days in a fortnight, a word with whoever holds it, or fists in front of the boat queue.
- The Widow's and Agar's first looks (Act I, above), then their visits and first pressures: pay, defy or counter.
- Ron's lessons; Garbutt's card; Rita's back room; a first round; the habit.
- Sheila's trust: Mickey's locked office, the real book with at least five new names, the page about the fire, and the revolver under it, which Ron puts back.
- The contract. Eric Tharratt will talk once a firm with at least four cars has met the packing shift on time five mornings in one game week. His drivers run four; the fifth is Tom's, with the Widow's men swinging the dock gate shut on every car he has. Through it (pay, bluff or push), or round by Agar's cold-store road and the fish dock in the dark.

By path. With Danny: car three is real, Quay Street goes quiet and the docks get harder. With the Widow: the gate opens early and Quay Street gets loud. Alone: all three ladders climb, and Garbutt's truce is worth most. Wound down: the same rise by lawful work, harder pressed. It closes when the midpoint wakes, usually at the third rung, with Pauline saying "Tom" on the air.

### The midpoint: the Landing

Wakes when Tom holds the third rung, or any rival reaches "threat", or at the end of game week four; never before the end of week three (PROPOSED). A planned job: a day of looking, a night of doing. The Widow's weekly load, boxed goods and tobacco, comes over on the last boat. Whoever takes it, and has a man standing there in the morning, holds the landing and its nights.

Three ways in, opened by the people he has made: the road (the van, driven by a man he has met), the gate (in with the contract cars at shift change, to the net store under her office), or the boat (on the stage as she ties up). If it wakes by the start of week four it is set on the night of the display on the quay: the whole town out in the dark, every bang covered, and more witnesses than he has ever had. Ron is in it, and Danny if allied. One thing always goes wrong. Then Tom is needed at the load, and the car has to be brought round by a man with four lessons.

This is where play changes. Before it, every job adds something. After it, what he holds is held by people, and people can be got at. A post stands for as long as the man on it is loyal and has the nerve; Tom never stands one himself (PROPOSED, in place of the blueprint's stare and three-day count). What reaches the player is one authored crisis for each post, each its own kind of job, each aimed at a man he chose. Ellis moves to asking round. A gun may have been shown. The question stops being "what can I take?" and becomes "whom have I put where, and will he hold?"

By path. Widow as ally: the load is Danny's, and the post is the landing he took. Alone: the target is whichever rival reached "threat". Wound down: it is done in the open, every car on the quay for the display, carrying the town home past her taxmen. Fail it and the story goes on, with him holding less. The rest beat after it is Remembrance Sunday at the chapel, the men of the pen keeping their backs to Ron.

### Act IIb, The Squeeze

Opens after the midpoint; closes at a sit-down with one head. Stakes: a person, then Tom's body and his liberty; Ellis's manhunt is possible from here.

- The collision, guaranteed: an evening with his day life, and his night life comes to that door. A crewman bleeding on Ada's step; or Danny, far too cheerful, sitting down at Alison's table.

- The sit-down, with whichever head's ladder gets there; the blueprint's fail-safe, which starts four game days before the act ends, makes sure of one. Accept, defy, or counter with what he knows. He goes in hurt.

By path. With Danny: he turns, and he talks. With the Widow: she never talks to the law; she forecloses. Alone: a crewman past his nerve names the night, or Iveson does. Wound down: the line still names the old arrangement. It closes on the fall: a player who stops here has had a rise and its price.

### The night between (OPEN 2, written as A)

Tom is arrested on the entry: custody in hours. Then, through the wall: "That you, boss? Say nowt." Ron has walked in by himself and offered a statement that covers whoever the line exposed, to be signed at nine. Tom is bailed that evening. The night between is the player's, and the loss is his to stop, at a price.

- Sign first. Before nine he can walk back into the station and put the line on paper under his own name. Ron walks. Tom is charged and on bail into Act III: Ellis's case is on him, the licence is suspended for a fortnight (PROPOSED: a new trigger beside RT2-18's hearing; back before the books open), a post goes with it, and the charged state (the hunted one, if he runs) is live to the end.
- A cheaper way, if his record has earned one. A truthful first line and a Ron kept on the wage give a brief something to argue: Ron out as a messenger, at the brief's fee, and the brief is Agar's man. Or Wardell can lose the statement that named the night: £200, once, and Ellis notices. Ron lets either be done in his name only if Tom came for him at the gate, or redeemed Mickey's watch.
- Or let it stand. At nine Ron signs: that Mickey's arrangement was his to carry, that the envelope on the night in the book was part of it, and that he will not say who took it at the landing. Not a word is false on any path, and Tom is not in it. It does not break the rule that Mickey's people never go to the police about one of their own (ruling of 1 October): he informs on nobody but himself. Ron goes in against anything Tom only says, and never against what Tom signs: "Saying's nowt, boss. You've a firm to stand in. I've a door."

If Ron is gone, the most loyal man stands in his place. The loss is prepared for hours by rule (PROPOSED): until the turn Ron is at Tom's shoulder on every main job, and rides collections and side jobs too unless he is sent away, so even a roaming player has spent a third of his hours beside him. Either way it breaks the want, and play changes: he stops taking and starts asking, with a charge over him or with no door man, and in the last week he spends what he built.

### Act III, The Ledger Comes Due

Opens on the Customs letter, after the sit-down or by the end of week six. Canon's last act: five mornings, one item each, graded from the books; three calls on the last day; the books open at five. Each item has to be fetched, by a different kind of play away from the counter, and each is a deed of his read back (PROPOSED):

1. The wages. A man paid off the book has to be found at his own door and brought in, or kept away: by his need, or by a hook.
2. The cars' records. Kevin has gone, with car one's log and its missing miles: a chase across the Hook, or his price.
3. An invoice. The cold-store book is in Agar's office: a break-in by night, or a word with Simon Ombler.
4. The gate. The book's dock-gate entries want a name behind them (M. Jensen, if he ever got his receipt): a drive to the gate, the Widow in the back of his cab, and her price for signing.
5. Week one: the line.

Sheila, if she trusts him, says each evening where the lie will hold. Between mornings the men and posts the fall cost him can be won back door by door, each door answering from what he did to that person. Ellis offers her trade: give her a rival, and the street learns who talks. The most loyal man with three game weeks' service stays late on the first evening, with the successor's offer. Ron is at his back again if Tom kept him out; if not, there is the visit ("You keep that rank going, boss. I've not sat in here for you to finish with nowt") and a job Ron sends him on from inside.

The last day is one piece. In the morning the inspector asks for week one. By noon, three calls at most. Then Pauline, on the air: car two is not answering. Danny has taken Mickey's own car on his old key, and the day book off the counter with it, and is parked on the quay's edge with the front wheels over the water. At four, the quay: fists, unless the dial stands at Fired or War. Tom may have one good arm, and he can lose: then the book goes where Danny takes it and the choice is made for him (PROPOSED: a new input to the ending rule); car two is back on the rank by morning, its keys on Mickey's board, as the blueprint's floor has it (RT2-06). If he wins he has the book back and Danny at the edge, to let go on the morning boat, put back on the board under him, or keep for Ellis. At five, the counter, the book, the pen.

The final choice (shape decided by this lane; PROPOSED to him) is whose name the line carries when the books open:

- Leave it as it stands. The firm, if the books hold. Whoever is carrying the line goes on carrying it: Ron stays on remand, as he asked (PROPOSED: the blueprint has no prison, 2.6.9; remand, which 2.6.4 allows, runs to the end); or, if Tom signed, his own charge stands.
- Write it true, in his own hand, and let the books open on Sheila's true count, with Ellis at the counter to read it. Whoever was carrying the line is let go: Ron walks out, or the weight comes off Tom's own charge (PROPOSED). The business does not survive its own books. It is the firm's book opened at its own counter, not a man confessing. It touches the no-redemption ruling (D27, D30); it is not a redemption because the firm is lost for it, and that reading is his to overturn.
- Put Danny's name to it: Ellis's trade. One charge goes, Tom's or, if he asks it, Ron's (PROPOSED). The street learns that Mickey's talks, and the Kingdom's best turn is closed.

Each part of it has been taught by smaller choices: what goes in the book (weeks one, three and five), the firm or Ron (the cut; the gate; the night between), and whether to hand a man to Ellis (side stories 11, 23 and 31).

By path. Widow as ally: on the fourth morning her price is the landing. Alone: fewer doors, each worth more. Wound down: the true count is mostly Mickey's years, and it is his washing the firm cannot survive.

## 6. The story director

Decided by this lane, PROPOSED to him; not yet in the blueprint. A small scheduler over a pool of authored beats. It never moves the world: the Core moves the dial, the ladders and Ellis's stage, on deeds. The director only chooses which beat, among those whose conditions are already true, plays next, and when.

Each beat is a card: conditions (world state), a priority, a weight (light, middle, heavy), a cooldown, a shape card of seven fields (opening, turn, ending, action, place, company, mood), who can carry it and a fallback role, and the sign shown before it lands.

- Pacing. After Act I something reaches Tom at least every 30 real minutes. Act II runs in at least four stages of at most 4 real hours, each ending in a rung change. At most one heavy beat a game week (the Landing, the night between, the yard, the quay), a rest or a funny beat after each, and a minimum of play between two heavy beats (PROPOSED: two real hours), so a fast player cannot stack them. A slow player's gaps fill with side stories, never with standing at a post. The weekly rule holds: one rival rung, one firm change, one return of a deed.
- Cooling. When the dial or a ladder has risen twice in a game day it offers only rest beats until morning. When nothing has risen for a game week it reaches for the highest beat whose sign has been shown.
- No repeats. Neighbouring beats differ on at least three of the seven shape fields; no beat plays twice; a gap is filled by a side story or a street happening before any filler job.
- The next want. The Ledger always holds one named next thing in a person's own words (who, where, what for) and, beneath it, Tom's aim for the rung in his. No markers. "Eric Tharratt, cold stores. The packing shift met on time, five mornings running. Then he'll talk."
- Late, absent, or the carrier is dead. Every main beat has a second way in: the old firm's man brings the ask to the door; if car three sits a day, Danny moves it and tells it his way. The simulation may take away only who is alive, who knows what, who is where, who will talk, and how the law stands. The turn has its tip by the end of week five; only as a last resort does the inspector's reading of week one carry it.

Three chairs keep the spine standing. If Danny is dead or gone, Craig Fewson has his ladder at the same rung (RT2-05), his key and his grudge, and is the man on the quay. If Ron is gone before the turn, the most loyal man stands in his place and Sheila has the last word (PROPOSED, a question for RT2-05: until the turn, a rival's harm to Ron stops at the hospital). If Sheila has left, the book lies on the counter with the pen on it on the last morning.

## 7. How memory, gossip and talk carry the story

The rules (decided by this lane, PROPOSED to him; the blueprint holds the first two in part):

- Said to his face: every deed of weight with a named witness returns as a named person's remark within a game day.
- Told never above seen: tellings lose detail and never gain it, so the town's version of Tom is something he can hear, correct or use.
- Returning people: anybody he hurt, shamed, spared, paid off, poached or let down goes into a pool, and later beats are cast from it.
- Live talk: anybody can be spoken to, and says only what the simulation knows. The spine's facts sit in the mouths that hold them: Ron on the line, Sheila on his name, Danny on Mickey.
- The name ladder is the town's verdict, spoken.

Worked example 1: said to his face, and a telling he can use. Car three is seen by Ada from her window and Shaun Hewson from his ladder. By noon Brenda has it whole: "You're never him that put Cammack's motor on the edge." Lee Drewery has it third-hand and thin: a man from Mickey's did it. "Was it Ron?" The player can leave Ron's name on it, which the pen respects and Danny's men will act on, or claim it, and that wins Lee.

Worked example 2: one debt, three keys, three returns. Dennis Iveson owes £80 and has it. Front up his yard hand, Neil Screeton (proposed), and in week six Screeton is the hardest fight in the yard: "Eighty pound, Mr Nowak. I've your change here." Go into the hut at night, and Norman Dent has a noise and a shape, suspicion and never "he did it": in week five it is a description in Ellis's notebook. Lean on what Iveson bought out of the burned warehouse, and he pays on the spot and remembers: he is the one who trades Ellis the date of a landing night.

Worked example 3: a returning enemy with a mark. On the ferry rank in week two the Widow's taxman, Mark Billany (proposed), goes down in front of the boat queue. Tom can hand him his cap, walk off, or put the boot in. In week four Billany, with a stitched eyebrow, is driving the van at the Landing. Spared, he lays the keys on the roof and walks: "That's us straight." Kicked, he locks himself in and leans on the horn until the gate men come.

Five side stories, and exactly how each comes back:

- Looking costs nothing (25). Mickey's watch, pawned by Ron for the funeral flowers. Redeemed for him, it is what he leaves at the station desk if he goes in ("Tell him it's five minutes fast. Mickey liked to be early"), or wears on the door at five. Left in Rita's window, it is still for sale in the close.
- The name on the board (6). What Tom has Shaun Hewson paint is the question in paint, and in the close the sign keeps Mickey's name in every ending, as his approved draft has it; what changes is how fresh the paint is.
- Mickey's Friday (4). Two of number nine, kept or dropped. In the close Lily Tang counts them out: two, or one.
- Friday means Friday (27). If he told Sylvia Rennison "I'm not Mickey", she says it back to him in the close, word for word.
- The window at the caff (11) and Lead off the roof (23). Handing a man to Ellis in small is the trade in small: Grace calls it police and says so at her counter, and the parish thanks nobody by name. At the trade the same doors shut.

With the line, car three, the gate tax (the fourth morning), Iveson's debt, the man on the ferry rank, Ada's tea, the cut and the gate, more than a dozen acts of his own return as scenes. No wrong deed is forced. Every main job has at least two ways through, and failing one moves the story on.

## 8. How the ending is assembled

From three things. The spine's choice: whose name the line carries. The empire's state when the books open at five: the built rule (RT2-04) reads the save, with two PROPOSED changes: who holds the book after the quay, and OPEN 4's friend (the business, a friendship (OPEN 4), charges, anybody given to Ellis, a successor), and the close shows what can be seen: the rung, the posts, who is still on the board. And what the town remembers: up to about five lines, spoken by named people who each hold a deed of his at first hand; what each rival still holds and calls him (RT-05); and the last name he is called, and by whom. Nobody sums up. Signs come before every cost. Every close is the same Saturday: the Harbour Board chains the old dock gate, the pen stands empty, and what differs is who is there to see it. The three endings are the built ones, each with a better and a plainer turn.

### The Kingdom

The books hold. The board is full, Pauline is calling cars to every door on the Hook, and people on the parade step aside. He has exactly what he said on the first night: they say Mickey's and they mean him. The sign still says Mickey's. His man stands his post in front of a chained gate.

If Ron went in, he is where he asked to be left, and their last scene is through a hatch: "Boss." Nothing comes after it. The Man in Black, putting his hat on: "I don't mark those." And the Widow, from her chair: "Tommy." It is the top of the ladder, and she is the one saying it.

If Tom signed, Ron is on the door and the charge is read in the close: he has the Hook, and a date. The name is Ron's to say. The better turn (the built Both, and rare) needs what the rule already asks, a friend, a street that holds none of his rackets as fact, no serious charge and nobody given to Ellis, and so only a Ron kept out the cheaper way.

### The Straight Life

At five he writes the line true, and the books open on the true count. The firm ends at its own counter. In the better turn (the built Quiet) the successor took it over first, and there is still a cab office on Quay Street under Mickey's name, run by another man. In the plainer, the cars pay the assessment and the bay is whitewashed.

### Burn Both

Neither the firm nor a friend. He held on and a morning fell; or he lost the book on the quay; or he named a name and lost both anyway. Another firm's cars stand on the rank, and the chain goes on the gate with nobody of his there to see it. Ron takes the Widow's coat. Shaun Hewson has his ladder against the front: "Wants doing. 3. After the readers, on the lead's decisions: the loss is the player's to stop, at a price; posts are held by their men and never stood by the player, which asks a change of RT2-09; the five mornings are each fetched by play; the quay moves to the last afternoon, with the book on it.
