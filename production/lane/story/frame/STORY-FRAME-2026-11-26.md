# LEDGER: the story frame

Written 24 November 2026; revised the same day for phase 2 (the story on paper) from the lead's brief (paper/PAPER-BRIEF.md), the owner's decisions of 24 November and the six blind readers' notes. The text those readers scored is kept in working/STORY-FRAME-v2-before-paper.md. The scenes in paper/scenes/ are written into this frame. Canon and the rulings outrank it. PROPOSED marks what reaches past them; (proposed) marks a name minted here. Sample lines are not final script.

## For Jafar: the story on one page

What Frank wants. Frank Nowak steps off the ferry with £23.50 and a dead uncle's cab office. On his first night he tells Ron: by Christmas every night fare, every door and every quay on the Hook goes through Mickey's, and when people say Mickey's they will mean him. He wants to be somebody, fast.

How the empire grows. You never see a number. You see cars on the board, your men at their posts, shops that wash your money, and the name the town calls you: the new owner, Nowak, Frank, Frankie. You can fight the three gangs, pay them, side with one against another, and turn their men. An honest firm runs cars and no rackets, and stops at the name Nowak.

The opponent. Danny Cammack, who drove Mickey's own car and never gave the key back. Quicker and funnier than Frank, he wants the same thing, and can be your best earner or your loudest enemy. Every week you watch him gain something, and his complaint is chalked on your board.

The seven weeks, each with one aim somebody says to your face. One, Sheila: £18 by Friday, and on Sunday say what this firm is. Two, Ron: a third car, and the ferry rank. Three, the cold stores: five mornings on the minute. Four, the Fixer: the Widow's load, and whoever is stood on the landing on Sunday morning has it. Five: they will each try one of your men; then the police come for the book. Six: three jobs, to walk into a room with all three heads holding something each of them wants. Seven: five things the tax inspector wants, two of them the loudest nights in the game; then Danny.

The turn and the loss. On your first morning Sheila finishes a half-written line in the fare book with your words. Five weeks later DS Ellis reads that line back and tells you to write it again. Ron walks in to take the blame. You can let him go inside, sign for it yourself, or buy a cheaper way that somebody then holds over you. Ron lives on every path. Something is lost on every path too: since his first driving lesson Ron has named every building you pass, and after that night he stops.

How it ends. On the last afternoon Danny is on the quay with the day book, sat in Mickey's own car: take the book back, or bargain for it across your own counter. At five you decide whose name the line carries: Ron's, your own or Danny's. The town tells the ending in its own voices, from what you did.

The five choices of the earlier page were decided on 24 November and are written in here as fact. One gap is noted, not solved (section 9).

## 1. The empire goal

### What Frank wants

Night one. Ron comes in off the rank with a dark coat over his arm and an envelope: Mickey's arrangement, down to the ferry landing after ten. Frank asks what Mickey got for it. Whatever he then does about it, he first says this, a fixed scene:

> RON: Left alone.
>
> FRANK: I had that for nothing, all the way up from the boat. Forty-one doors on this street, Ron. I counted. Not one opened. Thirty years. Two cars, a rent book, and this. Not for me. Every fare after dark goes in my book. Every door on this street rings my telephone. Every quay with a rope on it. By Christmas, Ron. Mickey's runs the Hook. And when they say Mickey's, they'll mean me.
>
> RON: Aye. Well. Envelope still wants carrying.

"Mickey's runs the Hook" is what the town repeats, at first as a joke on him.

The want is something to get, and a little wrong in canon's register: class, not greed. Frank is 32, the son of a Polish settler and Mickey Suddaby's sister, a stranger with £18 of rent due on Friday, and he wants to be somebody, quickly, in a town that did not come to the ferry to meet him. What he needs, and cannot see, is a place where somebody waits up for him. He has it on the first night (Ron on the rank, Sheila's kettle), and the empire is paid for in exactly that.

The question the game scores and the ending answers, which nobody ever says: what is a name on the Hook worth, and who pays for it? Two things keep the score in view. The name people call him. And Sheila's red book, which he first sees in week three: one line for every night, and beside each the name of whoever brought the job in. It is Ron's, unless Frank went alone. From week three, every Sunday, she reads the week's column back.

### How the rise is felt

In position, never in numbers, on four rungs that the town names: the new owner, Nowak, Frank, Frankie. Each change is felt: a named person says the new name to his face, a door opens, a new kind of night job appears. The weeks below are the pace of a player who works his nights; every change wakes on its state.

| Week | What he can see he has | Pauline, on the air | What opens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | two cars, Ron, Sheila, a debt book, a funeral suit | "the new owner" | the street, the rank, the landing |
| 2 | a third line on the board, a first man, the ferry rank | "Mr Nowak" | Mickey's locked office, Rita's back room |
| 3 | four cars on terms, a night desk, a first front and round, the contract, the decent jacket (£45) | "Mr Nowak", and she has stopped spelling it | the dock gate, the Harbour Board's counter |
| 4 | the landing and its nights; a fifth car | "Frank" | Garbutt's table, a solicitor |
| 5 | men at all three posts, two fronts, two rounds, the good overcoat (£90) | no name: "he wants you at the gate" | people step aside; then a cell, and out again |
| 6 | less: a post gone, the office searched, perhaps an arm in a sling | his name, said carefully | the back room at Grace's |
| 7 | what he can win back, door by door | what the week has earned | the last line of the book |

Cars come on terms (£90 down, £22 a week), which is why the board can fill. A firm with no fronts and no rounds stops at the second rung and the name Nowak. That is the honest firm. It may run four cars on terms and win the contract by lawful work, and it is a whole game: its Landing is done in the open, and its rivals still come.

Before the books open he can lose a post, the contract, a man, the licence for a fortnight; never the office, the radio or car two.

Frankie is the town's word for the man who runs the Hook. The Widow says it first, to own him. Ron says it once, if ever. Danny's needle is "nephew" until he is under Frank.

> What the player has to understand: three things, all on the surface. The name he is called. The board in the office, where cars, men and posts are chalked up and rubbed out. And what people tell him: each week's aim, each ask and each warning is said to his face before it lands, and kept in that person's words in the Ledger.

## 2. The town's powers

### The peace that died with Mickey

Mickey's outfit was half dead (canon): Ron, Sheila, two drivers and the arrangement. What it ran was the room every gang needed: a book of every night fare on the Hook and a radio nobody could help overhearing. Every other night a carbon of the night sheet went down to the landing in an envelope, to the old firm that worked it before the three rivals were anybody: who rode where after dark. For it the Widow let his cars through the dock gate untaxed, Agar renewed his licence unasked, and Danny Cammack drove car two. None of the three could take the Hook without the other two closing on it. The morning Ron found Mickey, that ended: in week one all three are looking at the office.

### Pacts, wars, truces and betrayals

PROPOSED. The player meets all of it through people.

- A pact is made with one rival against another, at his visit or at Garbutt's table. Its terms are spoken and kept in the Ledger. While it holds, that rival does not move against Frank.
- It breaks when Frank grows past what the ally can live with, when his men do a deed against the ally's, or when either side is offered more. A betrayal never comes without signs the player has seen: the ally stops coming in person, and asks for his share early. Its form is the ally's own: Danny talks and takes the rank, the Widow takes a man, Agar withdraws a paper.
- War is felt as what the other side brings: fists, then a gun shown, then a gun fired. It moves only on what was seen or heard, and never cools back to fists.
- A truce is Garbutt's one a game, holding one rival for two game weeks; paying a rival holds him as long.
- A rival's man who has been paid short, shamed or spared can be turned by a need met or a hook spent. A whole firm can come over once its head has lost: Danny back on the board, under Frank.

## 3. The core six

Frank, and five people carried through every week. Everybody else is light.

### Frank Nowak, 32

- Wants: to be the Hook's name by Christmas (section 1). His bond is Ron.
- Flaw: pride, and a stranger's need to be seen to win. Good at the wheel, and at walking straight up to things; canon never says where he learned, and nobody asks.
- His voice, in four lines. (1) Short and in order: what it is, then what he will do about it; he counts aloud: doors, minutes, money to the penny. (2) His temper is quiet: treated as Mickey's lad he does up the middle button of his jacket, goes formal (Mr Cammack, Mrs Jensen), says it once and does it with his hands; he does not shout, explain or say sorry. (3) Both names to everybody, spelt if needed; he never asks the way, a price twice or a favour. (4) His jokes are dry, come late, and are at his own cost or a thing's, never at somebody weaker.
- At every decision the player can pick one of two or three written lines in that voice, or speak his own. Samples: "Frank Nowak. Both names are mine." "Forty-one doors on this street. I counted. Not one opened." "That leaves five pound fifty. I'll try not to go mad."
- The habit the camera sees: the middle button, done up before anything he means. In plaster he cannot do it.
- The one deed only he would do, in week one. Danny Cammack leaves his car across Mickey's rank with the keys in it, because nobody on the Hook would touch it, and sits on Frank's counter. Frank, in the suit he brought for a funeral he missed, drives it down Quay Street, parks it on the quay's edge with the front wheels over the water, walks back, hangs the keys on Mickey's board and chalks a third line: CAR 3. He risks his body (Craig Fewson is on the wall beside Danny) and his standing (the parade is watching). Danny answers: he laughs, pays the fare, and a week later comes back with an offer. How far the player takes it is his (the corner, the edge, the water). The edge is where the two meet at the end.
- His second is offered in week five: his own name, signed, to keep Ron out (section 5).
- By path. With Danny, loudest and laziest. With the Widow, safest and smallest. Alone, most at risk in his own body. The honest firm, the same want by daylight.
- He can be hurt, arrested and held for hours. He cannot die.
- Fixed lines, at authored beats only; the rest of his talk is the player's:
  - "It's on my rank, so it's on my board. Car three. Two pound forty to the quay. Sheila, put it in the book."
  - "Nowak. N, O, W, A, K."

### Ron Kirby, 58: the bond

- Wants: to be a man who is sent for. A docker until 1989, and a docker is a man somebody wants at a gate at six. Mickey gave him a door to stand in.
- Flaw: he does not ask and does not tell. He will carry a thing for years before he says it is heavy.
- Code: you do not grass, you do not leave a man on the quay, you turn up.
- He cannot drive. Every morning since Mickey died he has started car two and sat in it with the handbrake on, so the battery keeps.
- The one deed only he would do: the day the office is searched he puts on the coat he wore to Mickey's funeral, walks into the station, asks for DS Ellis by name and offers a statement before anybody has asked for one. He will sign it at nine, unless Frank has signed first.
- By path. With Danny: they bicker down the dock road ("I've been overtaken by a hearse, Ron." "It were in a hurry."). With the Widow: back at her gate under the pact, and he may come out of the cells hers. He lives on every path.
- Voice:
  - "Coming up on the ice house. That's the ice house. Leaving the ice house."
  - "I've told her nowt that isn't so. I've just not told her you."

The bond is built in the hands, week by week:

- Week 1. Car two's engine, run for nobody. The ask on the rank, where Frank says what he wants (talk; trust). Lee Drewery's £15: Ron fills the doorway (action). Car three: Ron brings it onto the edge with a docker's hand signals; the closed fist is stop (action; the first time the player trusts his hands).
- Week 2. Lesson one, the dock road: Ron drives; the player has the handbrake and one word, "Stop" (drive). The naming of buildings begins, and there is one he does not name. The ferry rank, back to back before the boat queue (action).
- Week 3. The dawn convoy, Ron naming buildings a street late (drive). The gate: Frank Stathers offers him his old place in front of Frank, and a brass tally; he pockets it and says nothing (action; the first strain, his want against Frank's).
- Week 4. The Landing: in the plan, and at the wheel when it goes wrong (action and drive; trust at its height). The cut, settled in the car going home: paid from the tin like a crewman, or kept on Mickey's wage (drive; strain).
- Week 5. The gate again: Ron stood in the pen with the tally in his pocket, and nobody calling him (action, or Frank's absence; strain). Lesson five: he stops the car properly for once, puts the handbrake on himself and asks what is in the book (drive; the breach: Frank answers, true or false, and lets the brake off). Clearing the office the night before the search (action). The cells, and the night between (Frank's deed, or Ron's; the loss, or its price).
- Week 6. Free: holding the lamp in the yard, silent in the car. Inside: jobs he sends Frank on, by Father Walsh (action).
- Week 7. Free: he drives Frank to the quay, naming nothing. Inside: the visit. The last scene (a drive, or a hatch; the last word).

Seventeen beats, at least one a week, three strains before the breach; twelve are jobs or drives. Extra lessons, Mickey's Friday and Mickey's watch are invitations that cost nothing if refused.

### Sheila Dunn, 53: the book

- Wants: one book. Thirty-one years she has kept two, and she would like to end with one she could leave open on the counter.
- Flaw: she writes what she is told. She has never told a man no; she withholds instead: his name, a key, her opinion.
- The one deed only she would do: she sits at her desk through the whole search with her handbag on her knee, Mickey's real book and her own red book inside it under her knitting, and offers DS Ellis a mint from the same bag. (The red book is PROPOSED: her true count, in her hand, of each night as it really was and who brought it in.)
- Bond: she makes him say what he did, each morning, in words she can write, and reads them back in her own. She calls him new management until she trusts him; the day she says "Frank" is a rung of its own. She pulls against him at the first wash: "Same as your first night?"
- By path. Trusted: the firm's brain, who hands him the pen at the end. Asked to put the books off three counts running: she leaves until he comes to her door. Never trusted: she keeps the red book anyway. She is exact and slow, in book words; Pauline is quick and dry on the radio, kind off it.
- Voice:
  - "The book will hide ten pounds and four fifths of what the cars really drove. Past that I'm not keeping accounts, love, I'm writing you a reference."
  - "Thirty-one years I've written what I was told. You write this one."

### Danny Cammack, 27: the opponent

- Wants what Frank wants: to be the Hook's name. For himself: his name back on Mickey's board, at the top.
- Flaw: he cannot wait and cannot be second. He spends what he has not yet earned, money and trust alike.
- The one deed only he would do: he drove car two for Mickey and never gave back his key. A week after car three he takes Mickey's own car off the rank with it, roof sign lit, and drives Frank the whole Hook in six minutes by his own count, pricing every door, to stop on the quay's edge with the front wheels over. "That's how you park it." Asked for the key, he hands one over cheerfully. It is a door key.
- Bond: the only man on the Hook who treats Frank as an equal from the first day. He calls him "nephew" (his needle, outside the name ladder). He wants to know what Mickey's letter said of him. He pulls against him the morning after the Landing, when the town says Cammack took it and Danny lets it.
- His grievance is on the board from week two, where the player chalked it. Under car two's line the ghost of D. CAMMACK still shows in the paint, rubbed and never washed. At the offer Danny holds out the chalk, and the player chooses: the top line, the third line, or nowhere. On the third line he chalks a small arrow pointing up beside his name every time he is in the office; Sheila rubs it out each morning, and it is back by night. Left off the board, he chalks CAMMACK a foot high on the shutter of the empty unit three doors up, and goes over it. He asks twice to be the top line. That is the cause of his turn, made and seen. If he was given the top line no grievance is planted, and he does not turn: Craig Fewson, who wanted the seat Danny left, takes the old key off him and is the man on the quay, and Danny stands beside Frank or is the price of the fight.
- By path. Pact: car three is real, and he is the best earner the firm has. When he turns he does it in sight: from the box across the street he books car two to the police station over Mickey's own radio, and Frank can drive the fare himself. Refused: the loudest rival. The honest firm: he picks up the nights Frank put down, and offers Frank a wage.
- Voice:
  - "He's moved my motor. Mickey would've asked nicely for a fortnight and then said sorry."
  - "You've got the book, nephew. I am the book."

What the player watches him gain, week by week, each at Frank's cost even under a pact: (1) his car on Mickey's rank, and himself on the counter; (2) the ferry rank, and Mickey's car out on his own key; (3) the empty unit three doors up, with his men inside; (4) his name on the town's telling of the Landing; (5) a driver who starts calling him boss; (6) with Ron inside, his car on Mickey's rank again, and nobody moving it; (7) if he turned, the day book, off Frank's own counter.

### Maureen Jensen, the Widow, 57

- Wants: the dock gate and the men at it. For herself: to owe nobody and be owed by everybody.
- Flaw: she cannot ask. She can only give, and count.
- The one deed only she would do: the night he comes back from casualty the office is dark and the back light is on. Maureen Jensen, who has not walked up Quay Street in years, is sitting in Mickey's chair facing the door in her fur-collared coat, with a pot made and his dinner under a plate.
- Bond: the only rival who sees what he is short of. Frank's line protects her. She pulls against him at the Landing; and before it, at the gate in week three, when her man offers Ron his old place in front of Frank.
- By path. Ally: her men stand his posts and stay hers, and at the fourth rung she forecloses: they are gone in one morning. Rival: the wall he breaks at the Landing; she answers through Ron. Alone or honest: she taxes, poaches and waits.
- Voice:
  - "I never ask anybody for anything, Mr Nowak. I find out what they're short of."
  - "Sit down. It's been in the oven twice."

### DS Carol Ellis

- Wants: one case. For herself: the fire file closed in her own handwriting.
- Flaw: she moves only on paper. A thing is not so until somebody has signed it, which is why the street always knows more than she does.
- The one deed only she would do: in the searched office she reads Frank his own first line aloud, turns the book round on the counter, holds out her pen and says, "Write it again. Underneath. Same words."
- Bond: the one person who wants to hear everything he has done and would never pass it on as talk. She pulls against him at every deed with a witness, in the cells, and at the trade.
- By path. The more that is seen, the faster she climbs; nothing unseen moves her. A killing gives her the case by another door, and he can be hunted. Bought: her case cools, and she loses her best line once, when he needs it. She is the same woman bought: every envelope goes down in a notebook of her own, with the date. A missed Friday and the notebook is the case.
- Voice:
  - "I'm not asking you to remember everything. I'm asking you to remember it the same way twice." (her approved line)
  - "Somebody always saw, Mr Nowak. My trouble is finding the one who'll sign."
  - Bought: "A hundred, Fridays. I'll write it down. I write everything down; that's what you're paying for."

### The early deed: the line in the book

Night one's job leaves half a line in the day book. Pauline logs every time a man leaves the rank, so for ten she wrote the time, "Office", and in the column for whoever brought the job in, R.K. She left the rest. The old firm's man sees that half line on the carbon at the landing and says only: "Somebody's started writing you down."

Next morning Sheila is cashing up, and the half line is in her way. It is the first job's paperwork, nothing more.

> SHEILA: Pauline's started a line and not finished it. Ten o'clock, booked by Ron, then nothing. I can't carry half a line forward. What was it?

### The midpoint: the Landing (week four)

Frank is arrested on the entry: custody for hours. Then, through the wall: "That you, boss? Say nowt." ## 6. The story director

PROPOSED; not yet in the blueprint. A small scheduler over a pool of authored beats. It never moves the world; it only chooses which beat, among those whose conditions are already true, plays next. A game day is 48 real minutes, so every rule below is in game time, with the real time in brackets.

Each beat is a card (paper/SPINE-CARDS.md): its conditions in world state, its weight, a shape card of seven fields (opening, turn, ending, action, place, company, mood), who can carry it and a fallback, and the sign shown before it lands.

- Pacing. After Act I something reaches Frank at least once in every fifteen game hours (thirty real minutes). Act II runs in at least four stages of at most five game days (four real hours), each ending in a change he can see on the board. At most one heavy beat a game week, a rest or a funny beat after each, and at least two and a half game days (two real hours) between two heavy beats. A slow player's gaps fill with side stories. On an authored ride the game clock slows to the pace of the talk.
- The week's aim. The Ledger always holds the week's aim in its speaker's words and, beneath it, one named next thing (who, where, what for). No markers.
- No repeats. Neighbouring beats differ on at least three of the seven shape fields; no beat plays twice.
- Late, absent, or the carrier is gone. Every main beat has a second way in: the old firm's man brings the ask to the door; if car three sits a day, Danny moves it and tells it his way. The simulation may take away only who is alive, who knows what, who is where, who will talk, and how the law stands.

Two chairs keep the spine standing; the third is never empty. If Danny is dead or gone, or was given the top line and never turned, Craig Fewson has his key and is the man on the quay. If Sheila has left, the book lies on the counter with the pen on it on the last morning. Ron lives.

## 7. How memory, gossip and talk carry the story

- Said to his face: every deed of weight with a named witness returns as a named person's remark within a game day.
- Told never above seen: tellings lose detail and never gain it, so the town's version of Frank is something he can hear, correct or use.
- Returning people: anybody he hurt, shamed, spared, paid off or let down goes into a pool, and later beats are cast from it.
- Live talk: anybody can be spoken to, and says only what the simulation knows.
- The name ladder is the town's verdict, spoken.

Worked example 1: a telling he can use. Car three is seen by Ada and Shaun Hewson. By noon Brenda has it whole; Lee Drewery has it third-hand: "Was it Ron?" The player can leave Ron's name on it, or claim it, and that wins Lee.

Worked example 2: one debt, three returns. Dennis Iveson owes £80 and has it. Take it off him, and he stops it out of his yard hand's wages; in week six Neil Screeton is the hardest man in the yard: "Eighty pound, Mr Nowak. I've your change here." Go into the hut at night, and Norman Dent has a noise and a shape: in week five it is a description in Ellis's notebook. Lean on what Iveson bought out of the burned warehouse, and he is the one the player later sees leaning in at Ellis's car window.

Worked example 3: a returning enemy. On the ferry rank in week two the Widow's taxman, Mark Billany, goes down. Frank can hand him his cap, or hit him again while he is down. In week four Billany is at the Landing. Spared: "That's us straight." Struck when down, he leans on the horn until the gate men come.

With the line, car three, the chalk, the tally, Iveson's debt, the cut, the pen and the side stories, more than a dozen acts of his own return as scenes. No wrong deed is forced.

## 8. How the ending is assembled

Every result at five reaches one close. The firm survives: the Kingdom. The firm is lost and a friend still counts him (Ron, Sheila, Alison or Danby): the Straight Life. The firm is lost and no friend does: Burn Both. Each is assembled from three things. The spine's choice: whose name the line carries. The empire's state when the books open at five: the built rule reads the save (the business, a friendship, charges, anybody given to Ellis, a successor), with two changes: who holds the book after the quay, and Ron and Sheila counting as friends. And what the town remembers: about five lines, spoken by named people who each hold a deed of his at first hand; what each rival still holds; and the last name he is called, and by whom. Nobody sums up. Every close is the same Saturday: the Harbour Board chains the old dock gate, the pen stands empty, and what differs is who is there to see it. The three endings are the built ones, each with a better and a plainer turn.

### The Kingdom

The books hold. The board is full, Pauline is calling cars to every door on the Hook, and people on the parade step aside. He has exactly what he said on the first night: they say Mickey's and they mean him. The sign still says Mickey's. His man stands his post in front of a chained gate.

If Ron went in, he is where he asked to be left, and their last scene is through a hatch: "Boss." Nothing comes after it. Frank drives home past the ice house, and the car is quiet. The Man in Black, putting his hat on: "I don't mark those." And the Widow, from her chair: "Frankie." It is the top of the ladder, and she is the one saying it.

If Frank signed, Ron is on the door and the charge is read in the close: he has the Hook, and a date. The name is Ron's to say, and he does not. The better turn, which is rare, needs what the rule already asks, a friend, a street that holds none of his rackets as fact, no serious charge and nobody given to Ellis, and so only a Ron kept out the cheaper way. Whoever holds that hook is in the close, holding it. If Frank came for him at the pen and Mickey's watch is on his wrist, Ron names one building on the last drive. One.

### Burn Both

Neither the firm nor a friend. He held on and a morning fell; or he bargained the book away at his own counter; or he named a name and lost both anyway. Another firm's cars stand on the rank, and the chain goes on the gate with nobody of his there to see it. Ron goes to work for the Widow, and her tally hangs where his coat did. Shaun Hewson has his ladder against the front: "Wants doing. What's it to say?" Nobody tells him. The long letter from Customs is spelt right. What is left is the player's to pick up: the new firm's envelope, to carry down to the landing for three pounds a night and answer to another man's name; or his suitcase, and the last boat.

If he is hunted for a killing when the books open, each ending plays from that state: the close is spoken about him, and not to him.

## 9. Decided on 24 November, and three answers

Decided by the owner, and written in above as fact:

1. The lead is Frank Nowak; the ladder is the new owner, Nowak, Frank, Frankie.
2. The main opponent is Danny Cammack. He once drove Mickey's own car, car two, and never gave the key back.
3. The big loss: Ron goes inside unless Frank pays to stop it. Ron never dies.
4. The ending counts Ron and Sheila as friends, beside Alison and Danby.
5. Mickey's envelope holds a carbon copy of the night's fares.

With them: the 48-minute game day; the economy (Ellis can be bought; an honest firm stops at the second rung); a living town with a light spine, never a mission line.

Three questions raised by this revision, answered by the lead the same day:

1. The 48-minute day stands. Seven game weeks at that rate is about 39 real hours against the release ruling of 15 to 20; the gap is noted, not solved.
2. Ellis can be bought on the night between only with £800 cash in hand; otherwise that way is closed.
3. Frank Stathers stays: "There's one Frank on this gate."

## 10. What changed in this revision

1. The lead's name changed on 24 November. The ending is kept on the book.
2. The five choices are decided; Ellis can be bought; the honest firm stops at the second rung.
3. The loss, the weekly aims and the 25 side stories are the brief's. The breach is Frank's answer in lesson five.
4. The line in the book finishes Pauline's half line, and he reads it again in week three.
5. The turn's tip is a leak the player watched; Danny's grievance is chalked where the player put him.
6. The arm is lost only by a choice in the yard; a lost quay fight brings Danny to the counter.
7. Two of the five mornings are set pieces; pacing is restated in game time.
8. After the blind scene readers: Frank has a written voice and a habit; the want speech starts from the forty-one doors; the yard is a midnight settle; the room at Grace's is a funeral tea; Burn Both ends on an errand or a boat.

## 11. The readers' marks

Six blind readers scored the earlier frame 24.3 of 30, then 26.3. Three more gave the 23 scenes about 7.0 of 10, and named Frank's missing voice first.
