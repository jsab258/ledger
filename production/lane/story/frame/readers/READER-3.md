# Reader 3: score of the story frame (STORY-FRAME-2026-11-26)

Read as a story editor who has shipped open-world crime games, and as a player who loves them: what the play described would make a player feel, in most playthroughs, not only on the best path. Only the brief, the principles and the frame were read.

## 1. The fifteen principles

| Principle | Mark | Quote from the plan | To raise it |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 A lead the player wants to be: loud want, buried need | 2 | "By Christmas, Ron. Mickey's runs the Hook. And when they say Mickey's, they'll mean me." | Make the after-state a change of verbs the player can name (he cannot take a post by force, only by asking), not a sentence about him. |

## 2. Total

24 out of 30.

Of the seven heavy principles (P1, P2, P3, P5, P6, P8, P14), none fails; P2 and P8 are halves.

## 3. The three largest lacks

1. The middle after the Landing turns into upkeep. Posts that need a man every day, a stare every time, a post lost after three empty days, poaching of the least loyal: to a player this is the turf-holding chore loop that has worn out other crime games' second halves. The plan names it as the change in play, but what the player does with his hands in weeks five and six is maintenance between a few big beats. Week three's five mornings and week seven's five mornings also share one shape.

2. The heaviest moment of the story belongs to Ron and a pen, not to Tom. The loss is Ron's own deed, and the plan says nothing Tom does changes it; Tom is arrested for two minutes, bailed and walks out past the hatch while Ron signs. The yard is a fight he "cannot win". The climax is writing a line at a counter after a week of a courteous VAT officer. For a player who came for tension and a rise to power, the fall happens to him and the end is paperwork, however well it is written.

3. Too much machinery under the feeling. A dial of four, five-step ladders per rival, four police stages, four name rungs, pacts, truces, bought men, a post timer, graded Customs mornings. The plan hides numbers, but several weekly aims still need a rule explained, and side stories, the ending's dock loss and the friend count are covered by general sentences rather than named payoffs.

## 4. Where the simulation will most likely break the spine

1. The turn may never wake in the middle. It needs Ellis at "investigating" and someone close naming the night. A careful player who keeps witnesses down, pays the bought men and never shows a gun may never get Ellis there, and then the loss slides to the inspector in Act III or does not land at all; Act IIb loses its fall and week seven loses Ron's visit. Fix: give the turn the same fail-safe the sit-down has: by the end of week five a tip arrives regardless (Danny, Wardell, Customs, or the old firm's man), its source read from the record, so Ellis reaches investigating on time on every path.

2. Ron's sacrifice will feel forced to the players who did most for him. A player who told the truth, paid Ron the cut, went to him at the gate and redeemed the watch still watches Ron walk in, with "nothing" able to change it, then loses the yard fight by design. Good conduct that buys nothing reads as the rails. Fix: let the record open one real way to keep Ron out (claim the line on paper, a brief, the bought statement) at a price the player feels for Act III, such as the licence or a post; Ron then goes in only if Tom will not pay it, or goes in against Tom's spoken offer, which is a choice of Ron's the player can respect. Let the yard be survivable on his feet if the right people come.

3. The middle will repeat and bunch. Weeks are only "the likely pace"; a fast player reaches the third rung early, the Landing wakes, and the holding, the collision, the post or Ron and the search can stack within a few hours, while a slow player gets long stretches of post-standing with the director offering rest beats and side stories as filler. Fix: make each post's defence a single authored crisis with its own place, job and danger, let posts be held by assigned men without a daily timer unless a beat targets them, and have the director hold back the next heavy beat until a minimum of play has passed since the last.

## 5. Revision or rethink

Revision, 70 per cent sure. The bones are right and rare: a want a player can say on the first night, an opponent who wants the same thing and sits in Tom's own car, a routine first-morning line that comes back as the turn, a bond built on drives, a world visibly closing, and humour from character in every week. What holds it below the bar is where the weight falls in play (Ron's deed, a paper climax, an upkeep middle) and how much rule sits under the feeling, which revision can move without changing the frame. The doubt is whether a light spine on a living town can guarantee the turn and the shared hours with Ron in most playthroughs; that needs fail-safes written in, not hoped for.

## 6. Do I recognise it?

None. It borrows recognisable shapes (a debt collected early that returns, as in Red Dead Redemption 2; territory that must be held after it is taken, as in some Mafia games), but I know no published game or project with this premise, setting or spine.
