## 3. The three largest lacks

1. The heaviest scene is Ron's, not Tom's. Ron walks in, Ron signs, Ron chooses; the plan says plainly that "nothing changes what Ron means to do". The player's hands only shade who Ron is shielding. A player who has spent five weeks building will feel the writer take the wheel at the one moment the story most needs it to be his. And after the loss the central bond is two weeks of talk through a hatch, so the relationship ends in standing scenes, the opposite of how it was built.
2. The book machinery is clever on paper and hard in play. A free-typed line compared five weeks later, a day book, Mickey's real book, Sheila's proposed true count, five audit mornings graded from the books: the last act, which should be the loudest stretch, is largely paperwork at a counter, and the player must understand an accounting idea to know what the final choice costs. The quay fight with Danny sits the night before, apart from the choice, so the climax is split between a brawl and a form.
3. The opposition is spread across too many forks. Danny can be ally or rival, his turn is conditional with a Customs-letter fail-safe, and on the pact road his gains are the player's gains. The turn itself is driven by Ellis, and the ending by a VAT officer met only in week seven. The player risks feeling that the person he finally fights on the quay is not the person who brought him down.

## 4. Where the simulation will break the spine

1. The turn will not wake. It needs Ellis at "investigating" and somebody close naming the night, and Ellis "moves only on paper" and only on what was seen. A careful, quiet player never gets her there, so the fallback pushes the line's reading into Act III, Ron never goes in, and weeks five and six lose their spine. Fix: give the turn a guaranteed authored trigger by the end of week five that does not depend on her stage (the old firm's man taken at the landing with that night's carbon on him), and let her stage set only how hard it lands.
2. The loss will feel forced. Ron signs whatever Tom says through the wall, whatever the line held, even after a truthful line that put nothing on him but the column. A player who did everything right sees the same sacrifice as one who did everything wrong. Fix: give the player one real, costly way to stop it (Tom walks into the station first in the night between, taking the charge, losing the licence and the posts for the rest of the game), so Ron's going in becomes something the player allowed.
3. The middle will repeat. After the Landing "a post needs a man every day, and one empty three game days is lost", rivals poach, a man near his limit looks away. Empire-defence loops of this kind turn into chores in every game that has tried them, and Act III then adds five mornings of the same counter scene. Fix: let posts hold themselves on men's loyalty, with the player called only to authored threats each of a different shape, and give each of the five mornings a different kind of play away from the counter (a chase for Kevin's missing miles, a break-in for the invoice, a drive to the gate for the receipt).

## 5. Revision or rethink

Revision, 70 per cent sure. The bones are the right ones: a loud want said on the first night, an early ordinary deed that returns as the turn, an opponent who sits in the lead's own car, a bond built at the wheel, and a ladder that climbs to the lead's body and liberty. What keeps it from the owner's bar is who holds the climax, how much accounting the player must follow, and whether the triggers fire in a quiet playthrough, and all three can be mended without changing the premise. The doubt is that a frame waking on world state rarely delivers authored peaks of Red Dead Redemption 2's weight, so the fixes above must make the five heaviest scenes land on every path, not only on the one the plan describes.

## 6. Do I recognise it?

None. It echoes the rise-and-hold shape of the Mafia games and Sleeping Dogs, and British crime films of the late 1970s and 1980s, but I know no published game or project with this premise.
