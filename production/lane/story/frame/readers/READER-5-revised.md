## 3. The three largest lacks

1. The loss is soft and can be bought back. Ron goes inside alive, Tom can stop it, and "Write it true" lets Ron walk out at five. A careful player, the very one who loved Ron, will lose nobody for good. The heaviest feeling in the game sits on a typed line from a ten-second chat in week one, and on paperwork at a counter. RDR2's losses are in bodies; this one is in a ledger, and players remember bodies.
2. The spine is held up by its engine. Rungs, ladders, the dial, fail-safes for the tip, the sit-down and Danny's turn, three chairs, a director with cooldowns. Each is sensible; together they mean the week table describes one likely run, and the player's aim in the middle (why the Landing now, which head at the sit-down) comes from states he is told he will never see.
3. The ending is split and the last act is a checklist. A fistfight on the quay at four, then a choice with a pen at five; five mornings of fetching items for a VAT inspector before that. The middle (the Landing, the gate, the yard) is louder than the last act, the opposite of what P10 asks, and a lost fight hands the choice away entirely.

## 4. Where the simulation is most likely to break the spine

1. The turn will not land (the line in the book). Players type anything: jokes, half-truths, "dropped something off", nonsense. A classifier sorting that into lie, truth or blank will misfile some, and five weeks later Ellis's reading feels like a trick the player was never told he was playing. Fix: after the typed answer Sheila reads it back in her own words and names its kind ("So that's a waiting fare, cash. I'll put that."), and in week three the player is shown the line itself in the red book, so the return is recognised, not sprung.
2. A loss that will feel forced (the yard and the quay). "The hardest fight in it costs him the arm either way" is a scripted injury in a fight the player can win, which players resent more than any other forced loss; and losing the quay fight "makes the choice for him", removing the ending from the player one-armed by design. Fix: let the arm be lost only if he takes the hardest man himself (a choice with a sign), and let a lost quay fight change the terms of the five o'clock choice (Danny holds the book; Tom must bargain at the counter) rather than remove it.
3. A middle that will repeat (Act IIb into Act III). The three post crises each wake only "when its man is leaned on"; a player with fewer posts, a lost Landing or a wound-down firm sees fewer of them, and the director fills with side stories. Then Act III is five mornings, each a fetch, with "won back door by door" between. That is a daily round in all but name. Fix: one authored week-five set piece that wakes on the calendar regardless of posts, and turn two of the five inspector items into heavy-ish set pieces with their own danger (Kevin's chase at night with Danny's men also after the log; the break-in at Agar's with Ellis's constable on the beat).

## 5. Revision or rethink

Revision, 70 per cent sure. The bones are right for the bar: a lead with a loud want in the first hour, an opponent inside the office with a key to its car, a bond built in drives and jobs, and an early deed that returns as the turn. What holds it below Red Dead Redemption 2 is weight, not shape: a loss that can be bought back and undone, a last act of errands, and a middle whose week-by-week shape is promised only on the likely path. Each of those can be fixed inside this frame, but if the owner will not let the loss be permanent on every path, I would drop to 50 per cent that revision alone gets there.
