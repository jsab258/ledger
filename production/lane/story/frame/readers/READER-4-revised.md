## 3. The three largest lacks

1. Too much engine for the player to follow. Four paths, pacts, the dial, four ladders, posts held by loyalty and nerve, Ellis's stages, three kinds of answer in the book, three ways through the night between each with conditions, three chairs. The plan says the player sees only three things, but weeks five and six only make sense once the reader knows what fires the sit-down and what a post is. Clarity in the middle is where it is weakest.
2. The heart loss is soft. Ron goes inside alive, by his own choice, and can be stopped; on the "sign first" path no person is lost at all. The "breach" is one question answered in a car, not a rupture the player causes and feels. Set against Hosea or Arthur, this is a sacrifice of loyalty, which moves less than a loss the lead did with his hands.
3. The turn rests on a typed sentence. Sheila's speech ("Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule") flags the moment, so a careful player treats it as a test; a careless one forgets what he typed. Either way the return in week five risks reading as a gotcha rather than as a deed coming home. And the side stories, beyond five, are named only by number.

## 4. Where the simulation will most likely break the spine

1. Danny on the quay will feel forced on the pact path. A player who keeps Danny happy has a best earner who, if nothing else turns him, turns on the Customs letter by fail-safe and steals the book. That reads as the writer's hand. If Danny is dead, Craig Fewson stands on the quay as a man the player barely met. Fix: make Danny's grievance a visible line on the board from week two, rising each time the player chooses where to chalk his name, so every path has seen at least two player-caused steps before the turn; and give Fewson a scene beside Danny in week one or two.
2. The night between will feel forced on a clean record. The tip reaches Ellis "by the end of week five regardless", and Ron walks in on his own whatever the player has done. A player who told the truth finds Ron's name in a column he never saw. Fix: show that column in week three when Sheila shuts the red book (the player sees R. Kirby beside the landing nights), and let Ron's walk-in follow from his question in lesson five, so the answer the player gave is visibly what sends him.
3. Act IIa will repeat. Its beats come "in any order the states allow", the ferry rank wants "five days in a fortnight", the contract five mornings of the same convoy, the rounds are daily, and the weekly rule (one rung, one firm change, one return) is itself a loop. A slow player gets a fortnight of standing, driving and collecting. Fix: split Act IIa into two fixed grounds (the ferry rank and the docks), each ending on one built set piece with danger to Tom, and turn the counted requirements into one run the week leads up to.

## 5. Revision or rethink

Revision, 65 per cent sure. The frame is already sound where most plans fail: a plain want said on night one, a close opponent who wants the same thing, a bond kept in the car, a ladder of stakes and a last day that pays off the first morning. What stands between it and Red Dead Redemption 2 is execution risk, not shape: the system load on the player's clarity, a central loss gentle enough to be skipped, and a turn hung on a typed line. Those are fixed by cutting and sharpening inside the same frame, though the doubt is real that a director over a simulated town can land the turn with the weight a fixed script gives.
