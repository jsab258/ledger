# LEDGER: the story frame

Dated 26 November 2026, written 24 November. Phase 1 of the story: the frame the full scenes will be written into, from the lead's structure of 24 November, inside canon, the rulings and the blueprint. PROPOSED marks what reaches past them, OPEN a choice that is Jafar's, and (proposed) a name minted here. Sample lines show a voice; they are not final script.

## For Jafar: the story on one page

What Tom wants. Tom Nowak steps off the ferry with one suitcase, £23.50 and a dead uncle's cab office. On his first night he says it to Ron on the rank: by Christmas every night fare, every door and every quay on the Hook goes through Mickey's, and when people say Mickey's they will mean him. He wants to be somebody, fast, in a town that has never heard of him.

How the empire grows. You never see a number. You see cars on the board, your men at the dock gate, the cold store and the landing, shops that wash your money, doors that open, people stepping aside, and the name the town calls you: the new owner, Nowak, Tom, Tommy. You can fight the three gangs, pay them, side with one against another, turn their men, and take a beaten gang in.

The opponent. Danny Cammack, Mickey's old driver, who thinks the office should have been his. Quicker and funnier than Tom, and wanting the same thing, he can be your best earner or your loudest enemy. Every week you watch him gain something. At the end you face him on the quay.

The seven weeks. One: nothing, nobody, rent due Friday. Two: cars, ranks, your first men. Three: the docks at dawn, and the big contract. Four: the Landing, a planned night job on the Widow's ground; after it you hold what you have instead of grabbing more. Five: they come for your men, and for Ron. Six: they come for you. Seven: the tax inspector every morning, and Danny at night.

The turn and the loss. On your first morning Sheila asks what goes in the fare book for the night before, and you choose the words. Five weeks later DS Ellis reads that line back and tells you to write it again. Ron takes the blame and goes inside in your place. He lives.

How it ends. With the book open on the counter, you decide whose name pays: keep the firm and leave Ron inside, give Ellis the true book and lose the firm to free him, or hand her Danny. The town then closes the story in its own voices, from what you actually did.

Your choices:

1. Main opponent: (A) Danny [recommended]; (B) the Widow; (C) all three equal.
2. The big loss: (A) Ron inside in Tom's place, alive [recommended]; (B) Ron dies; (C) none fixed.
3. Canon: Danny once drove Mickey's own car: (A) yes [recommended]; (B) no.
4. The friend the ending counts: (A) Ron and Sheila too [recommended]; (B) only Alison or Danby, as now; (C) cut them.
5. Mickey's envelope holds: (A) a copy of the night's fares [recommended]; (B) money; (C) never shown.

## 1. The empire goal

### What Tom wants

Night one. Ron comes in off the rank with a dark coat over his arm and an envelope: Mickey's arrangement, down to the ferry landing after ten. Whatever Tom does about it, he first says this, a fixed scene under the one lit sign:

> TOM: Thirty years, and that's the firm? An old man walking an envelope down to the water?
>
> RON: He was nobody's trouble, boss. There's a living in that.
>
> TOM: Not for me. Every fare after dark, every door on this street, every quay with a rope on it: it goes through this office or it doesn't go. By Christmas, Ron. Mickey's runs the Hook. And when they say Mickey's, they'll mean me.
>
> RON: Aye. Well. Envelope still wants carrying.

"Mickey's runs the Hook" is what the town repeats, at first as a joke on him.

The want is something to get, and a little wrong in canon's register: class, not greed. He is 32, a stranger, with £23.50 and £18 of rent due on Friday. He wants to be somebody, quickly, in a town that did not come to the ferry to meet him. What he needs, and cannot see, is a place where somebody waits up for him. He has that on the first night (Ron on the rank, Sheila's kettle), and the empire is paid for in exactly that.

The question the game scores and the ending answers, which nobody ever says: what is a name on the Hook worth, and who pays for it? Two things keep the score in view: the name people call him, and whose name stands beside which night in Sheila's book.

### How the rise is felt

In position, never in numbers (ruling of 30 October), on the blueprint's four rungs: the nephew, Nowak, Tom, the port's man. Each change is felt: a named person says the new name to his face, a door opens, a new kind of night job appears. Every week brings one rival rung, one firm change and one return of a deed. Weeks are the likely pace; every change wakes on its state.

## 2. The town's powers

### The peace that died with Mickey (PROPOSED)

Mickey ran no gang. He ran the room every gang needed: a book of every night fare on the Hook and a radio nobody could help overhearing. Every other night a carbon of the night sheet, names, doors and times, went down to the landing in an envelope, to the old firm that worked it before the three rivals were anybody (OPEN 5). For that the Widow let his cars through the dock gate untaxed, Agar renewed his licence unasked, and Danny Cammack drove car two (OPEN 3). None of the three could take the Hook without the other two closing on it. The morning Ron found Mickey, that ended, which is why in week one all three are looking at the office.

### Pacts, wars, truces and betrayals

PROPOSED, the frame's main new system; the dial, the ladders, the truce and the bought men are the blueprint's.

- A pact is made with one rival against another, at that rival's visit, a sit-down, or a meeting Garbutt brokers. Its terms are spoken and kept in the Ledger: which posts each holds, one joint job, whose ground each keeps off. While it holds, the ally's ladder does not climb against Tom.
- It breaks when Tom grows past what the ally can live with (the fourth rung makes every ally a rival), when his men do a deed against the ally's, when the ally is offered more, or when Tom breaks it.
- Betrayal is a beat the director may wake once those conditions turn, never without signs: the ally stops coming in person, asks for his share early, and his men are seen with another firm's. Its form is the ally's own: Danny talks and takes the rank, the Widow takes a man, Agar withdraws a paper.
- War is the dial (0 Fists, 1 Shown, 2 Fired, 3 War) and each rival's ladder (noticed, a visit, pressure, a threat, a sit-down). The dial moves only on what was seen or heard, and falls one step after about two quiet game weeks, never to 0.
- A truce is Garbutt's one a game, holding one rival at its rung for exactly two game weeks; or a pact. Paying a rival holds him two game weeks unless Tom does a deed against him.
- A rival's man whose loyalty to his head is low (paid short, shamed, his head lost a sit-down, Tom spared him or helped his family) can be turned by a need met or a hook spent. He brings a true read of his head's habits, and his old head may send for him. A whole firm can come over the same way once its head has lost: Danny back on the board, under Tom.

A pact with Agar plays as paper, and counts below as the road alone.

## 3. The core six

Tom, and five people carried through every week. Everybody else is light.

### Tom Nowak, 32

- Wants: to be the Hook's name by Christmas (section 1). His bond is Ron.
- Flaw: pride, a stranger's need to be seen to win, and a temper that goes when he is treated as Mickey's lad.
- Good at: the wheel, and walking straight up to things. Nobody asks where he learned (canon: his past is never told).
- Laughed at for: giving his whole name to everybody, like a man standing for the council, while the town calls him the new owner; and never asking the way.
- The one deed only he would do, in week one. Danny Cammack leaves his car across Mickey's rank with the keys in it, because nobody on the Hook would touch it, and sits on Tom's counter. Tom, in the suit he brought for a funeral he missed, drives it down Quay Street, parks it on the quay's edge with the front wheels over the water, walks back, hangs the keys on Mickey's board and chalks a third line: CAR 3. He risks his body (two of Danny's men are on the wall) and his standing (the parade is watching). Danny answers: he laughs, pays the fare, and a week later comes back with an offer. How far the player takes it is his (the corner, the edge, the water). The edge is where the two meet at the end.
- By path. With Danny he is loudest, and laziest: Danny does the running. With the Widow, safest and smallest. Alone, most at risk in his own body. Wound down, he wants the same thing by daylight.
- He can be hurt, arrested and held for hours. He cannot die.
- Fixed lines, at authored beats only; the rest of his talk is the player's:
  - "It's on my rank, so it's on my board. Car three. Two pound forty to the quay. Sheila, put it in the book."
  - "Nowak. N, O, W, A, K." (the line he says most)
  - "I wasn't lost. I was early." (kept from The Knowledge)

### Ron Kirby, 58: the bond

- Wants: to be a man who is sent for. A docker until 1989, and a docker is a man somebody wants at a gate at six. Mickey gave him a door to stand in.
- Flaw: he does not ask and does not tell. He will carry a thing for years before he says it is heavy.
- Code: you do not grass, you do not leave a man on the quay, you turn up.
- The one deed only he would do: the day the office is searched he puts on the coat he wore to Mickey's funeral, walks into the station on his own feet, asks for DS Ellis by name and offers a statement before anybody has asked for one.
- By path. With Danny: he loses the front seat, and they bicker down the dock road ("I've been overtaken by a hearse, Ron." "It were in a hurry."). With the Widow: he is back at the gate under the pact, and may come out of the cells hers. Alone: he is hurt worst. Wound down: glad of it, and he still goes in for the crooked years.
- Voice:

  - "On the quay you never wrote a man down. You remembered him or you didn't."

### Sheila Dunn, 53: the book

- Wants: one book. Thirty-one years she has kept two, and she would like to end with one she could leave open on the counter.
- Flaw: she writes what she is told. She has never told a man no; she withholds instead: his name, a key, her opinion.
- The one deed only she would do: she sits at her desk through the whole search with her handbag on her knee, Mickey's real book and her own true count inside it under her knitting, and offers DS Ellis a mint from the same bag. (The true count is PROPOSED: a third book, in her hand, of each night's money as it really was. It is the true book of the final choice.)
- Bond: she makes him say what he did, each morning, in words she can write. She calls him new management until she trusts him; the day she says "Tom" is a rung of its own. She pulls against him at the first wash: "Same as your first night?"

- Wants: the dock gate and the men at it. For herself: to owe nobody and be owed by everybody.

### DS Carol Ellis

- Wants: one case. For herself: the fire file closed in her own handwriting.
- Flaw: she moves only on paper. A thing is not so until somebody has signed it, which is why the street always knows more than she does.
- The one deed only she would do: in the searched office she reads Tom his own first line aloud, turns the book round on the counter, holds out her pen and says, "Write it again. Underneath. Same words."

- Voice:

  - "Somebody always saw, Mr Nowak. My trouble is finding the one who'll sign."

## 5. The spine

Beats wake on states, never on a mission list. Dates appear only where a person names them or the town's calendar has them. No beat can fail the run.

### The early deed: the line in the book (decided)

The morning after the first ask that reaches him, whatever he did with the envelope (carried it, refused it, opened it), Sheila turns the day book to that night.

> SHEILA: Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule, and it's mine now. So. What was last night?

The player answers in his own words; she reads them back; his plain yes puts them in ink. Three kinds of answer: a lie, which is a cover fare ("Ferry landing and back, waited, cash"); the truth ("An envelope to the landing, for Mickey's arrangement"); or blank ("Leave it"), after which Sheila, who has never left a hole in a book, writes something of her own.

The line is stored word for word. It is ordinary play, the first job's paperwork, done in ten seconds and forgotten in an hour. It returns three times.

1. The habit (Act IIa). The first time night money must pass through the book, her pen is up: "Same as your first night?" His first answer has become the firm's way of writing, and every wash hangs from it. He can change it here; most players will not notice they were asked.
2. The turn (Act IIb). In the searched office Ellis reads the line aloud and has him write it again underneath. The Core compares the kind of answer and the facts in it with what he said five weeks before; the model only classifies.
   - A lie: can he remember it the same way twice? If so, she has one false entry under the owner's name. If not, two.
   - The truth: he can write it without thinking. It is a confession with a date, and in the column for the man who brought the job in, the name is R. Kirby. The book has no column for owners.
   - Blank: there is a line there, in Sheila's hand, that he has never seen and cannot write. "Then these are Mrs Dunn's words."
3. The last line (Act III). The inspector keeps week one for his last morning. Sheila turns the book round, lays her pen on it and takes her hand away. What Tom writes, and under whose name, is the ending.

### Act I, The Inheritance

Opens on the ferry; closes on Sheila's Sunday question. Stakes: money. The most authored act; the nine first-week designs stand. Its beats: arrival, the ask and the want, the line, five debts (every debtor goes into the pool of people who will meet him again), Ada's tea, Alison's fare to the warehouse row, Friday's rent, Sunday.

Each rival takes a first look, and Tom answers in his own way. The Widow's men tax Kevin at the dock gate: pay, defy or counter. If he pays, he asks for a receipt in front of the whole pen, and a day later gets one in her own hand, made out to Mr Nowack. Agar's letter about the licence is addressed to Mr Novak. Danny's car is across the rank.

By path. Take it over: the arrangement stands. Wind it down: it ends that night, and every rival starts a rung higher. Refuse to say: the street decides, once a third car, a first man or the ask taken twice in week two makes the firm his. Every road reaches the second rung. A player who stops here has had a small whole story: a stranger arrives with nothing, makes one enemy laugh and gets a name.

### Act IIa, The Rise

Opens at the second rung; closes when the midpoint wakes. Stakes: the firm's things and his own skin. Beats, in any order the states allow:

- The old firm's end. Kept: it hands over the landing, its men and its night custom, and its own people go out on the morning boat. Ended: the landing stands empty until the highest rival fills it.
- A first man, by need (slow, and it holds) or by a hook (quick, and brittle). Sheila: "What do I put him down as?"
- Danny's visit: car two, six minutes, the offer. His men, his car on the board and his nerve, for a share of the nights; he opens at half. Take it, refuse it, or keep him waiting.
- The ferry rank: a car on it five days in a fortnight, a word with whoever holds it, or fists in front of the boat queue.

### The loss (OPEN 2, written as A)

Tom is bailed that evening and walks out past the hatch. Not a word is false, and Tom is not in it. A man who admits a thing and will not name the rest is a man Ellis has to keep.

The player's hands change whom Ron is shielding (Tom after a lie, Sheila after a blank, nobody after the truth); how deep Ron is in (the wage or the cut); what Ron thinks of him as the door shuts (lesson five); what waits at the desk (Mickey's watch, if Tom redeemed it: "Tell him it's five minutes fast. ## 6. The story director

Decided in the structure; the blueprint does not yet have it.

A small scheduler over a pool of authored beats. It never moves the world: the Core moves the dial, the ladders and Ellis's stage, on deeds. The director only chooses which beat, among those whose conditions are already true, plays next, and when.

Each beat is a card: conditions (world state), a priority, a weight (light, middle, heavy), a cooldown, a shape card of seven fields (opening, turn, ending, action, place, company, mood), who can carry it and a fallback role, and the sign shown before it lands. Lesson five, for example, wakes when Ellis has stopped Ron about Tom and a car stands idle at night; it is middle weight, plays once, and turns comedy into a question on the dock road; if Ron is gone, Sheila asks across the counter; its sign is Ron's street line, "Ellis was at me about you, boss."

- Pacing. After Act I something reaches Tom at least every 30 real minutes. Act II runs in at least four stages of at most 4 real hours, each ending in a rung change; the frame's five are the ranks, the dawn, the Landing, the posts and the fall. At most one heavy beat a game week (the Landing, the search, the yard, the quay), and a rest or a funny beat after each. The weekly rule holds.
- Escalating and cooling. It reads the dial, the ladders and Ellis's stage, and may not move them. When one has risen twice in a game day it offers only rest beats until morning: a lesson, a fare with nothing in it but a person, Hal's Monday, Friday's number nine with Ron. When nothing has risen for a game week it reaches for the highest beat whose sign has been shown.
- No repeats. Neighbouring beats differ on at least three of the seven shape fields; no beat plays twice; a gap is filled by a side story or a street happening before any filler job; and each week changes its ground, its kind of job and its danger.
- The next want. The Ledger always holds one named next thing in a person's own words (who, where, what for) and, beneath it, Tom's aim for the rung in his. No markers. "Eric Tharratt, cold stores. The packing shift met on time, five mornings running. Then he'll talk." Beneath: "Five cars. A night desk. The contract. Then they call me Tom."
- Late, absent, or the carrier is dead. Every main beat has a second way in. The simulation may take away only who is alive, who knows what, who is where, who will talk, and how the law stands.

Three chairs keep the spine standing (the writer's). The opponent's chair is Danny's: if he is dead or gone, Craig Fewson has his ladder at the same rung (RT2-05), his key and his grudge, and is the man on the quay; a killing also gives Ellis her case, and the hunted endings are open. The bond's chair is Ron's: if he is gone before the turn, the most loyal man takes the fall and Sheila has the last word. (PROPOSED, a question for RT2-05: until the turn, a rival's harm to Ron stops at the hospital.) The book's chair is Sheila's: if she has left, the book lies on the counter with the pen on it on the last morning.

Smaller beats bend the same way. The old firm's man can bring the ask to the door (RT2-05), and Sheila asks about that night whenever he next stands at the counter. If car three sits a day, Danny moves it and tells it his way. The Landing's load lands without him, and the holding begins anyway. And if Ellis never reaches investigating, the inspector reads the same line in Act III.

## 7. How memory, gossip and talk carry the story

The rules (decided):

- Said to his face: every deed of weight with a named witness returns as a named person's remark within a game day.
- Told never above seen: tellings lose detail and never gain it, so the town's version of Tom is something he can hear, correct or use.
- Returning people: anybody he hurt, shamed, spared, paid off, poached or let down goes into a pool, and later beats are cast from it.
- Live talk: anybody can be spoken to, and says only what the simulation knows. The spine's facts sit in the mouths that hold them: Ron on the line, Sheila on his name, Danny on Mickey.
- The name ladder is the town's verdict, spoken.

Worked example 1: said to his face, and a telling he can use. Car three is seen at face range by Ada from her window and Shaun Hewson from his ladder. By noon Brenda has it whole: "You're never him that put Cammack's motor on the edge." Lee Drewery has it third-hand and thin: a man from Mickey's did it, he does not know which. "Was it Ron?" The player can leave Ron's name on it, which the pen respects and Danny's men will act on, or claim it; then Lee holds it from Tom's own mouth, and that wins Lee. Danny, meanwhile, tells it his way: "I told the nephew to park it. I'd a flat." Ada saw. If Tom came for his tea, the terrace has it his way.

Worked example 2: one debt, three keys, three returns. Dennis Iveson owes £80 and has it. Front up his yard hand, Neil Screeton (proposed), and in week six Screeton is holding the yard gate shut: "Eighty pound, Mr Nowak. I've your change here." Go into the hut at night, and Norman Dent has a noise and a shape, which is suspicion and never "he did it"; he says so to Tom's face, and in week five it is a description in Ellis's notebook. Lean on what Iveson bought out of the burned warehouse, and Dennis pays on the spot and remembers: when Ellis asks round, he trades her the date of a landing night. The debtor squeezed in week one is the witness in week five.

Worked example 3: a returning enemy with a mark. On the ferry rank in week two the Widow's taxman, Mark Billany (proposed), goes down in front of the boat queue. Tom can hand him his cap, walk off, or put the boot in. Within three game days Billany is at the caff with a stitched eyebrow, saying how he got it. In week four he is driving the van at the Landing. Spared, he lays the keys on the roof and walks: "That's us straight." Kicked, he locks himself in and leans on the horn until the gate men come. A spared Billany can later be turned, and one day the Widow sends for him.

## 8. How the ending is assembled

From three things. The spine's choice: what he wrote at the counter. The empire's state when the books open at five: the built rule (RT2-04, unchanged) reads the save (the business, a friendship (OPEN 4), charges, anybody given to Ellis, a successor), and the close shows what can be seen: the rung, the posts, who is still on the board. And what the town remembers: up to about five lines, spoken by named people who each hold a deed of his at first hand; then what each rival still holds and calls him (RT-05), and the last name he is called, and by whom. Nobody sums up. Signs come before every cost, as the blueprint has them. The three endings are the built ones, each with a better and a plainer turn.

### The Kingdom

The books hold. On Saturday the board is full, Pauline is calling cars to every door on the Hook, and people on the parade step aside. He has exactly what he said on the first night: they say Mickey's and they mean him. The sign still says Mickey's. Ron is where he asked to be left, and their last scene is through a hatch: "Boss." Nothing comes after it. That morning the Harbour Board chains the old dock gate, and Tom's man stands his post in front of a lock.

Voices, by record. Lily Tang, on Friday: "One of number nine tonight? I remember." The Man in Black, putting his hat on: "I don't mark those." And the Widow, from her chair: "Tommy." It is the top of the ladder, and she is the one saying it.

The better turn (the built Both, and rare) needs what the rule already asks: a friend who still counts him, a street that holds none of his rackets as fact, no serious charge, nobody given to Ellis. Then the one who talked takes it back, Ron is out without the book (PROPOSED), and the name is his to say.

### The Straight Life

He writes the line true and carries the true count across town. Ron comes down the station steps at four. At five the inspector closes a firm that has ended itself. In the better turn (the built Quiet) the successor took it over before five, and there is still a cab office on Quay Street with another name going up. In the plainer, the cars pay the assessment and the bay is whitewashed.

Outside the station a minicab with an L-plate waits at the kerb, engine running. Nobody sounds the horn. Tom gets in on the passenger side, arm in its sling.

### Burn Both

Neither the firm nor a friend. He held on and a morning fell; or he named a name and lost both anyway; or he stood still until five. Another firm's cars stand on the rank. Ron, when he is let out, takes the Widow's coat, because a man has to be sent for by somebody. Shaun Hewson has his ladder against the front: "Wants doing. What's it to say?" Nobody tells him.

Voices. Sylvia Rennison, giving him back his own phrase, word for word. The last name comes from a stranger in the doorway with a suitcase, come about the office: "Mr Nowak?" It is spelt right on his letter.

If he is hunted for a killing when the books open, each ending plays from that state: the close is spoken about him, and not to him.
