# The Passenger

A treatment for LEDGER's story. Spoken lines are samples.

## 1. The shape in a paragraph

Tom Nowak inherits a minicab office, and with it an old docker who cannot drive and rides in his passenger seat. The story is the friendship. For three weeks the two of them work the Hook from one car: Ron names every building by what it was, brings the firm his old gang one man at a time, and learns to drive on Sunday mornings at eleven miles an hour. Then the port's labour contractor, the Widow, who never threatens and prices everything, offers Ron the dock gate. On Bonfire Night he is standing at her fire in a foreman's coat, and the seat is empty. The second half is played alone, against a woman who takes a firm apart by asking each of its people what they want, while the player finds out with his hands why Ron went: on the night of last year's warehouse fire one of Mickey's cars was driven to the row and back by a man with no licence and no idea how, and the Widow knows who. Ron did not leave the firm. He sold himself to keep its car out of a detective's file. When a mild inspector from Customs and Excise reads that night's two lines aloud and asks for the driver's name in writing by five on Friday, there are three names that can go in the box: a dead man's, the friend's, and the name of the one man in Meridian whose whereabouts a year ago nobody has ever asked about. The last thing the player does in the game is done from the passenger seat, with a handbrake.

Nobody in this story is a hidden boss. The secret is in the seat beside him from minute seven.

## 2. The hours

Act I, the Inheritance (15 to 21 October): 2 hours. Act II, first half, the Seat (22 October to 4 November): 5 hours. The midpoint, the Fifth: half an hour. Act II, second half, the Empty Seat (6 November to 2 December): 4.5 hours. Act III, the Ledger Comes Due (3 to 15 December): 2.5 hours. Side stories: 2 hours. About 16.5 hours in all, with fifteen played scenes after week one.

## 3. Tom

His want. Mickey's postscript: "Don't let them call you Mickey's nephew for ever." His own name, said by people who mean it.

His flaw. He lets nobody give him anything, and he asks nobody for anything: not a favour, not a reason, not what they want. He pays for tea that was on the house. It looks like pride and it is fear: a man who owes nothing can pick up his suitcase. The offers run all game as one press, take it or pay. Ron sees it by the first Thursday: "You never ask for owt, do you. Mickey was the same."

The load the flaw cannot carry. Ron is the same man. He pays for the supper, tells half a story and takes nothing. Two men who never ask sit eighteen inches apart for three weeks, and one of them is carrying something the other could lift if either would open his mouth. The Widow takes Ron away with the one move neither of them can make: she asks him what he wants. The second half teaches the player to do it, one person at a time, because it is the only defence against her. The end asks whether a man who would take nothing will take the heaviest thing there is, for someone else.

His change. Three choices only he can make: what he says when Ron asks him about the gate; what he does with what he reads in the gate hut; and whose name goes in the box.

His past. Canon's rule is the story's last key. Nobody asks where he was before the gangway, and he never tells. Ada says it for the street on the first Tuesday: "I'll not ask where you were before. Nobody on this street will. We've all been somewhere."

## 4. The people

Nineteen who matter.

- Ron Kirby, 58. A docker for thirty years, on the door and the rank since the scheme ended. He held the old club's seat on the warehouse row. He cannot drive: "Never needed to. The work was at the end of the street." He has a name for every man in his old gang, Tapper, Shiner, the Bishop, and a story for none of them that he will finish.
- Sheila Dunn, 53, the bookkeeper. As written. She has said good morning to Ron every day for a year.
- Maureen Jensen, 50, the Widow, the main opponent. Broad-shouldered, dressed like a harbour master. Since the scheme ended she is the port's labour contractor, registered and lawful: sixty men stand at the dock gate at six and she picks twenty. She never threatens. She prices, she puts each offer once, and she keeps every deal she has ever made. She wants every job at the gate through her hands, Mickey's as the gate's cab firm, and the old club's book shut: "A pension for men who'll never work is a tax on men who do." She is right about more than she is wrong about, and Ron is better at her gate than he ever was on Mickey's door.
- Tapper and Shiner, of Ron's old gang, on the Widow's gate now. Shiner talks.
- The Bishop, 60, of Ron's old gang, who is not on anybody's gate.
- Pauline Kitching, 44, the radio. Nine years without a Saturday off. Terry Hodgson, 52, nights, four words a shift. Kevin Needham, days, twelve years in Mickey's car, who wants a plate of his own.
- Darren Milner, 25, who sells what he hears and wants to be somebody's man.
- Ada, 74, the widow at her window. Exactly what she seems: on the club's list, the best witness on Quay Street. Her tea is tea.
- June Suddaby, 34, Mickey's daughter, night sister at the infirmary.
- Father Brendan Walsh, who buried Mickey and counted who came.
- Danny Cammack, 26. Hook Cars, yellow, £1.50 a ride. Small here: he takes whatever the street leaves undefended, and a firm with nobody on its door is undefended.
- Geoffrey Agar, 61, the landlord. Small here: the hearing, the rent, and one walk in the rain.
- DS Carol Ellis, 44, with a fire file her inspector closed, and in it a docker's brass tally found in the ashes that nobody has ever matched to a man.
- Alison Sedman, 31, the paper's port reporter, the someone worth being honest for. One question: where did an hour go on the row last November?
- Mr T. A. Reese of Customs and Excise.

## 5. Act I: the Inheritance (2 hours)

The approved first week stands. In order, with what this version plants.

1. Monday. The ferry and the letter. Sheila's walk-round and the fare-book rule. On the forecourt, car two: "Mind that wing. It's been done once." The first fare, Father Walsh and the forty-one empty envelopes. The debt book. Darren's £22. Ron, "boss", the coat, the first ask. The carry to the landing, three routes, a constable who can turn round. Cyril's thumb.
2. Tuesday to Thursday. The debts, the fares, Grace's tea (take it or pay), Terry's flask. Ada's question about her alley, and her one invitation. Wednesday's tea: she tells him what the envelope is, Walter's sick club, forty-one names, four pounds, and then, of his doorman: "He's a good man, Ron Kirby. He came home at two one morning last November with no sleeves to his coat, and I never asked. You'll find I don't." Thursday night, two dockers waiting by car two: the first time Ron rides. Afterwards, in the car: "You never ask for owt, do you."
3. Friday. Rent to Agar's agents, wages by hand, and the round, twenty doors with a holdall. Then the Tangs', and the quay wall. Ron buys three of number nine and puts one inside his coat. "Feller I know." Ron pays. Take it or pay.
4. Saturday. Danny Cammack on Quay Street, buying chips, friendly.
5. Sunday. The first lesson. The empty dock road, car two, Ron at the wheel, the player beside him with his voice and the handbrake. It ends on a bollard, and on car two's wing. Kevin, on Monday, looking at it: "That bollard's had a Mickey's wing off it before." Then Sheila on her day off: "So which is it going to be?"

What the player believes at the end of week one: he has a firm, a club that pays old dockers, three rivals who have noticed him, and a friend. The friend is the only one of those he would miss.

## 6. Act II, first half: the Seat (5 hours)

Two weeks. The spine is one car and two men. Ron brings the firm his old gang one at a time; each is a job, and each is a story Ron starts and does not finish. It is the happiest stretch of the game and it is built to be.

Scene 1. Tapper, and the freephone. Danny's number is on the freephone in the ferry terminal. Tapper works the ferry's ropes and knows when the ticket hall is empty. Getting Mickey's number back is a night's work with a screwdriver, a word with the clerk, or a price war. In the car, after: "Tapper could tap a tea chest so it sang the weight. One time him and the Bishop had a crate of..." Rain by four. He does not finish.

Scene 2. The gate at six. The Widow books a car herself and sits in the front. "Save you turning round." Ron has to get in the back, and does, without a word. Sixty men, twenty picked; Shiner picked first. Her first offer, put once: fifty pence on every fare through the gate. She does not look at Ron and says to him, getting out: "You're wasted on a door, Kirby. You always were." In the car, Ron moves back to the front before he speaks. "Shiner. Shiner could talk a customs man out of his own hat. He was talking the night we..." He stops.

Scene 3. The long way round. Kevin's day fares come in short against his mileage; Sheila sees it in the book before anyone says it. Followed, he is carrying parcels for the gate in Mickey's car. Fronted on the rank, in front of the street: "Twelve years I've driven his car. She asked me what I wanted. Nobody here ever did." Sack him, keep him and use him, or ask him the question now. It is the first time the game shows how the Widow works, and it is the first time the player can ask a person of his own what they want. If he asks, Kevin tells him, and it is small, and it can be met.

Scene 4. The hearing, Tuesday 30 October. The licensing sub-committee, referred by a councillor who owes Agar, over the dead man's badge. Only people Tom has let do him a turn have written a reference. Ron is at the back of the room in his funeral suit. "I can't write you one, boss. I can stand here." Licensed, licensed with conditions, or refused; none ends the game.

Scene 5. A wing, twice. Shaun Hewson, the signwriter, repaints car two's wing in the yard. "Second time I've done this one. Same place and all. Mickey said he'd clipped the bollard on the dock road. He was a careful driver, Mickey. I thought it at the time."

Scene 6. The offer. In the third week the Widow puts it to Ron in the open, at the gate, in front of his old gang: foreman, a hundred and forty pounds, sixty men under him. He tells Tom himself, at the wheel of nothing, in the passenger seat, on the quay wall on Friday with two of number nine and one in his coat. "She's offered me the gate, boss. What do you think?" The player answers in his own words, once: go, and he goes as a friend; stay, and Ron says "Right, boss"; or you owe it to Mickey, and he says nothing at all.

Scene 7. The second lesson. Sunday 4 November. L-plates. Pauline tells every cab to keep off the dock road. Ron drives its whole length at eleven miles an hour, talking the whole way, and asks to go round again. At the far end the warehouse row opens ahead of them. He stops short of it. "Not down there, boss. Surface is bad." It is not.

A watchful player has by now a wing done twice, a coat with no sleeves, a third portion and a road Ron will not drive down. Nothing stops him following the third portion on a Friday, or putting any of it to Ron. If he does, before the Fifth, Ron tells him, and the midpoint is a different scene.

## 7. The midpoint: the Fifth

Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990. The quay is full of the town's grown people for the fireworks off the old coal jetty, and at the dock gate the dockers have their own fire, as they have had every year: pallets, dunnage, a blaze the size of a van. It is the busiest night of the cab year. Ron has asked for the night off. He has never asked for anything.

The fare. Late on, a fare to the gate. The Widow is standing on a pallet with a clipboard, reading the winter's hire list by the light of the fire. Beside her, in a foreman's donkey jacket with the gate's name across the back, straight-backed, is Ron. His old gang is round him. Whatever Tom said on the quay wall, he has taken it. If Tom said go, it is how he has gone: without a word, on the one night he knew Tom would be too busy to see. If Tom said stay, it is a betrayal. If Tom said he owed it to Mickey, the docker's hook he wore in his belt for thirty years is on the office counter in the morning.

The fire. On top of the dockers' bonfire, where the guy goes, somebody has sat a figure in a car coat with a cab's roof sign round its neck. The sign says MICKEY'S. It is Danny's lads' joke, and the gate laughs. Ron does not laugh. He does not take it down either. He watches it burn, with his hands in the pockets of her coat. It is the thing burning that should not be, and half the Hook sees who stood by.

The radio. Driving back along the dock road, Pauline, to every cab at once, kindly, which is worse: "Car one. You on your own?"

The window. With nobody on the door, Danny's lads have put the office window in while the street watched fireworks. Ada is on her step in her coat. "You'd better come in, Tom." It is the first time anyone on the street has used his name. Tea. She tells him who did the window, and then the thing she has been turning over: "He'll not have gone for money. I've known Ron Kirby forty years. He'd not cross the road for money."

What is lost: the seat. For the next four hours of play nobody names the buildings.

What the player believes: his friend has been bought, or was let go. Ada's sentence is the first crack in it.

If the player found it out first. He has put the wing and the sleeves to Ron before the Fifth, or followed the third portion. Then the Fifth is planned by two men on a quay wall: Ron goes to the gate anyway, because it is the only coin that buys her silence, but he goes as Tom's man, and the foreman's coat is a thing they chose. The effigy still burns, and Ron still has to stand and watch it. The second half is then two men working one woman from both sides of a gate.

## 8. Act II, second half: the Empty Seat (4.5 hours)

Four weeks. The spine is the Widow taking the firm apart by offers while the player finds out, with his hands, what she bought.

Scene 8. Who sits in front. The week after. Fares alone. Other people start to take the front seat. Darren: "Can I sit up front? Only it's empty." Let him, or not. Darren wants the door Ron left. The Widow's offers go round the firm, each put once: Kevin gets his plate; Julie Kettlewell is offered a van; and Pauline is asked what she wants. The defence is the thing Tom never does. Each of his people has one want he can learn by asking and meet with his hands: Pauline has not had a Saturday off in nine years, and if he takes the radio for a day the drivers test him at once and Sheila corrects every call without looking up. Terry, asked, uses a fifth word: "Tell Pauline it don't count." What the player does not ask, the Widow does.

Scene 9. Car two. The back seat has to come out for the wing to be done properly. Under it, where a thing would roll: a cotton glove, burnt through at the palm, and a docker's brass tally, the kind every registered man carried, stamped with a number. Sheila looks at it on the counter and goes on writing. In the 1989 fare book, on the shelf behind her chair since the walk-round, in her small upright hand, two lines for Saturday 18 November:

"22.50. Car one. Office to Infirmary. One. No charge."

"23.20. Car two. Landing to warehouse row and return. Wait. No charge."

The night of the fire. There is a driver's sheet for car one that night, in Terry's hand. There is none for car two. Terry, asked, uses seven words: "I took Mickey up. Not the row." Mickey Suddaby was on a ward with his heart at eleven o'clock on the night his cab went to the fire, and his daughter was the sister on it. The dead uncle did not do it. The book says so.

Scene 10. The third portion. Friday. Ron still comes to the Tangs' for three of number nine, in the gate's coat, and does not look at the rank. Followed, he walks to a terrace on Tannery Row. Through the window, a man of sixty in a chair, hands in grey cotton gloves, breathing like a man who has to think about it. If Tom knocks after Ron has gone, the Bishop looks at the tally in his hand for a long time. He says one thing: "He carried me out on his back. Then he drove that car like a man killing it." Tapper, Shiner, the Bishop and Ron went into the importer's warehouse that night for a consignment of cigarettes, the last thing on the quay worth lifting, to carry the club through a winter. Mickey was to drive. Mickey was on a ward. A lamp went over on the cotton bales.

Scene 11. Remembrance Sunday, 11 November. The dockside memorial. Father Walsh reads the names: the wars, and the men the docks have taken since; this year, Michael Suddaby. Everybody is in one place for the only time. Ron is across the square with the gate's wreath. Two minutes of silence in which the player chooses only where Tom looks, and whether Ron looks back depends on what was said on the quay wall.

Scene 12. The gate hut. The Widow keeps a book, because she prices everything and keeps every deal. It is in the hut at the dock gate, and getting to it is a night's work with three keys: Tapper's hour on the night gate, a way over the cold-store roof, or the door with whoever will still stand with him. Inside, a page for every person she has bought, in a clear round hand. Kevin: a plate. Pauline: a line, and beside it a blank, or "Saturdays. Nowak met it." A page headed with Tom's own name and what she thinks he is worth. And: "Kirby, R. Foreman. £140. Consideration: his silence and mine, 18.11.89, car two."

The player reads it himself, by torch, in her hut. Ron did not leave. He was the price. Shiner talked; she knows who drove a Mickey's cab to the fire; and she has done with it what she does with everything. Ron went to her gate so that the line in Sheila's book would never have a name beside it and the firm's car would stay out of Ellis's file. He bought it with the only thing he had, and he could not say so, because the man it was for never asks.

Scene 13. The third lesson. The player goes to the gate at six. Ron is picking twenty men, and he is good at it, and they like him. Tom holds out the keys. Ron drives. He drives properly, which is the thing to notice, out along the dock road and, this time, down the warehouse row to the burnt plot, and puts the handbrake on. Then he finishes a story for the first time in the game. It is short. And then: "I can drive, boss. Near enough. I did it once, eleven mile round the houses with a man burning in the back, and I swore I'd not sit that side again. Then you were that pleased when I got the length of the dock. I couldn't tell you." Every laugh the lessons had in them is still there, and is now something else.

What the player can do with it: leave Ron where he is, safe and bought; ask him back, and the Widow's silence ends the day he comes; or go and see her.

Scene 14. The Widow in the passenger seat. She books car one and sits in front, in his seat. Her last offer, put once. Mickey's becomes the gate's firm; Tom keeps the name on the roof and a wage; the old club's book is shut, the eleven who can work get work, and she will see the thirty who cannot onto what the state allows. "I've a thing of yours in my hut worth more than both your cars. I'd sooner you had it back as part of a sale than that I went on minding it." She is not threatening. If he says he has read her book, she is not angry: "Then you know I keep my deals. Ask Ron if I've ever asked him for a thing since." She has not. Say yes, say no, or say what he wants from her. Refused, she does what she always does with a thing that will not sell: she finds another buyer for it. She telephones Customs and Excise about a fare book with a car out and no sheet.

Scene 15. The door. With no Ron, the street is Danny's to take: yellow cars along the kerb, his lads on the rank. Tom holds his own door with his own hands, in front of the Tangs' queue, and Darren stands beside him or sells the news of it.

Scene 16. The anniversary, and the rain. Sunday 18 November. Alison's piece is on every counter: "An hour on the row." Ellis walks the row again with the watchman, and comes to the office to look at car two's wing. She asks whose it was to drive. That night Geoffrey Agar walks down Quay Street for the first time in his life, without a hat. He has come to offer for the yard, and to say one thing more: "I am told your man Kirby may need a solicitor before the year is out. I keep three. It is a gift, Mr Nowak. I do not expect you to believe that." Take it or pay. It is the heaviest press in the game, and nothing marks which it is.

The act ends on a letter: courteous, dated, signed T. A. Reese.

## 9. Act III: the Ledger Comes Due (2.5 hours)

Why he comes: a registered trader has died and the name on the form has changed; and a lady has rung.

Monday 10 December. Reese at the counter. "A fare at no charge is either a kindness or a hole, Mr Nowak. I'm paid to ask which."

Tuesday. The ride-along. Reese gets into the front passenger seat without asking. "I always ride in front. My father drove a cab." He sits where Ron sat, with his flask, and Tom drives. Brenda and the crabs: "Are those declared, Mrs Laverack?" "They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in."

Wednesday. The old books. He goes quiet twice and makes two small marks.

Thursday. He reads both lines aloud. "Ten to eleven, car one, office to infirmary, one, no charge. A kindness. I see the name against it. And half an hour on, car two, landing to warehouse row and return, wait, no charge. There is a sheet for car one. There is none for car two, and the only men licensed to drive it were in the other car and in a hospital bed. Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak. A car out with no driver is a hole. I shall want the driver's name in that box by five tomorrow." The form has one box.

Thursday night. Sheila's kitchen. He has never been to her house. The book is on the table with the slip in it. "I wrote what the car did. I never wrote who. I've known a year, and so has he, and we've said good morning every day of it. Every fare goes in the book. I'd not write a name I didn't see." There is a pen and a fire in the grate. He can leave both lines. He can strike the first, the true one, Mickey's alibi, so that a dead man drove; June nursed her father that night and will know what was struck. He can put the book on the fire, after which the books cannot hold. On the pavement outside, in the gate's coat, is Ron. Kevin talks. "I'll go in tomorrow and say it, boss. Don't tell me not to."

Friday. The phone box on the corner, five coins, the afternoon. He can ring only people he once let give him something, and each call that is answered is an errand: the Bishop, who will come in a chair and say whose lamp it was; June, who can swear where her father was; Agar's solicitor, if the gift was taken; Ada; Father Walsh. Each changes what a name in the box will cost the man it belongs to. Then five o'clock, the counter, Reese's pencil and one box. Three names can go in it.

- "M. Suddaby." It needs the first line gone, and June's silence. The dead take it. It is the thing the letter asked him not to be.
- "R. Kirby." Ron says it himself, at the counter, in his own coat. Reese: "Were you paid, Mr Kirby?" "No." "Was anybody?" "No." "Then I have no fare to tax. I have a car driven without a licence, which I am glad to say is not a matter for Customs and Excise. I am obliged to note it." The note goes along the corridor to Ellis, and what it costs Ron is what Friday's errands made it.
- "T. Nowak." Nobody in Meridian can say where Tom Nowak was on the eighteenth of November 1989, because nobody has ever asked and he has never told. The one man in the Hook with no alibi for the fire is the man with no past. Reese looks at him for some time. "You came to this town in October, Mr Nowak." The player answers, or does not. "I am paid to ask. I am not paid to doubt a man against himself." He writes it. If Ron is in the room he says, "He was never here," and Reese, mildly: "I can only put one in the box, gentlemen." The last word is the player's.

Then Reese closes his notebook. "That all seems to be in order." Or, if the firm's own books for this year do not hold: "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak."

Saturday 15 December. The last scene is driven, and Tom does not drive it. Ron comes to the rank and holds out his hand for the keys. Tom gets in on the passenger side, where the lessons put him: his voice, and the handbrake. Ron drives. Where he is going depends on the name in the box: up the hill to the police station, to say the rest himself; to the ferry ramp, with a bag on the back seat, before Ellis comes; to the dock gate at six, in a foreman's coat; or just round. The Widow is on the pavement at the gate as they pass, hands in her pockets, and she nods, once, to whichever of them kept a deal. At the end of the drive the player has the only input the passenger has ever had. He can pull the handbrake, or leave it off.

## 10. The central turn, and why it is fair

The turn is in two parts, and both are found, not told. In the gate hut the player reads that the friend who walked out on him was paying for him. On the warehouse row the friend says that the man the player taught to drive could already, near enough, and why he swore not to. What it changes: every lesson; the bollard; "Mind that wing"; three of number nine; "Not down there, boss"; Ron climbing into the back seat for the Widow without a word; the effigy he watched burn; "You never ask for owt"; and Ada's "I never asked".

The last key is canon's oldest rule. Tom's past is unsaid, and the game has kept that rule so strictly that the player has stopped noticing it. On the Friday it turns out to be the one thing he owns that nobody else in the Hook does: a year with nothing written in it.

Why it surprises. A reader watching for the hidden boss, the guilty dead uncle and the crooked landlord finds a widow who is a widow, an uncle with an alibi in his own fare book and a landlord offering a lawyer. The opponent hides nothing and breaks no law. The secret is in the comic relief.

Why it is fair. The wing is named in the first ten minutes. The sleeves are in Ada's tea. The third portion is bought in front of him every Friday. Ron will not drive onto the row. Shaun says it was done twice and that Mickey was a careful driver. Kevin says how the Widow works three weeks before she works it on Ron. The lines are on a shelf in plain sight. And everyone, all game, has not asked.

## 11. The endings

States, never a menu. Each has a last scene, a line Sheila writes in the fare book and a line Tom writes in his Ledger. In each, the verdict is which seat Ron is in.

The Kingdom. The business stands and the books hold, because a dead man's name is in the box or because Tom did to the Widow what she did to Ron: he holds her book now, and Mickey's is the gate's firm or the gate is Mickey's. Last scene: six in the morning, the dock gate, sixty men. A cab comes down the dock road and stops. Ron gets out of the passenger side in a foreman's coat with NOWAK'S across the back, and picks twenty, and does not look at the car. He is in that seat now because he is held there. Above the gate hut, a light. If the true line was struck, Sheila's keys are on the counter on top of the fare book. If not, she says "Goodnight, Mr Nowak". On the better turn (he asked his people what they wanted and met it; one friend counts him; nobody was sold) she stays, and Ron, at the gate, looks across once. Fare book: "Dock gate. Account. No charge." Ledger, in a hand that has started to look like Mickey's: "20 picked. T.N."

The Straight Life. No kingdom, and the friendship holds. The better turn has two faces. If Ron's name went in the box with Tom beside him: Ron has been up the hill and is bailed; the Bishop has said whose lamp it was; a solicitor nobody is ashamed of has it in hand; Julie Kettlewell has the firm and Terry the night desk. On the last evening Tom gets into the back of car one. Julie drives. Ron is in the front passenger seat, by habit. "Where to?" "Just round." At the office Sheila writes the fare herself. "Mind how you go, Tom." Fare book: "Nowak, T. Round the Hook. £2. Paid." If Tom's own name went in the box: it is Tom who has been up the hill. He comes down the station steps on bail, and car two is at the kerb with L-plates on and Ron at the wheel, on the right side, and Ron looks across. He drives him home at eleven miles an hour. Sheila has found out for herself whose name it really was; it is the thing she says "Tom" for. Fare book, her hand: "Police station to office. One. No charge." In both, the Ledger: "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four." The plain turn: he sold to the Widow, kindly; somebody passing says "Morning, Tom." Fare book: "Closed." Ledger: "Stayed."

Burn Both. The Reckoning, for the man whose own books did not hold whatever name he gave: "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak." Sheila hands her keys to a constable half her age. Saturday night is the first night again, the quay, the lamp, and the constable does not call "You there." He calls, "Nowak!" Stand or run; the close cuts on the press. Fare book: closed unwritten. Ledger: the last page cut out. The Suitcase, for the man who never asked anybody anything: a grey afternoon, the gangway he came up, and the gate's van passing with Ron in the passenger seat, not looking across. Fare book: "Ferry landing. One. No charge." Ledger: a date, and nothing under it.
