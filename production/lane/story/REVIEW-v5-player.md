# LEDGER story bible v5: a player's review

## 1. Scores

**Overall: 7**

1. **Tom: 7.** The want is clear from the postscript, and "lets nobody give him anything" is a flaw I would actually play, press by press. But most of his change shows up in the closing lines of each ending, and in the middle he is mostly the man who chooses, not a man I get to know.
2. **The cast: 9.** Sheila, Ron, Pauline, Terry and Reese are people I would talk about afterwards. Even the small ones, like Hal and four minutes of his own feet, have a shape.
3. **The antagonists: 7.** They really do come at him in different ways: Agar by letters with dates, the Widow by offers, Danny by fares. But Agar is the 1990 marina developer I have seen before, and the Widow is a ladder of offers until the two widows scene finally makes her a person.
4. **The central mystery: 7.** The truth does change earlier scenes: "That's the last one, Cyril", "new management", the "No charge" line. But it has too many hands in it, and you find it by reading documents more than by facing people.
5. **The opening and its retold scene: 8.** The priest's "forty-one envelopes on the plate, and every one of them empty" is a superb first fare, and the carry at minute nine has real stakes. A failed carry that puts "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book is the kind of thing I would tell a friend about.
6. **Structure and pace: 6.** The calendar's days pull you forward, and the midpoint lands. The last act crams a day item, a night job and a revelation into every one of five days, which is the hurried GTA V finish all over again.
7. **The middle: 7.** Scenes 1 to 20 are mostly strong and they rotate their threats. But the weekly loop between them (fares, rungs, Friday) is sketched in one paragraph, and the rungs on both ladders are mostly a line each.
8. **Surprise against cliché: 7.** "He did to Ada what Ada did to Tom" is a real turn. Around it sit familiar shapes: the sweet old widow who runs it all, the obvious suspect who is innocent, the kindly dead uncle who planned it.
9. **The endings: 8.** Each one has a last image I can picture: Ron's seat, "Nowak's?", "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four", "Nowak!" under the lamp. Every one is earned by state, not by a menu.
10. **The side stories: 8.** Ivy and the sergeant, each protecting the other from something both know, is the best thing in the section. Philip's is the thinnest.
11. **Tone and humour: 8.** Pauline on the radio is a real running joke, and Terry's fifth word on a hospital bed ("Tell Pauline it don't count") is funny and heartbreaking at once. The crabs in Reese's cab are a proper set piece; Brenda's postcard lines are the weak spot.
12. **Story and play feeding each other: 8.** Take or pay coming back at the hearing and in the phone box is the best idea in the bible, and nearly every beat is a drive, a carry or a break-in. The climax, though, is a list of errands on a clock, and that is where play and story feed each other least.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The mystery has too many hands, and its last truth arrives as reading.**
What is wrong: by Act III I am holding Ada, Cyril, Mickey, Agar, Garbutt, the brothers Coultas, Ron, Sheila, the importer, the far bank's figures, the Monday runs and the Saturday lifts. The final turn (section 8, Thursday night) is Tom alone in a car boot, turning pages. Then on Friday morning Sheila explains the rest in a speech.
Why a player feels it: I poke at every mystery, and even reading carefully I had to go back to work out who supplied which men to whom. Over fifteen hours of scenes that fire on states, a player will lose the thread, and the moment it all comes together has nobody else in the frame.
Fix: shrink the Garbutt and Coultas line to one man. Les Coultas is the stranger at the counter and the only fire man you meet; Garbutt pays him and stays a fixer who "does not name his clients", and you drop the brother. Then put a person in the boot scene. Have Sheila come down the street under the landlady's window while the book is open in the torchlight, and play her confession there, at the boot, with the pen in his hand and her watching what he does with her line.

**2. Act III is overloaded, and the climax is errands.**
What is wrong: section 8 gives five days, each with a Reese item by day and a job by night: the gate hut, the yard and Agar in the rain, Alison's proof, Ada's carry, Ellis at the plot, the boot, Sheila. Then the Friday is a phone box, five coins and fetch-and-carry against five o'clock, with hooks called in and a rival in the way. That is three to four hours.
Why a player feels it: this is the hurried last act the bar names. The best scene in the week (Agar in the rain, "I found I could not afford to mean it") gets one night among five, and the finale is played as a delivery timer.
Fix: move the missing-sheets break-in to the end of Act II, as the Widow's last move after Garbutt's table. Fold Wednesday's carry to Ada into scene 18, so the real book moves once, not twice. Give the freed time to Friday. Keep the phone box, but cap it at two or three errands, and end the afternoon on one face-to-face scene with the rival who rang Customs, in the place he is blocking, before Tom walks back into the office with whatever he has.

**3. The middle's loop is thinner than its scenes.**
What is wrong: section 7 describes a week in one paragraph ("every third fare is somebody from the Friday round"). The keeper's rungs (the launderette, the cold store, a man at the landing) and the straight rungs (trustees, money in a bank) mostly have no scene of their own beyond 3, 9 and 14. Six weeks of Fridays run on the same rhythm.
Why a player feels it: the bar says the reviewers who complain of the loop had played forty-two hours; here, hours eight to fourteen are when I would start to notice that every Friday is the same holdall. The scenes are strong; what I would drift through is the stretch between them.
Fix: give each rung one named person and one turn, the way Lily Tang's politeness is the whole of scene 9. The cold store as its foreman's story; the landing man as Darren's audition. And change one door on the round every Friday: a new name, a death, a refusal, a door that will not open.

**4. The big turns lean on shapes I have seen before, and the hints give Ada away early.**
What is wrong: the widow at the window who secretly runs the club; the rival everyone blames who is innocent ("He is telling the truth"); the developer with a marina; the good dead uncle who planned it all. In week one alone, Ada gets Ron's "Walter Jensen's widow", the "Does he." on her step, Alison lodging over her, and an invitation that will turn out to be an interview.
Why a player feels it: when Ada asks "Does he." about the tallyman's order, a GTA and KCD player marks her. By the midpoint the window is a confirmation, not a shock. And once a yellow car is "seen pulling away", experienced players assume Danny did not do it. This risks the midpoint guessed on the first Friday.
Fix: cut "Does he." and let Ada take her envelope on the round like everyone else, without comment. Keep the lodger detail but hold it until scene 4. Give Danny something he really is guilty of that same week, say his lads really did rough up Darren in the crowd, so his "That wasn't me" is half a lie and you cannot read him. Give Agar one human scene before the rain (the empty marina office, a family name on a wall) so he stops being the developer type.

**5. Terry, and the cost of the Fifth, fade out.**
What is wrong: the Fifth's wound is a man's hands. After scene 11 Terry has no scene. At Ada's table (section 9) she says "He found me a reason" and "she has no more to say about Terry than that, and it is enough". The endings list "who still has work (Terry)" as a state, not a moment.
Why a player feels it: Terry's flask on the second night is the first kindness I took, and his hospital line is the one I would quote to a friend. If he gets no last word, the game has spent its most human cost and kept the change.
Fix: give Terry one beat in the last week. Make him the voice that answers the phone box (he cannot drive, but he can sit at Pauline's desk), or the man at Ada's table when Tom tells her what Mickey did. Then let the player choose whether to tell him who lit the car. Put him in each ending's last image in a seat of his own: beside Pauline at the radio, or on the wall with two of number nine.

## 3. Would I single this story out

No, not yet, but it is close. If a friend asked, I would tell them about Ron learning to drive at eleven miles an hour and asking to go round again, about the night car two burns and the tallyman standing in Ada's front room with his cap in his hand, about a Customs man with a bad knee and a box of crabs on his back seat, and about a bookkeeper who waited a year for somebody to read a fare book. Those are scenes I would retell. Ron and Tom could stand beside Henry and Hans.

What stops me telling them to play it for the story is the back third. The mystery is cleverer than it is clear. Its last truth is read alone from a page and then explained in a speech, and the finale is a timed round of errands. A reviewer would praise the people and the first half, then say the plot "gets tangled" and the end "rushes". Fix those two things and this is a yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. **Ron and Tom, written for the hands.** The passenger seat, the map as a list of things that shut, the three lessons (the third not funny), two of number nine on the quay wall, the Widow's offer of the gate, and the verdict told by which seat Ron is in as the car passes. This is the relationship people would remember.
2. **The fare book as the only book that never lied.** Every fare goes in the book, from Sheila's walk-round to the line she wrote in her own hand when Mickey would not, the "No charge" that comes back as the Suitcase's last line, and Reese, the first person who only wants to know what happened. And with it the turn that Mickey found Ada a reason the way she found Tom one.
3. **Take or pay as both story and play.** Gifts and hooks that look the same, the hearing where only the people he let help him can stand up, the silent full bench for the man who paid for everything, and the phone box where only those people answer. It is the theme built as a system.
