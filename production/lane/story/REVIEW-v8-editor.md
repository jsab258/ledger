# LEDGER, story bible v8: a script editor's note

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7.

1. Tom: 7. The flaw (he will take nothing) is the best-built thing about him, because it is played rather than stated, and the Ledger gives an unvoiced man a dry, likeable voice. He reacts more than he wants, though; his want is a postscript, and much of his change is done to him by other people's reveals.
2. The cast: 8. Sheila, Ron, Terry, Pauline, June, Ellis and Reese are distinct people with one clear want each, and several of them pay off on a single line ("Tell Pauline it don't count"). It is crowded at the edges.
3. The antagonists: 7. Agar's glass-door scene and his walk down Quay Street, Maureen's priced offers and Danny who claims a fire he did not light are each good and each come at Tom a different way. Garbutt is a costume, the final face-offs on the Friday are not written, and Ada's plan has a hole at its centre (point 3).
4. The central mystery: 6. Sheila writing the line Mickey would not is a real surprise that recasts the first walk-round. Under it sits the most familiar plot in British regeneration crime, a developer paying for an arson, shown at scene 4 and confessed before the climax, with money that does not add up.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The priest's forty-one empty envelopes is a first-hour hook a reviewer would quote, and a failed carry that ends with "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book is the right kind of retold failure.
6. Structure and pace: 6. The calendar spine is sound. Act II is overfull, its dig is pinned to fixed dates that fight the "states, not sequence" rule, and Act III has an unaccounted week.
7. The middle: 7. The Fifth, the lock-up, Hal's tape, Remembrance and the two widows in the cab are a strong run. The warehouse thread sits still for most of it, because Ada confessed to the fire on the first Sunday and the dig mostly confirms her.
8. Surprise against cliché: 6. Danny's innocence, Ada's car, the importer's rent card and Sheila's line are fresh. Against them: the developer, the incorruptible detective with a personal file, the reporter whose editor sits on the Board, and a mob sit-down.
9. The endings: 7. The last images are earned and specific ("Nowak!", "42", "Stayed.", the blank page), and Ada's table is a genuine last choice. Five states in builder's shorthand ("the built Both") blur from the player's side.
10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and her daughter, each protecting the other from what both know, is the best thing outside the main line. The watch's turn is guessable, and Lilies is a version of the main round rather than its own story.
11. Tone and humour: 8. Pauline's radio, Terry's word tally, Ron's lessons, the crabs and the ham with a seat are funny because they come from people and situations.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 7. The offers, the hearing, the phone box with five coins and the fare book under a pencil are story made of systems. The lock-up board and Garbutt's paper trade are features used once, and the fixed-date dig scenes are rails.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The climax's truth is spent before the climax, and the truth is the familiar one.
What is wrong: the answer is a landowner paying to clear a plot. The player suspects Agar from scene 4; at scene 17 the importer and Ron establish the man paid; at scene 21 Agar says, in full, "I paid your uncle four thousand pounds to see that a plot came free." Sheila's kitchen then repeats it, and the Friday page repeats it a third time ("Had of G.A. £4,000").
Where: section 7, scenes 4, 17 and 21; section 8, Thursday night and Friday.
Why it matters: the only new fact in Sheila's kitchen is whose hand wrote the line. It is a good fact, but the player walks in knowing the sum, the payer and the lie.
Fix: in the rain Agar admits only what a careful man would: he bought a plot cheaply from a man who wished to sell. The £4,000 is first hinted in the 1989 fare book ("Office to Harbour Board. Wait forty minutes") and named only on the Friday page. Agar's confession becomes something Tom can force out of him on the Friday pavement, if Agar telephoned. The climax gets a true reveal and that face-off a scene.

2. The money and the fare book do not hold, and the climax rests on both.
What is wrong: forty-one at £4 is £164 a week, about £8,500 a year. £4,000, less what the Coultas brothers were paid and paid again, cannot have "filled the forty-one envelopes for the year since". Second, "Importer a/c. Paid" is entered every Monday in the fare book, in Mickey's hand. A fare book records fares. If this is protection money, why did a careful liar write the truth into the one book a VAT officer reads? If it is a genuine cab account, it does not prove the importer paid his rent. The rent card stamped PAID raises the same question: who stamps a card for protection money?
Where: section 0, item 5; section 7, scene 17; section 8, Thursday night; section 10, item 5.
Why it matters: the game's idea is that the plainest book never lied. A player who has carried £164 a week for two months will do the sum, and a VAT inspection only lands if he understands why that book is dangerous.
Fix: say outright that the row's rents were always entered as account fares and declared as takings. That wash is what puts Mickey's over the VAT line and why Reese reads the accounts: alibi and noose for one stated reason. Make the £4,000 a top-up closing a stated gap ("short £70 a week since the gate"), or raise it to £9,000. Make the importer's proof a receipt book from the club's watch, not a stamped rent card.

3. Ada's plan has no reason to need Tom.
What is wrong: Ada has Darren burn car two so that Tom will burn Danny's lock-up. But Darren will set a fire for the landing and already holds the lock-up's key; she could have had him burn it directly. "Everybody needs a reason" explains why Tom would act, not why Ada needs him to.
Where: section 0, item 1; section 7, the Fifth (beat 4) and scene 13.
Why it matters: she is billed as the most dangerous person in the story, and her one big move falls apart when a player asks "why him?" The ending recasts her as Mickey's dupe, so with a weak plan she ends sad rather than frightening.
Fix: give her the motive the warehouse sets up. A keeper who has burned something can never shut the book. One line on the Fifth, heard again in scene 13: "Mickey never left because he couldn't. I want you the same." Her plan becomes binding Tom, and the lock-up is a gift with a hook in it.

4. The dig is stuck for most of the middle, and it runs on fixed dates.
What is wrong: Ada admits on the first Sunday that the club burned the warehouse. Scenes 4, 8, 10 and 11 then confirm that (the club hired a man, the club moved the watchman, the book says "Importer refused"). The only crack before scene 17 is Agar's interest in the land, which fits Ada's story anyway. Meanwhile scene 10 is fixed on Saturday 27 October and needs an "earlier night" walking Norman's round, scene 4 floats on "a Sunday", and scene 11 is fixed on Thursday 1 November whether or not Sheila's trust has been earned. This contradicts "scenes fire on states, never on sequence".
Where: section 7, first half; section 15.
Why it matters: for ten hours the warehouse question is "how exactly did the thing we were told happen?", which is the definition of thin.
Fix: give the dig a turn at the midpoint. In the week of the ward, Alison gets the importer's insurer's file: the claim was paid because he had receipts up to the week of the fire. The player carries "he paid, so why the lesson?" through Remembrance, and the anniversary becomes the face, not the first doubt. Take the dates off scenes 4, 10 and 11 and fire them on states.

5. Garbutt's table is a borrowed scene, and the Friday face-off is not written.
What is wrong: three rivals at one back-room table, brought by a fixer we have barely met, is the American gangster sit-down. A Harbour Board landowner would not sit down with a lad who sells stolen radios. Section 0 puts it in Garbutt's shop, section 7 in Hal's; Garbutt's "free read" is never explained. The rival in the Friday run is "settled there, with what Tom knows or with his hands or by walking round", which is a placeholder, not a scene.
Where: section 5; section 7, scene 20; section 8, the Friday.
Why it matters: this is the turn into the endgame, and players remember the last face-off; this one has no words.
Fix: make it a driving job. Garbutt books car one and has Tom drive him between three cars parked on the dock road, Agar's, the Widow's and Danny's, carrying the pages and free to read, keep or swap them on the way. British, hands-first, and nobody shares a room. Then write the three Friday face-offs as scenes: Agar and the padlock (point 1's forced confession), the Widow with Kevin at the wheel (whether Kevin gets out), Danny across Quay Street (what the tape and the page have done to him).

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Ron carries Frank Cammack's packet to his door every Friday in plain sight, yet Danny never noticed in six years. Fix: Mickey's own goes through Edith at the pension counter or Father Walsh.
2. Who claims car two in the Tangs' queue, Danny (section 5) or one of his lads (the Fifth)? And the office window "a week ago" was never played. Fix: Danny says it himself; play the window as a short scene in scene 7's week.
3. The firm has two cars, yet Darren sleeps in "the spare car", and car three appears on roads that never won it. Fix: Darren sleeps in car two (which also explains why Terry's nap there was ordinary), and car three exists only on the K road.
4. A pre-probate deposit would sit with solicitors, not be carried as cash. Fix: the buyer pays cash for a private option.
5. A friendly society cannot be registered and paying out in six weeks. Fix: it starts as an unregistered benefit club with a bank account, registration applied for.
6. In 1990 private hire licensing had no third-party objection hearing of that kind. Fix: a councillor who owes Agar refers the application to committee over the badge.
7. Ellis gets a search warrant on the strength of a newspaper piece. Fix: her grounds are Kevin's copied sheets, passed to her by the gate.
8. A police seizure does not stop Customs reading a book. Fix: Reese narrows his visit to 1990 by his own choice.
9. The importer just happens to be at the plot on the anniversary. Fix: Alison has traced him and asked him over.
10. Who put the new paraffin in the lock-up? Fix: Darren, on Ada's word, which is the set-up for "whose idea fire always is".
11. Act III runs from 3 to 15 December, but only Reese's week (10 to 14) is written. Fix: the rival's nights run in the week of 3 December, and Reese's week is the days.

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review

No. It is close, and parts of it I would quote: Ron in the passenger seat, Sheila's kitchen, the phone box, "Tell Pauline it don't count". But a reviewer singles out a story when its centre surprises, and this centre is a developer's arson, suspected at scene 4, confessed in Act II and repeated twice in the climax, on sums that do not add. The people are better than the plot. Fix the first two points above and this becomes a story I would name as a reason to play.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. Ron and Tom: the lessons, the passenger seat, the Bishop story that is really about Alan Jensen, the year of silence, and the verdict given by which seat he sits in at the end. This is the game's Henry and Hans, built for the hands.
2. Sheila and the fare book: the withheld name, the old books at her house, and the line in her small upright hand, left a year for a reader. "Nobody reads a fare book." It recasts her from the first scene.
3. The offers: Tom's flaw as one press, with gifts and hooks that look alike and do not sort by friend and enemy, paid off at the hearing and in the phone box. Story and play are one thing here.
