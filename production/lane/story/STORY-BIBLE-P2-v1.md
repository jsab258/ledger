# LEDGER: the story bible, phase 2, version 1

The Passenger. 20 November 2026. Canon outranks this file; nothing here is built; canon.md and RULINGS.md are untouched. Spoken lines and Ledger lines are samples of the voice, not a script.

How to read it. Section 0 is a list for the owner and gives away every turn; a reader who wants to meet the story as a player would should read it last. Sections 1 to 5 are the story and its people as first met. Sections 6 to 9 are the story as scenes, in the order played. Section 10 is what was true all along. Sections 11 to 16 are the wind-down player's story, the side stories, the retold scene, the tone, the pay-offs and the rules of play with the budget.

## 0. Changes to canon or rulings, for his ruling

Each with its reason and the plain alternative.

1. The warehouse fire. The ruling of 30 September stands as the street's version (the owner burned his own warehouse and went south) and "the truth is never the street's". The truth here is new: on Saturday 18 November 1989 four old dockers went into the importer's warehouse to lift a consignment of cigarettes for the dockers' club, a lamp went over, and one of Mickey's cars was driven there and back by Ron Kirby, who has no licence. Nobody paid for a fire and nobody meant one. Reason: every hidden hand tried in phase 1 was guessed. Alternative: the fire as phase 1 had it.
2. The outfit. Canon: Mickey left "a half-dead criminal outfit". Here it is an old dockers' sick club that pays forty-one families £4 a week, fed for thirty years by what Ron's gang lifted at the dock gate and moved in Mickey's cabs, and since the dock scheme ended in July 1989 by a small weekly payment from the warehouse row and by Mickey's own pocket. Nobody is its secret head. Alternative: a fuller outfit with a boss.
3. Ada stays what canon says: a widow at her window. Minted: she is the widow of Walter Harker, who started the club in 1953, and she is on its list. She is nobody's boss. This undoes phase 1's first proposed change.
4. Ron's old gang is minted: Tapper, Shiner and the Bishop. The Bishop was burned in the fire and Mickey paid his keep every Friday until he died.
5. One opponent and two pressures. Maureen Jensen, the Widow, is the story's main opponent; Danny Cammack and Geoffrey Agar are smaller, each with his own way of coming at Tom and one scene that is his. Canon gives the three equal standing. Alternative: three equal rivals, at the cost of the hours.
6. One love interest, and small. Alison Sedman is the someone worth being honest for. Philip Danby, canon's second, is a regular. Keith Garbutt, the Fixer, is kept as the man who carries the Widow's offers to people she will not be seen talking to, and has no table of his own.
7. Sheila's name for him has two steps: "Mr Nowak" when she trusts him with the real book, and "Tom" once, later, for something she finds out for herself. Alternative: one step, as built.
8. Tom's line (QUESTIONS 2): no hands on a woman, anyone of sixty or over, or the priest. Adopted as recommended.
9. Tom's letter has words, and his Ledger is in his own dry voice. Canon's rule holds throughout: nobody asks about his past and he never tells.
10. The rule about his past is used once as a key. In the last act a name is wanted for the driver of a car on a night a year ago, and Tom can write his own, because nobody in Meridian can say where he was. Nobody asks him. DS Ellis remarks that nobody does. Alternative: that route is cut and two names remain.
11. Mickey's first heart turn was on the night of the fire; he was on June's ward when his car went out. The ruling on his death stands unchanged.
12. The inspection has causes: the registration changed hands on Mickey's death; Tom's own wash, if he has one; and a telephone call from Agar.
13. Burn Both is shown as two closes. Three endings shown, five states beneath (QUESTIONS 1).
14. Small things minted: the old fare books stand on the shelf behind Sheila's chair; Friday is wages as well as rent; the licence hearing is on Tuesday 30 October; the dockers keep their own fire at the gate on Bonfire Night.

## 1. The spine in a paragraph

In October 1990 Tom Nowak, a stranger with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter from an uncle he never met, takes over a minicab office in a port whose docks have died. With it he gets an old docker who cannot drive and rides in his passenger seat. For three weeks the two of them work the Hook from one car: Ron names every building by what it was, brings the firm his old gang a man at a time, and learns to drive on Sunday mornings at eleven miles an hour. Then, on Bonfire Night, he is standing at the dock gate in a foreman's coat with another firm's name on the back. The port's labour contractor, a widow who never threatens and prices everything, has taken him the way she takes everyone: she asked him what he wanted. The rest of the game is the player finding out, with his hands, what was done with one of Mickey's cars on the night of last year's warehouse fire, why a man who would not cross the road for money sold himself, and what it would have cost either of two proud men to ask the other one question. Then a mild man from Customs and Excise reads two lines in the fare book and wants a driver's name. What the books hold, whose name goes on the paper and who is in which seat of the car decide whether Tom ends as the port's man, a man with a name and a friend, or a stranger with a suitcase again.

## 2. The theme

You never ask for owt. LEDGER is about what people owe each other in a place the official books have written off, and about how much of it goes unpaid because nobody will ask.

- The club's book is the old answer: forty-one names, paid weekly out of thirty years of theft, and nobody on the list has ever asked where it comes from.
- The Widow is the new answer: work for those who can, what the state allows for those who cannot, and every person asked once what they want and held to it.
- Tom takes nothing and asks nothing. So does Ron. So, it turns out, does half the Hook. The story's one real crime was an accident; its real damage was done by a year of good mornings.
- Every fare goes in the book. The fare book is the one thing in the Hook that says what happened without being asked.

The story never says which answer is right. It makes every answer cost a named person something the player can see.

## 3. Tom Nowak

Tom is 32, the son of a Polish post-war settler and Mickey's sister. He has never been to the Hook. His life before it is unsaid: nobody asks and he never tells. He is not voiced.

The letter. In his hand in the first shot and pasted inside the cover of his Ledger:

"Tom. You don't know me. I was your mother's brother. I have a cab office on Quay Street in Meridian, two cars, and a good woman who keeps the books. It is yours when I am gone, and the doctor says that is sooner than I would like. There is more to it than cabs. There is a book. People are owed. Come and look before you say yes. There is a man on the door called Kirby. Keep him if he'll stay. If you cannot stomach it, shut the door, and nobody will blame you, least of all me. Every fare goes in the book. M. Suddaby. P.S. Don't let them call you Mickey's nephew for ever."

His voice. Tom speaks aloud only in the words the player types or picks. Who he is lives in the Ledger, his notebook: a used one, hard-backed, with the first thirty pages cut out close to the spine. Nobody remarks on it. Every entry is short, dry and written from what the player did, and he is funnier on paper than he lets anyone see.

- "Ron says rain by four. Ron has said rain by four every day. It has rained by four every day."
- "Mrs Rennison booked a car to go forty yards with a ham. Asked no questions. The ham had a seat."
- "Lesson two. Eleven miles an hour. He asked to go round again. I said yes before I thought."
- After the Fifth: "Car one. On my own."

What he wants. A place that is his, and his own name said by people who mean it. The letter's postscript is the want.

His flaw. He lets nobody give him anything, and he asks nobody for anything: not a favour, not a reason, not what they want. He pays for tea that was on the house, counts money twice in front of people, carries alone what two could carry. It looks like pride and it is fear: a man who owes nothing and is owed nothing can pick up his suitcase.

The flaw is played, two ways.

- The offers. All through the game people offer Tom things for nothing, and each is one press: take it, or pay for it. Some are what they look like: Grace's tea, Terry's flask on a cold rank, Ron's supper on the quay wall, Alison's umbrella. Some have a hook in them and look the same: Danny's fifty-pound note, the Widow's lift, Agar's forgotten rent. Nothing marks which is which. To take is to owe. A man who takes nothing is owed nothing and known by nobody: at the hearing on 30 October only people he has let do him a turn feel entitled to write for him, and on the last Friday only they will open a door. A man who takes from everybody is everybody's.
- The question. From the third week the player can ask a person, in his own words, what they want. It is never a prompt and never free: a question asked is an answer owed, and some answers cannot be met. The Widow asks it of everyone. It is how she wins.

The load the flaw cannot carry. Ron is the same man. Two men who never ask sit eighteen inches apart for three weeks, and one of them is carrying something the other could lift if either would open his mouth.

What he needs. To learn that being known is a debt, and that asking is the price of it.

His line. He lays hands on no woman, nobody of sixty or over, and not the priest. The game never offers him the blow. It offers him other people's hands, and whether he lets another man do what he will not is his choice.

What he fears. The suitcase: being nobody again. It stays packed under the bed in Mickey's flat until the player unpacks it, one press, whenever he likes. Sheila notices the day he does: "You've unpacked."

His past as a key. The game keeps canon's rule so strictly that the player stops noticing it. Ada says it for the street on the first Tuesday: "I'll not ask where you were before. Nobody on this street will. We've all been somewhere." In the last act it turns out to be the one thing he owns that nobody else in the Hook does: a year with nothing written in it.

How each ending changes him. Each close ends on two lines: one Sheila writes in the fare book and one Tom writes in the Ledger.

- The Kingdom: he becomes the man who holds people. At its best he holds them the way a friend does.
- The Straight Life: he becomes a man with a name and no kingdom. His last entry is about somebody else.
- Burn Both: he becomes what he was on the gangway.

## 4. The people, as the player meets them

Twenty-one who matter. Nobody on this list is secretly anybody else. What each is carrying is in section 10.

- Ron Kirby, 58. A docker for thirty years, on the door and the rank since the scheme ended. He calls Tom "boss" and brings the asks. He cannot drive: "Never needed to. The work was at the end of the street." He wants to be of use the way he was when he had a hook in his belt and a gang.
- Sheila Dunn, 53, the bookkeeper, at Mickey's since she was 22. Exactly as written: "New management", the walk-round, the fare-book rule, the locked door, the Sunday question. She wants the office to outlive Mickey and the fare book kept true. She is sizing him up, as she promised.
- Maureen Jensen, 50, the Widow. Broad-shouldered, dressed like a harbour master. Her husband ran the dock gate; she has run it better since. Since the scheme ended she is the port's labour contractor, registered and lawful: sixty men stand at the gate at six and she picks twenty. She never threatens. She prices, she puts each offer once, and she has kept every deal she ever made.
- Tapper and Shiner, of Ron's old gang, on the Widow's gate now. Tapper says little. Shiner cannot stop.
- The Bishop, 60, of Ron's old gang, who is on nobody's gate.
- Pauline Kitching, 44, on the radio: the firm's running voice, heard in every cab at once. Nine years without a Saturday off.
- Terry Hodgson, 52, the night driver: four words a shift, always the right four. Pauline keeps a tally of her attempts to get a fifth out of him on air.
- Kevin Needham, days, twelve years in Mickey's car, who wants a plate of his own.
- Julie Kettlewell, laid off from the cold store, who can drive and owes the firm £9.
- Darren Milner, 25, who sells what he hears, sleeps where it is dry, and wants more than anything to be somebody's man.
- Ada, 74, the widow at her window. Walter Harker's widow, on the club's list, lonely, sharp, the best witness on Quay Street. Her tea is tea.
- Cyril Blenkin, 63, the tallyman at the landing, who takes the far bank's half of the club over the water. "Mickey's?" He counts an envelope with his thumb, eyes on the water.
- June Suddaby, 34, Mickey's daughter, night sister at the infirmary. "I don't want anything out of that office. I'd check what you're getting, if I were you."
- Father Brendan Walsh, the parish priest, who buried Mickey and counted who came.
- Danny Cammack, 26. Hook Cars, two yellow cars, £1.50 a ride, pagers for his drivers. Loud on purpose, quicker than he lets on.
- Geoffrey Agar, 61, the landlord: silver, unhurried, apologises before every blow. His agents hold the freehold of the parade and half the warehouse row.
- DS Carol Ellis, 44, born two streets from the row. The fire was her file; her inspector closed it as undetermined; she has not stopped. In the file is a docker's brass tally found in the ashes that nobody has matched to a man.
- Alison Sedman, 31, the paper's port reporter. Quick, funny, slightly ashamed of her notebook. She has one question: where did an hour go on the row last November?
- Keith Garbutt, the Fixer: a moustache, a car coat and a percentage. He carries the Widow's offers.
- Mr T. A. Reese, 57, Officer of Customs and Excise, who comes at the end: a flask of tea, a bad knee, a propelling pencil. His father drove a cab. He is sorry, truly, when a book does not add up.

Regulars: Grace at the caff, the Tangs, Brenda Laverack at the fish shop, Edith Stockill at the pension counter, Winifred Duggleby, Shaun Hewson the signwriter.

### Ron and Tom

The friendship is the plot, and it is written for the hands.

- The passenger seat. From the first Thursday Ron rides unless told not to. He names every building by what it was ("That was Stannard's ropewalk. That was the Seamen's Mission. That's still a wall"), and the player learns the map as a list of things that shut. He forecasts rain. He starts a story about Tapper, Shiner or the Bishop on most drives and finishes none of them.
- The lessons. On Sunday mornings on the empty dock road Tom teaches a fifty-eight-year-old docker to drive car two. Ron has the wheel; the player has his voice and the handbrake. Ron sets the terms himself, getting in: "That's yours, boss. I go, you stop. That's the arrangement." Lesson one: if the player is quick, Ron is stopped a yard short of a bollard; if not, the bollard. Lesson two: he drives the whole dock at eleven miles an hour, talking, and asks to go round again. Lesson three comes late and is not funny.
- Fridays. After the round, the Tangs', and the quay wall. Ron buys three of number nine and puts one inside his coat. "Feller I know." Ron pays. If Tom lets him, it is the first thing in the game he has let anyone give him.
- What it builds to. A seat that is empty, and then a question.
- How it pays off. In every ending Ron is in a vehicle. Whose it is, which seat he is in, and whether he looks across, is the verdict.

## 5. The opponent, and two pressures

### Maureen Jensen, the Widow: his people, by asking

- Want: every job at the gate through her hands; Mickey's as the gate's cab firm; and the old club's book shut. "A pension for men who'll never work is a tax on men who do." Eleven men on the club's list could still work, and will not sign with her while the four pounds comes.
- Why she is right: she has sixty men in work that did not exist two years ago. Ron is a better foreman than he ever was a doorman, and everyone who sees him at the gate can see it. The club is dying anyway: twenty envelopes on the Hook's round, not twenty-one.
- Why she is dangerous: she breaks no law and no promise. She takes a firm apart by finding out what each of its people wants and offering exactly that, once. She does it to Kevin in the open, in the second week, so the player knows the move before it is used on the man who matters.
- How she comes, four ways. Offers to his people, carried by Keith Garbutt. The gate's custom: fifty pence on every fare through it, then the cold-store shift run, given or withheld. Her own body: when he will not pay, her men stand across the cold-store road at half past five and she stands in front of them, hands in her coat pockets, a yard from his bonnet. A woman, in front of his car. And the front seat: twice in the game she books car one and sits in Ron's place.
- Her rule, which the story never lets her break: she keeps every deal, she tells no lie when asked a straight question, and she has never in her life spoken to a police officer about anyone. "I'd as soon talk to the tide."

### Danny Cammack: his cover, in daylight

- Want: the Hook, and the end of the old lot. "You've got the keys. I've got the accent. One of us is in the wrong house."
- How he comes: not with a brick at first. He buys chips on Quay Street and talks to Tom like an old friend. He puts Hook Cars' number on the freephone in the ferry terminal. He rides in a Mickey's cab and pays a two-pound fare with a fifty: "Keep it. Call it rent." He takes whatever the street leaves undefended, and says so: "I'm not doing owt to you, Nowak. I'm just what happens when nobody's on a door."
- His scene is scene 10: the only man in the Hook to be frightened by two strangers in car coats, and the first to work out who they were.

### Geoffrey Agar: his clean money, by the calendar

- Want: the yard behind Mickey's, which carries the only right of way to the old basin. His loan on the basin falls due on the thirty-first of December.
- How he comes: by letters, each with a date in it. A rent review, "with regret". A referral of Tom's licence to the sub-committee. Last, a telephone call to Customs and Excise.
- His scene is scene 14: the rain, no hat, and the truest thing any rival says.

## 6. Act I: The Inheritance

Monday 15 to Sunday 21 October 1990. About two hours. The most scripted act. The beats of the approved first week stand; what this version adds is marked.

### Monday: the arrival and the first night

1. The ferry (0:00). The letter in his hand over grey water, the last line readable: "Every fare goes in the book." Nobody meets him. He has £23.50.
2. Quay Street (1:30). A line each, in passing: Darren, friendly and owing; Ron, "boss"; Ada at her window; June.
3. Sheila's walk-round (1:45). Her six lines as approved. "New management." The fare book: "Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule, and it's mine now." Added, on the forecourt, at car two: "Mind that wing. It's been done once."
4. The first fare (2:30). Father Walsh, from the infirmary to his house by the chapel, Pauline giving the way by landmarks. Sheila takes a driver's badge from the drawer: "Mickey's. Wear it till the council sits." The priest, at his door: "You missed a good funeral. Forty-one envelopes on the plate, and every one of them empty. They wanted him to know they'd come."
5. The rest of the office (4:30). The locked door ("Not today"), the flat, the debt book: five names, £156. Friday is rent, £38, and five wage packets, and an honest week will not cover both.
6. The first deed (5:30). Darren's £22: front him up, take what he offers, strike it out. Somebody sees.
7. The first ask (7:00). Ron, the coat, the envelope. "Who's it for?" "Tallyman. Counted cargo on that quay forty year. That's all I'll say on a pavement, boss."
8. The carry (9:00). As written and as section 13 finishes it.
9. The landing (11:30). "Mickey's?" The thumb, the water. "Right. Wednesday, same again. And tell Kirby the round's twenty this week, not twenty-one." Ron counts £19 carrier's money into Tom's hand. Home by the yard. The tin. Sleep. First Ledger page.

### Tuesday to Thursday

The approved beats, in order: Darren says the night to Tom's face; the first count with Sheila; fares with a wrinkle; Wayne Harrison's £30, paid in goods; Lee Drewery's £15 on the quay, where people stop to watch; Ada's "What were you doing in my alley?" and her one invitation; Julie Kettlewell's £9, mercy or shame; Wednesday's second envelope against Ada's tea; Dennis Iveson's £80; Ellis on the street if it is loud.

Added or changed:

- Grace will not take money for his first tea. Take it or pay: the first offer the game counts. Terry's flask, on Tuesday night's rank, is the second.
- Ada on the first Tuesday, on her step: "I'll not ask where you were before. Nobody on this street will. We've all been somewhere."
- The tea. Ada tells him plainly what he has been carrying: the sick club her Walter started in 1953, forty-one names, four pounds, half over the water. "The tallyman keeps the far side. The gate used to fill it. I get mine like the rest." Then, of his doorman, as a recommendation: "He's a good man, Ron Kirby. He came home at two one morning last November with no sleeves to his coat, and I never asked. You'll find I don't."
- Alison in the cab on Wednesday, to the warehouse row. "The brigade were called at half past eleven. The watchman swore he was on his rounds. Nobody was on that row for the best part of an hour. I'd like to know where an hour went." It is raining when she gets out, and she leaves her umbrella on the back seat. Give it back, or keep it till she comes for it.
- A letter from the council on Thursday: his licence application will be heard on Tuesday 30 October, referred by a member.
- Thursday night, two dockers waiting by car two: the first time Ron rides. Afterwards, in the car: "You never ask for owt, do you. Mickey was the same. Dead at a desk with the kettle on."

### Friday: rent, wages and the round

- Morning. Dawn Pybus with her clipboard, collecting for Mr Agar's agents.
- Noon. Wages by hand: Pauline, Kevin, Terry, Ron. Sheila will not take hers until the others are paid. If he is short he chooses who waits.
- Night five, the third ask: a holdall. The Hook's half of the club goes round by hand on Fridays, twenty doors, and Ron walks it. "You'll want to see where it goes, boss. Or you won't. Mickey always walked it." A carry with faces: Winifred Duggleby; Albert Suggitt mending a net; Ada at her own door; a door on Tannery Row where Ron says "Knock soft. Her daughter's police"; and, further along the same row, one door where Ron goes up alone with a packet that did not come out of the holdall. "Not that one. That's Mickey's own."
- Then the Tangs', three of number nine, and the quay wall.

### Saturday and Sunday

- Saturday. The street fills at three and at five. Danny Cammack is on it, buying chips. Shaun Hewson is up a ladder at the parade's boards and wants to know what Mickey's is to say now.
- Sunday, early. The first lesson. "That's yours, boss. I go, you stop." The bollard, or a yard short of it. On Monday Kevin looks at the bollard, not the car: "That's had a Mickey's wing off it before."
- Sunday, ten o'clock. Sheila comes in on her day off. If she trusts him: "Mr Nowak." The first time. The real book is on the counter, shut. "That's the club's book and what Mickey ran beside it. You've had your week. So which is it going to be?" Wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say; asked twice, as built. The street learns the answer.

What the player believes at the end of week one: an old dockers' club, kept alive by his uncle, pays forty-one families; something happened on the warehouse row last year that nobody will say; his uncle's letter says people are owed; and he has a friend. The friend is the only one of those he would miss.

## 7. Act II: The Rise and the Squeeze

Monday 22 October to Sunday 2 December. About nine and a half hours. Scenes fire on states, never on sequence, with two exceptions the town itself names: the calendar's days (the hearing, Bonfire Night, Remembrance Sunday, the anniversary) and dates that arrive in letters.

The act is in three movements: the Seat (two men and a car, about four and a half hours), the Empty Seat (one man, about three), and Two in the Car (about two). Eighteen scenes.

What a week is, between the scenes. Days are fares, each a person with something going on. Dusk is a job: something to win or hold. Night is the count with Sheila. Friday is wages, rent and the round. The firm rises as the design has it: a third car with Julie at the wheel, the ferry's freephone, the cold-store shift run, the row's weekly "watch" if he took the club over. Each of those is also something the Widow can price.

### First movement: the Seat

Scene 1. Tapper, and the freephone. Danny's number is on the freephone in the ferry terminal, and the boat's fares go to yellow cars. Ron brings Tapper, who works the ferry's ropes and knows when the ticket hall is empty. Getting Mickey's number back on that wall is a night's work with a screwdriver, a word with the clerk, or a price war that bleeds. Comes back: Danny, next day, delighted: "You've a screwdriver, then. I'll tell the lads." In the car, after: "Tapper could tap a tea chest so it sang the weight. One time him and the Bishop had a crate of..." Rain by four. He does not finish.

Scene 2. The gate at six. The Widow books a car herself and gets into the front. "Save you turning round." Ron has to get into the back, and does, without a word. Sixty men in the dark, twenty picked; Shiner picked first, talking. Her first offer, put once: fifty pence on every fare through the gate. Getting out, not looking at Ron: "You're wasted on a door, Kirby. You always were." Ron moves back to the front before he speaks. "Shiner. Shiner could talk a customs man out of his own hat. He was talking the night we..." He stops. Choice: pay the fifty pence, or do not.

Scene 3. The long way round. Kevin's day fares come in short against his mileage; Sheila sees it in the book before anyone says it. Followed, he is carrying parcels for the gate in Mickey's car. Fronted on the rank, in front of the street: "Twelve years I've driven his car. She asked me what I wanted. Nobody here ever did." Choice: sack him there; keep him and feed the gate what Tom wants it to hear; or ask him the question now. Asked, he tells: a plate of his own. It can be met, and it costs: an owner-driver is less a man's own. From this scene the question is in the player's hands for the rest of the game.

Scene 4. The hearing, Tuesday 30 October. The council's licensing sub-committee, one room in the town hall. A member who owes Agar has referred the application over the dead man's badge. The clerk reads the references the applicant has handed in. Only people Tom has let do him a turn have written one: Grace, Edith in fountain pen, Father Walsh. If he has paid his way with everybody, the clerk says "No references," and waits. Then the door at the back opens, and the public bench fills: twenty old dockers in funeral suits, caps on knees, Tapper and Shiner among them. Nobody asked them. Ron is standing by the door. "I can't write you one, boss. I can fetch them as'll sit." It is the biggest gift in the game, and the player was never offered the press. Outcomes: licensed; licensed with conditions; or refused, after which every fare is a risk. None ends the game.

Scene 5. The cold-store road. If the fifty pence was refused, or left three days, it is answered: the Widow's men stand across the cold-store road at half past five, and she stands in front of them, a yard from his bonnet. He can edge the packing shift forward until she steps aside or does not; pay; lose the shift; or send Ron. If Ron goes, he gets out, walks to his old gang, says six words the player cannot hear, and the line opens like a gate. The Widow watches him do it. That is the moment she decides what she wants, and the player sees her decide. If the fifty pence was paid, the same morning plays as her thanks: Ron is asked, in front of Tom, to settle a row between two gangs at the gate, and does, in six words.

Scene 6. A wing, twice. Shaun Hewson repaints car two's wing in the yard. "Second time I've done this one. Same place and all. Mickey said he'd clipped the bollard on the dock road. He was a careful driver, Mickey. I thought it at the time." That week Keith Garbutt is seen twice at the rank after dark, talking to Ron, who listens with his arms folded. Asked: "Fixer. Fixing." Ron is quiet for two days and buys two of number nine on Friday, not three, and then goes back for the third.

Scene 7. The second lesson. Sunday 4 November. L-plates. Pauline tells every cab to keep off the dock road. Ron drives its whole length at eleven miles an hour, talking the whole way, and asks to go round again. At the far end the warehouse row opens ahead of them. He stops short of it. "Not down there, boss. Surface is bad." It is not. Asked about it: "That's all I'll say in a car with a radio in it." Then, getting out, a thing he has never done: "I'll want tomorrow night, boss. Summat I've to see to." He has never asked for anything.

What the player believes going into the Fifth: Kevin is the man he might lose, Danny is the man to watch on Bonfire Night ("I'd park them cars indoors Monday, if I was you"), and his uncle clipped a bollard. A watchful player has more: a wing done twice by a careful driver, a coat with no sleeves, a third portion, a road Ron will not drive down, and a Fixer on the rank. Nothing stops him putting any of it to Ron, or following the third portion on a Friday. If he does, Ron tells him what the Widow has offered and what she knows, and the midpoint is a different scene.

### The midpoint: the Fifth

Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990. The quay is full of the town's grown people for the fireworks off the old coal jetty, and at the dock gate the dockers have their own fire, as every year: pallets and dunnage, a blaze the size of a van. It is the busiest night of the cab year, and the first night Tom has driven without Ron. Twenty minutes of the best fares in the game, fast and funny, the cars kept moving because Danny said to park them.

1. The fare. In Pauline's ordinary voice: "Car one, the dock gate, on the Widow's account, and she says bring a coat, there's a fire." At the gate the Widow is standing on a pallet with a clipboard, reading the winter's hire list by firelight. Beside her, straight-backed, in a foreman's donkey jacket with THE GATE across the shoulders, is Ron. His old gang is round him. He sees the cab. He does not come over.
2. The fire. On top of the dockers' bonfire, where the guy goes, somebody has sat a figure in a car coat with a cab's roof sign hung round its neck. The sign says MICKEY'S. It is Danny's lads' joke, and the gate laughs. Ron does not laugh. He does not take it down either. He watches it burn with his hands in the pockets of her coat. It is the thing burning that should not be, and half the Hook sees who stood by.
3. The radio. On the dock road, going back, Pauline, to every cab at once, kindly, which is worse: "Car one. You on your own?"
4. The window. With nobody on the door, Danny's lads have put the office window in while the street watched fireworks. Ada is on her step in her coat. "You'd better come in, Tom." It is the first time anyone on the street has used his name. Tea. She tells him who did the window. Then the thing she has been turning over: "He'll not have gone for money. I've known Ron Kirby forty years. He'd not cross the road for money."

What is lost: the seat. In the morning the docker's hook he wore in his belt for thirty years is on the office counter, and his door key is on top of it.

What the player believes: his friend has been bought, like Kevin, in the same way and by the same woman, and he watched it coming and did not see it. Ada's sentence is the first crack.

If the player found it out first. Then the Fifth is planned by two men on the quay wall. Ron goes to the gate all the same, because he believes it is the only coin that buys her silence, but he goes as Tom's man, and the coat is a thing they chose. The effigy still burns and Ron still has to stand and watch it. The second movement is then two men working one gate from both sides, and the truth in scene 12 lands on both of them at once.

### Second movement: the Empty Seat

About three hours. It is built to ache and not to drag: it holds the two funniest set pieces in the game, and Ron is never out of sight. Every morning at six he is on the far side of the dock road, picking twenty men. On the second morning he picks something else, without looking across: "Mickey's. Two cars for the packing shift. Six sharp." A gift from a foreman to his old firm. Take it or pay.

Scene 8. Who sits in front. Fares alone. Nobody names the buildings. Other people start taking the front seat. Darren: "Can I sit up front? Only it's empty." Let him, or not. He has forty transistor radios off the back of a lorry, every one of which picks up only the pirate station, and he sells them to fares from the front seat. Garbutt goes round the firm with the Widow's offers, each put once: Kevin has his plate by Friday, if Tom did not meet it; Julie is offered a van; and Pauline is asked what she wants. If the player asks Pauline first, the answer is a Saturday, and it is the game's best set piece of plain comedy. Pauline's hour: she takes her first Saturday in nine years and Tom has the radio. The drivers test him at once. Kevin reports himself in Copper Row with gulls behind him. Terry uses his four words to give four different addresses. Sheila corrects every call Tom makes without looking up from the book. Darren asks the question himself, unasked: he wants the door. A coat with a firm's name on it. It is the one want in the firm that it may be wrong to meet, and nothing says so.

Scene 9. Car two. The back seat has to come out for the wing to be done properly. Under it, where a thing would roll: a cotton glove, burnt through at the palm, and a docker's brass tally with a number on it. Sheila looks at them on the counter and goes on writing. The 1989 books are on the shelf behind her chair, where they have stood since the walk-round. The find is a count, done with the hands: the night's sheets, the mileage column and the fare book, set side by side. For Saturday 18 November 1989 there is a driver's sheet for car one in Terry's hand and none for car two. In the fare book, two lines in the small upright hand:

"22.50. Car one. Office to Infirmary. One. No charge."

"Night. Car two. Out and back. Eleven miles. No sheet. No charge."

The night of the fire. Terry, asked, uses seven words: "I took Mickey up. Not the row." Mickey Suddaby was on a ward with his heart on the night his cab went out, and his daughter was the sister on it. The dead uncle did not do it. His own fare book says so. And somebody took car two eleven miles on a night when the only two men licensed to drive it were in the other car and in a hospital bed.

Scene 10. The answer. The window and the effigy want answering, and the street is waiting to see whether a firm with nobody on its door can. It is the one job in the game planned like a job, on the board in the office, with a week to plan it. Three keys.

- Stage it. Tapper and Shiner, lent for a day by a foreman who does not ask what for, go over on the morning boat in borrowed car coats and come back on the last ferry as two strangers with a holdall. Car one meets them with its light off and drives them, slowly, down Quay Street, past the Tangs' queue, to Danny's lock-up at the dead end of the row, where they stand and look at it for five minutes and are driven back to the landing. Nothing is touched. The planning is who sees what: which clerk is on, which windows the route passes, and the one rule Ron sends with them: Shiner is not to speak, because half the Hook knows his voice. Shiner lasts as far as the Tangs'. In the car he cannot stop, and what comes out, to fill the silence, is the thing he has been not saying for a month: "I only told her who was driving. That's all. I thought she'd laugh. She didn't laugh. She went quiet and did sums." Tapper hits him with his cap.
- Answer it by hand. Go to the lock-up himself, at night, and put Danny's window in, or more.
- Do not answer it. Then the yellow cars are along the kerb by the weekend.

Comes back: Danny. Done well, he empties his lock-up that night and is seen doing it. Two days later he gets into car one, alone, in the back. "Them two. The ticket lass says they went over on the nine o'clock. You sent two old dockers on a day out and I shifted four hundred radios in the rain." A long look. "That's the best thing anybody's done to me." Then, serious: "Your doorman's on her gate and your door's a hole. I'm not doing owt to you, Nowak. I'm just what happens." Done badly, he knew on the night and says so in the Tangs' queue.

What the player now believes: Ron drove car two on the night of the fire; Shiner told the Widow; and Ron is at her gate because she is holding it over him. It is the shape every story of this kind has. Part of it is wrong.

Scene 11. Remembrance Sunday, 11 November. The dockside memorial. By the Hook's custom Father Walsh reads the names: the wars, and the men the docks have taken since. This year, Michael Suddaby. Everybody is in one place for the only time: Ada, out of her house; the Widow across the square; Agar with the Harbour Board's wreath; Danny at the back; Ellis; Sheila; June; Alison with her notebook shut; Ron with the gate's wreath, in the coat. And beside the dockers' wreath, in a wheeled chair with a rug, a man of sixty whose hands are in grey cotton gloves. Two minutes of silence in which the player chooses only where Tom looks, and sees who looks back. Afterwards Ron puts a foil parcel in the man's lap. The third portion.

Scene 12. Tannery Row. The player can follow the third portion on a Friday, or go to the door Ron always took alone. A terrace; a sister who answers; a man in a chair by the window who breathes like someone who has to think about it. Tom has the tally. The Bishop looks at it for a long time, and tells one thing, the only telling in the movement: "That's mine. He came back for me. Carried me out on his back, and then he drove that car like a man killing it. You'll be the nephew. Your uncle never missed a Friday. You needn't start." He says "came back". The player may hear it or not.

Scene 13. Two widows. Ada asks to be driven. She has not been in a car in four years. "The dock gate." The Widow has signed three of the club's eleven able men that week, and Ada means to say what she thinks of it. At the gate the foreman opens the cab's back door for his employer, and it is Ron, and he and Tom are a yard apart with a car door between them. Then two widows in the back of a minicab on the dock road in the dark, the meter running, Tom's eyes in the mirror. Ada: "A club is a thing you're in. What you're offering is a thing done to them." Maureen: "It's a wage." Then Ada, because she misses nothing: "You've Ron Kirby in a coat with your name on. What did that cost you?" Maureen: "Less than you'd think. He never asked what it was worth." Both of them look at the back of Tom's head. Maureen pays what is on the meter, to the penny.

### Third movement: Two in the Car

About two hours. It opens with the two questions the whole game has been teaching the player to ask.

Scene 14. The Widow in the passenger seat. She books car one and sits in front, in his seat. Her last offer, put once: Mickey's becomes the gate's firm; Tom keeps the name on the roof and a wage; the club's book is shut, the eleven get work, and she will see the thirty onto what the state allows. "It stands until your books have been looked at. After that I'm buying from whoever has the keys." The player can say yes, say no, or ask her a straight question. She has never lied to one. Asked what she holds over Ron: "Nothing. I told him what Shiner told me, and I told him what a foreman earns, in the one breath, and I let him do the sum. I never said I'd tell. I never said I wouldn't. He didn't ask." A pause. "I'd as soon talk to the tide as to that sergeant. Anyone on this dock could have told him so. He never asked them either." Ron sold himself for a silence that was never for sale. She let him, because she wanted a foreman. That is the whole of her wrong, and she will say so if asked.

Scene 15. The third lesson. Sunday 18 November, a year to the day. The player goes to the gate at six. Ron is picking twenty men, and he is good at it, and they like him. Tom holds out the keys. "I go, you stop." Ron drives, properly, which is the thing to notice, out along the dock road and, this time, down the warehouse row. Past the burnt plot he does not slow. He speeds up, the way he did that night, back towards the dock road and the bollard, and the player has the handbrake. Stopped, a yard short or with the wing gone a third time, he sits with his hands on the wheel and finishes a story for the first time in the game. Four of them went in for the cigarettes. The lamp went over. "I can drive, boss. Near enough. I'd sat next to drivers thirty year. I got Tapper and Shiner in and I went. I was at this bollard before I counted heads. Then I went back." Eleven miles: the row, the bollard, the row again, Tannery Row, the yard. "He's never once said it. Came back, he says, like it was a kindness. Then you were that pleased when I got the length of the dock. I couldn't tell you." Every laugh the lessons had in them is still there, and is now something else.

Then the player tells him what the Widow said, or does not. Told, Ron says nothing for a time. "A year. I could have asked her." And then the question from the quay wall, a month late: "What do you think, boss?" The choice is the act's, and it is put kindly both ways. Come back: he comes, and the seat is filled, and the gate has lost the best foreman it had, and he knows what he gave up. Stay: he stays at the gate as a free man, which is the best thing for Ron, and the seat is empty for good. Or tell him it is his to decide, and ask him what he wants. He has never been asked. He does not know. That answer comes in Act III.

Scene 16. The door. Whatever was decided, the street is still Danny's to take: yellow cars along the kerb, his lads on the rank after dark. Tom holds his own door with his own hands in front of the Tangs' queue. Who is beside him is the sum of the movement: Ron, if he came back; Darren in a coat too big for him, if he was given the door, who is brave and no use and gets a split lip that June stitches; Terry, who gets out of car two by himself; nobody. A lost fight is Grace's kitchen. Danny, after, win or lose: "Fair enough."

Scene 17. Ellis and Alison. The anniversary has put the fire back on every counter. Alison's choice comes due: her editor has offered her the news desk if she will leave the row alone, and she says so in the cab, laughing at it. "He asked me what I wanted. Nobody at that paper ever has." She asks Tom, as the one person in Meridian whose answer she will take, what he thinks she should do, and what he tells her truly she keeps, at a cost to her. Ellis walks the row again with the watchman and then comes to the yard to look at car two's wing, wet with new paint. She has a brass tally in an envelope and has had it for a year. "Whose is this car to drive?" She asks while a thing is answerable.

Scene 18. Agar in the rain. Geoffrey Agar walks down Quay Street for the first time in his life, without a hat, and knocks at the office. He has come to offer for the yard. Refused, he says the truest thing any rival says: "I have never had to burn anything, Mr Nowak. Things on that quay fall down by themselves, and I buy what is left. I shall buy what is left of you." Then, at the door, in another voice: "I am told a man of yours may want a solicitor before the year is out. I keep three. It is a gift. I do not expect you to believe that." Take it or pay. It is the heaviest press in the game, and nothing marks which it is. Either way he has already made a telephone call.

The act ends on a letter: courteous, dated, signed T. A. Reese.

## 8. Act III: The Ledger Comes Due

Monday 3 to Saturday 15 December. About two and a half hours. Seven beats.

### Why the inspector comes

1. The name on the form changed. When a registered trader dies, Customs and Excise look at the business before they register the next owner. Every player gets the letter.
2. Tom's own wash, if he has one: a reason to set fuel and mileage against the book.
3. A gentleman has telephoned to say the firm's cars are out at night with nothing written. Agar does not know how right he is.

The letter comes on Monday 3 December and names Monday the 10th. In the week between, each opponent moves once. The Widow's offer stands, and Garbutt says so at the counter. Agar's agents serve notice of a rent review, with regret. Danny's lads take the rank on the Wednesday night, and it is held or lost as scene 16 was.

### The week

Monday 10 December. Reese at the counter with the book since October. "A fare at no charge is either a kindness or a hole, Mr Nowak. I'm paid to ask which." Whatever cannot be put in front of him he asks for "in writing, by five on Friday".

Tuesday. The ride-along. "I always ride a route or two. Mileage is a truthful thing." He gets into the front passenger seat without asking. "My father drove a cab. I always rode in front." He sits where Ron sat, with his flask. If Ron came back, Ron gets into the back for the second time in the game, and the two of them discuss the weather with great seriousness. Brenda gets in with a box of live crabs that does not stay shut. "Are those declared, Mrs Laverack?" "They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in." Darren tries to sell a Customs officer a radio. Everything that happens is perfectly legal.

Wednesday. The old books. He goes quiet twice and makes two small marks.

Thursday. He reads both lines aloud. "Ten to eleven, car one, office to infirmary, one, no charge. A kindness; I see whose name is against it. And the same night, car two, out and back, eleven miles, no sheet, no charge. Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak." Then, mildly, the reason it is his business: "A car out with no sheet is a shift I cannot see. If I know who drove it, and he will sign that he took no fare, it is one night and an oversight. If I do not, I am obliged to treat it as a working shift, to suppose there were others like it, and to assess three years on that footing. I should not like to. It would close you." He lays a short printed statement on the counter. It has one line for a name and one for a signature. "By five tomorrow."

Thursday night: Sheila's kitchen. Tom has never been to her house and has to find it. The 1989 book is on the oilcloth with the slip in it. "I wrote what the car did. I never wrote who. I came in on the Monday and it had eleven miles on it and a wing gone and the back seat smelt like a grate. I've known a year, and so has he, and we've said good morning every day of it. I'd not write a name I didn't see." Then she turns to the first page under the new owner and shows him a line he has never read: "15.10. Ferry landing. One. No charge." She entered him the day he came. "You were the last thing Mickey sent for. It wanted writing down that somebody came. Nobody reads a fare book." There is a pen on the table and a fire in the grate. He can leave both lines of 1989. He can strike the first, the true one, Mickey's alibi, so that a dead man drove. He can put the book on the fire, after which the books cannot hold. She does not ask him to do any of them. On the pavement outside, in whichever coat he now wears, is Ron. Kevin talks. "I'll go in tomorrow and sign it, boss. Don't tell me not to."

Friday: the round, backwards. The phone box on the corner, five coins, and until five. He can ring only people he once let give him something, and every call that is answered is a door: he has to drive to it, knock and ask. Each thing asked for changes what a name on that paper will cost the man it belongs to.

- The Bishop, to say in writing whose lamp it was and who carried him out.
- June, to say where her father was at eleven that night. It is the first thing anyone has asked her for since the funeral.
- The gate. Tapper and Shiner can say what four men went in for, and cannot leave the gate without the Widow's word. He has to ask her. She prices it: one thing of his, named, once. Or, if he has dealt straight with her all game, she says, "Go on, the pair of you," and that is all.
- Agar's solicitor, if the gift was taken.
- Father Walsh and Ada, for what a street's word is worth.

The constable of the first night stops him on the lit quay with a folder of papers under his coat. "What's that, Nowak?" This time he shows it.

Five o'clock. The counter, Reese's pencil, one line. Three names can go on it.

- "M. Suddaby." Reese: "The dead sign nothing. I can take the proprietor's word that the late proprietor drove, if the book does not say he was elsewhere." It needs the true line gone and June's silence. It is the thing the letter asked him not to be.
- "R. Kirby." Ron signs it himself. Reese: "Did you take a fare, Mr Kirby?" "No." "Then I have one night and an oversight, and I am glad of it. I have also a man who drove without a licence, which is not a matter for Customs and Excise. I am obliged to note it." The note goes along the corridor to Ellis. What it costs Ron is what Friday's doors made it: with the Bishop's paper, June's and the gate's, a detective who has wanted the truth for a year gets it, and it is four old men, a lamp and a friend carried out. "I can't make that the crime of the century, Mr Kirby. I'm tired of trying." Without them, it is a burned warehouse and a name.
- "T. Nowak." Nobody in Meridian can say where Tom Nowak was on the eighteenth of November 1989. The player writes it. Reese looks at the name for some time, and at the date the registration changed, and asks nothing. "That is the name you wish to give me. Then I shall copy it out." If Ron is in the room: "He was never here." Reese, mildly: "I can only take one, gentlemen." The last word is the player's. Later, on the station steps, Ellis: "I've a name on a Customs paper and a street that can't say it wasn't him, because nobody on it knows where he was. I'm not going to ask you. I think that's what you're counting on."

Then Reese closes his notebook. "That all seems to be in order, Mr Nowak." Or, if the firm's own books for this year do not hold: "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak." Neither comes from nowhere: all week the Ledger has said, in Tom's words, what is at risk and why.

Saturday 15 December: the last drive. Ron comes to the rank and holds out his hand for the keys. "I go, you stop." Tom gets in on the passenger side: his voice, and the handbrake. Where Ron drives depends on the name on the paper and on what he was asked on the row. Up the hill to the police station, to answer for it himself. To the ferry ramp, with a bag on the back seat, to be gone before anybody comes. To the dock gate, in the foreman's coat. Or just round. The Widow is on the pavement at the gate as they pass, hands in her pockets, and she nods once, to the man who asked her a straight question, if he did.

The handbrake has meant one thing all game: Ron goes and Tom stops him, because Ron cannot be trusted to stop himself. At the end of the last drive there is a kerb, or a ramp, or a bollard, and the prompt is under the player's hand as it was on the dock road. If he pulls it at the ferry ramp, Ron stays, and has been made to. If he pulls it on the hill, Ron does not go up the steps, and Tom has decided for him again. And if things between them are right, and the player leaves it alone, Ron brakes, and stops, and puts it on himself. "Got it, boss." The last input in the game is one the player can decline.

## 9. The endings

States, never a menu. Three shown, five beneath, in the built order. Each has a last scene, a line Sheila writes in the fare book and a line Tom writes in the Ledger. In each the verdict is which seat Ron is in.

### The Kingdom

Reached when the business stands and the books hold.

The plain turn. A dead man's name is on the paper, or Tom said yes to nothing and no to nobody and simply outlasted them. He has the freephone, three cars, the shift run and the row's watch, and the gate deals with him as an equal. Last scene: the landing after ten. A carrier in a dark coat that does not fit him walks the envelope down the lit quay; it is Darren, if he was given the door. The tallyman asks, for the first time, "Nowak's?" On the rank a cab comes in and Ron gets out of the passenger side, as he always has, and stands by the door, and does not look up at the flat window. He is back because he was told he owed it, or because a line was struck for him that he did not ask to have struck. If the true line was struck, Sheila's keys are on the counter on top of the fare book. If not: "Goodnight, Mr Nowak." Fare book: "Landing. Account. No charge." Ledger, in a hand that has started to look like Mickey's: "41 out. T.N."

The better turn (the built Both: one friend counts him, the street does not hold his rackets as fact, Ellis's case is answered, the books hold). Ron's name is on the paper because Ron was asked what he wanted and said it, and Tom stood beside him at the counter with a folder the Hook had filled. Ron was fined for driving unlicensed and Ellis has shut her file. The Bishop's Fridays are in the fare book now as an account, in the open. Last scene: six in the morning, the dock road, car one. Ron is in the passenger seat by choice, naming buildings. "That was Stannard's ropewalk." At the gate the Widow is picking twenty, with Tapper as her foreman; she looks at the cab and nods. Sheila, at the office, as he goes out: "Night, Tom." A careful first player can reach it. It is hard, not hidden. Fare book: "Tannery Row. Account. Fridays." Ledger: "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four."

### The Straight Life

Reached when no business of his stands at the close and one friendship holds.

The better turn (the built Quiet). Tom's own name is on the paper. A man who has signed that he drove unlicensed cannot hold an operator's licence, so the firm has gone to Julie Kettlewell, with Terry on the night desk and the radio. Last scene: the police station on the hill, a grey afternoon. Tom comes down the steps on bail. Car two is at the kerb with L-plates on and Ron at the wheel, on the right side, and Ron looks across. He drives him home at eleven miles an hour, and what he says on the way is the truest line in the ending: "You never ask for owt. You've just done it again. I'd have signed it. You never asked." It is a gift, and it is the flaw, and it is both. Sheila has found out for herself whose name it really was; it is the thing she says "Tom" for. Fare book, her hand: "Police station to office. One. No charge." Ledger: "Ron drove."

The plain turn. He sold to the Widow, on her terms, kindly. The roof sign reads THE GATE. The club's book is shut, the eleven are working, and the thirty are on what the state allows; Ada has not taken hers. Sheila clears a desk of thirty-one years and gives him the last fare book: "It's a full one. I'd not throw it out." He stands at the landing in daylight with the suitcase, and the ferry goes out without him. The gate's van passes with Ron in the passenger seat, and Ron lifts one hand. Somebody passing says, "Morning, Tom." Fare book: "Closed." Ledger: "Stayed."

### Burn Both

Reached when nothing else fits. Two closes.

The Reckoning, for the man whose own books did not hold whatever name he gave. "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak." Sheila is asked for her keys by a constable half her age and hands them over without looking at Tom. By Saturday Agar's agents have a padlock on the door, and the Widow is buying from whoever has the keys. Saturday night is the first night again: the quay after ten, the lamp, no envelope. The constable turns round. He does not call "You there." He calls, "Nowak!" The top of the name ladder from the wrong side. The player has the two inputs of night one, stand or run, and the close cuts on the press. Fare book: closed unwritten. Ledger: the last page cut out, as the first thirty were.

The Suitcase, for the man who never asked anybody anything. No judgement, because nobody thinks enough of him to hold one. A grey afternoon. He walks down the gangway he walked up. Darren asks him for a pound. The gate's van passes with Ron in the passenger seat, and Ron does not look across. Ada watches from her window the whole way, as she did when he came. The fare book's last line, in the small upright hand, is the line she wrote the day he came, written again: "Ferry landing. One. No charge." Ledger: a date, and nothing under it.

### What every ending settles

Whose name is on the paper and whether the true line still stands; where Ron is and in which seat; whether Sheila is still at the counter, and what she calls him; who holds the forty-one, and who pays the Bishop's Fridays; who has the firm; what became of Darren, Kevin, Pauline's Saturdays and Terry; where the Widow, Danny and Agar stand; whether Alison took the desk; whether June was asked for anything. No ending redeems what was done. Reloading is never stopped.

## 10. What was true all along

For the builders: the truth in the order the player can find it. The street never has it. The street says the importer burned his own warehouse and went south.

1. The club. Walter Harker's sick club of 1953 grew a second purse under the dock scheme: cargo short-tallied at the gate by Ron's gang, held on the row, moved in Mickey's cabs. It paid forty-one names £4 a week, half of them over the water. When the scheme ended in July 1989 the gate stopped; the Widow went lawful and paid nothing in. Since then the envelopes have been filled by a small weekly "watch" from the row's tenants and by Mickey's own drawings from the firm, which is why there was never a third car.
2. The night. By November 1989 the club could not last the winter. The importer's warehouse held a consignment of cigarettes, the last thing on the quay worth lifting. Mickey planned it: four men, twenty minutes, the watchman on his break, a cab for cover. At ten to eleven Mickey's heart turned at his desk. Sheila took him to the infirmary in car one, with Terry driving. The men were already waiting at the landing. Ron, who had sat beside drivers for thirty years, took car two.
3. The fire. A lamp went over on the cotton bales. Ron got Tapper and Shiner into the car and drove. He was at the bollard on the dock road, with the wing gone, before he counted heads. He went back, carried the Bishop out, and drove him to his sister's on Tannery Row, because an infirmary asks questions and Mickey was in it. Eleven miles. He walked home at two with no sleeves to his coat. The brigade was called at half past eleven by a man on the far bank who saw the glow.
4. The year. Sheila found the miles, the wing and the smell on the Monday and wrote what the car did. Mickey came off the ward, paid the Bishop's keep every Friday, never forgave himself for a plan that burned a man, and told nobody who had driven, because nobody asked him. Ron swore he would not sit on that side of a car again. The Bishop has said "he came back for me" for a year and never once said "after he went".
5. The letter. Mickey wrote it when the doctor told him. "People are owed" meant the forty-one and one more. "Keep him if he'll stay" was the nearest he could come to saying the rest.
6. The Widow. In October Shiner, who talks, told her who drove. She told Ron what she knew and what a foreman earns in one breath, through Garbutt first and then herself, and let him do the sum. She would never have told. She did not say so, because she wanted him. He did not ask, because Ron Kirby does not ask.
7. The lessons. Ron could drive, near enough, the whole time. He let a stranger teach him because the stranger was pleased, because it was the one hour of the week that was light, and because a man who is being taught cannot be asked how he learned.

What the truth changes when it is known: every lesson; the bollard; "Mind that wing"; three of number nine; "Not down there, boss"; Ron climbing into the back seat for the Widow; the bench of dockers at the hearing, fetched by a man paying something off; the effigy he watched burn; "You never ask for owt"; and Ada's "I never asked", which was a boast in week one and is the whole fault by the end.

Why it surprises. A reader watching for the hidden boss, the guilty dead uncle and the crooked landlord finds a widow who is a widow, an uncle with an alibi in his own fare book and a landlord who has never needed to burn anything. Then, having found a friend with a secret and an opponent who knows it, the reader supplies the blackmail himself, and is wrong: nobody threatened anybody. The opponent hides nothing and breaks no law. The secret is in the comic relief, and the damage was done by silence.

Why it is fair. The wing is named in the first two minutes. The sleeves are in Ada's tea. The third portion is bought in front of him every Friday. Ron will not drive onto the row. Shaun says the wing was done twice and that Mickey was a careful driver. Kevin says how the Widow works three weeks before she works it on Ron. The lines are on a shelf in plain sight. Shiner talks. And the Widow has never told a lie to a straight question, which the player can test on small things from the gate at six onward.

## 11. The story of the player who winds the firm down

On the first Sunday he tells Sheila it is a cab firm and nothing more. The carry and the round stop. The two roads then run the same eighteen scenes, and each reads differently.

- Monday. June gets into his cab for her shift and says what this road is built on: "It doesn't have to be shut. It has to be lawful. There are friendly societies older than the docks. You'd want a room, a bank book and the names." Father Walsh has a room. Edith Stockill has the forms. The names are Ada's to give.
- The first Friday after. Ron: "There's one I'll still walk, boss. It's not the club's. It never was." The Bishop's door. A man who has shut the book still owes on the page that was Mickey's own.
- The Widow likes him. He has done half her work. Her first offer is warmer and her price for the firm is higher, and that is its danger: on this road she is the most reasonable person in the Hook. Refusing her means saying why a club should outlive its docks.
- Ada. "A club is a thing you're in. A charity is a thing done to you. I'll not be means-tested by Mickey's nephew." The society needs her name first on its list, and she gives it only to a man who has been in her debt and stayed there: the tea, taken, and never paid for.
- The hands. This road keeps every job the other has, because the jobs belong to the firm and the friendship, not to the club: the freephone, the cold-store road, the staged answer, the door, the count, the tail to Tannery Row, the lessons. What it lacks is the watch and the carry, and what it gains is three: the society's first pay-out, carried from the bank to the chapel hall in a holdall on foot with Danny's lads behind him; the hall held on the night Agar's agents send men to clear it early; and the eleven, who must each be asked.
- The last act. He washed nothing, and the 1989 line can still close him: an assessment of three years falls on whoever owns the firm now. His clean books for 1990 are the proof that he is not his uncle, and the name on the paper is the same choice.

## 12. Side stories that are whole stories

The thirty-two drafts of 8 November stay as the short ones, found on foot. Four are grown here into whole stories: a person with a want, a turn the player does not see coming, and an end the town keeps.

1. The watch (Ron). Found: Mickey's watch in the pawnshop window the week he died. Rita will not say who pawned it until it is redeemed; there is a florist's bill. Turn: Ron pawned it himself, for the funeral flowers, because the office was frozen and he would not ask Sheila. Ends: redeemed and handed back without a word; asked about; or left in the window. After the Fifth it reads differently: the wreath at the funeral was paid for by the man who drove the car.

2. A ride for the sergeant (Carol Ellis, Ivy Leadley). Found: rain, night, the sergeant's own car dead by the fish market, and Pauline, dry: "It's the police. She wants a car." The address is the door on Tannery Row where Ron said to knock soft. After that Ivy Leadley, seventy-one, books Mickey's every Sunday for chapel and asks for "the new one". Turn: Ivy knows exactly what her four pounds is. She has told her daughter for years that it comes from the chapel, because her daughter would stop it; and her daughter, who is nobody's fool, has chosen to believe her. Each is protecting the other from a thing both know. Ends: he keeps Ivy's confidence; tells Ellis; or lets Ivy know that he knows, and is asked in. It is the one story in the game that stays between three people.

3. One way (Karen Grummitt). Light. The ferry's ticket clerk wants a car at five on Thursday, with her case, and not a word to Mrs Rennison, whom she owes a fortnight. The case is a case, a wardrobe and a budgerigar. A cab with a wardrobe roped to the roof must pass Sylvia Rennison's front step, where Sylvia sits in her slippers from four. Turn: Karen does not get on the boat. She stands at the rail she sells tickets for and cannot do it. Ends: he drives it all back, past the step again, and Sylvia says only "Friday means Friday"; or pays the fortnight and Karen goes; or tells Sylvia. The town keeps: the wardrobe, twice. And Karen is the clerk who is on, or not, the night two strangers come off the last ferry.

4. The umbrella (Alison Sedman). Three beats across the acts: her fare to the row and the umbrella left; an evening on the quay wall with two of number nine, when she reads his Ledger over his shoulder and laughs out loud at the ham, the first time anyone in the Hook has found him funny; and her choice in scene 17. Turn: the person bought in this story is not the one she is investigating. It is her. Ends: she takes the desk, and the paper runs the row as an anniversary and nothing more; she keeps the row and loses the desk; or she asks him for the truth, and he gives it, and she holds it until he says. Every ending says where she is.

## 13. The scene in the first half hour a player retells

The carry, minute nine, kept as written: three routes with honest costs (the lit quay, the dark yard and lane, the cab with the firm's name on its roof), a constable who can turn round, and next morning Ada asking what he was doing in her alley and Darren saying it to his face.

If the constable takes the envelope. He calls out; Tom stands; the constable opens it. Small packets, a name pencilled on each. He is honest, so he takes it to the station, and Ellis has it by morning. Nobody comes for Tom with a hammer. Sheila, at the count: "Twenty people over the water are short this week. You don't know what that means yet." And in Mickey's debt book, under the five names, she writes a sixth in her own hand: "Nowak, T. £80." A failed carry is the fastest way in the game to learn what the envelope is.

The second retold scene is lesson one, on the first Sunday: a fifty-eight-year-old docker, a bollard, and the player's hand on the brake.

## 14. Humour and the mix of tones

Grounded noir carries the main line. The comedy is dry, local and attached to people, with seaside-postcard cheek used sparingly. With no drink in the game, the pub's jobs are shared out.

- The radio is the bar. Pauline's commentary goes to every cab at once. "Car one, the chapel, and the lady says not the way you went last time." "Kevin, wherever you are, you are not in Copper Row, because I can hear the gulls." Her tally of Terry's words is on the office wall. The fifth word comes when Tom runs the radio on her Saturday, and Terry, asked for his position for the fourth time, says five: "Tell Pauline it don't count."
- Tom's Ledger is the straight man.
- Five set pieces built for laughing, spread so that no stretch is without one: lesson one and lesson two (the first movement); Pauline's hour and the two strangers in car coats, with Shiner under orders not to speak (the second, where the game most needs them); Mr Reese's ride-along with the crabs (the last act). Karen's wardrobe floats.
- Darren is the clown with a cost: forty radios that pick up one station, sold from the front seat of a cab, and then a coat that does not fit him.

The darkest scenes (the effigy, Tannery Row, the Widow's straight answer, the third lesson, Sheila's kitchen) are played quiet and never joked across.

## 16. How play carries it, and the budget

- Hands first. Every scene is a fare, a carry, a round, a tail, a break-in, a fight, a drive, a lesson, a count or a planned job, with talk where the consequence lands. The last act is one day of driven doors, a counter and a car.
- Nothing is on rails. Scenes fire on states; the calendar's days and the letters' dates belong to the town. Every turn of the truth can be found early: the third portion can be followed in week one; the books are on the shelf from the walk-round; the Widow answers a straight question from the gate at six onward.
- No game over. A lost envelope is a debt. A lost fight is Grace's kitchen. A refused licence is a harder game. Tom cannot die; a car hurts and never kills; nobody is killed by the story.
- Guns are rare and late and come only through people: none is needed by this story, and the dial moves only if the player answers Danny with more than a window.
- No drink, no gambling, no children, anywhere.
- What is asked of the systems is plain: written lines chosen by what the town's memory holds (the hearing, the Friday doors); one prompt with two answers (the offers); a typed or picked question (what do you want); a fixed passenger (the lessons, the ride-along, the widows); a handbrake.

The budget, kept as it was written.
