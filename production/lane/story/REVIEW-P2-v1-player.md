# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 1: a player's review

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7 out of 10. There is one friendship in here that could be talked about for years. Around it is a middle that has not been designed yet, a climax that comes down to a pencil on a form, and a theme that keeps getting said out loud.

1. Tom: 7. The want (his own name, said by someone who means it) and the fear (the packed suitcase) are clear, and taking or paying for every offer is a clever way to make a silent man's flaw playable. But he is mostly a pair of hands and a dry notebook. The Ledger lines are funny, though there are not enough of them to make him somebody on their own.

2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Pauline, Terry, Ada and Reese all sound like real people from a real street. Kevin, Julie and June are close to being functions.

3. The antagonists: 6. The Widow is excellent: she never lies, never threatens, keeps every deal and is partly right. Danny ends up admiring Tom and Agar is mostly letters, which is exactly the GTA V complaint about villains thinner than the leads.

4. The central mystery: 8. Two lines in a fare book, a wing done twice, a coat with no sleeves and a third portion bought every Friday: it is fair and it is found by play. The truth (an accident, a rescue, a man who drove away before he went back) really does change the earlier lessons. It leans on one big coincidence.

5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The carry at minute nine, with a constable who can turn round, is a proper opening hook. Lesson one at the bollard is the second scene people will retell, and it earns that.

6. Structure and pace: 6. The three movements of Act II (the Seat, the Empty Seat, Two in the Car) are a strong shape. But nine and a half hours carry eighteen scenes, and the bible never says what the player does with his hands in between.

7. The middle: 5. "Days are fares, dusk is a job, night is the count" is a loop, not a middle. The Empty Seat is meant to ache for three hours, and an aching stretch with no named jobs is exactly where KCD2 players started saying the game dragged.

8. Surprise against cliché: 7. It dodges the hidden boss, the guilty uncle and the crooked landlord. Having the player assume blackmail and then learning it was never there is clever. On the pad, though, "she never said she would tell" may land as a shrug rather than a punch.

9. The endings: 8. The verdict being which seat Ron sits in is a superb idea. So is the last input being a handbrake the player can leave alone. The Straight Life's "Ron drove" ending is the best thing in the bible. The three names at the counter are still a menu, whatever the bible calls them.

10. The side stories: 7. The pawned watch and Ivy Leadley's four pounds are proper stories with turns. But only four are grown, and three of them come back round to Ron or the fire.

11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline's Saturday, the two strangers with Shiner under orders not to speak, and the crabs on the ride-along are proper set pieces. Some of the humour is only lines on paper, and the noir side has very little menace.

12. Story and play feeding each other: 7. The handbrake, the passenger seat, offers you can take or pay for, and planning the answer job on the board are real joins between story and play. Act III, though, is a phone box, a drive round doors, asking people for things and signing a form. That is thin for a game played with the hands.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The middle has not been designed. What is wrong: Act II is nine and a half hours, but its jobs between scenes are summed up in one sentence ("dusk is a job: something to win or hold"). Where: section 7, "What a week is, between the scenes", and above all the Empty Seat (scenes 8 to 13). Section 11, the wind-down road, is thinner still. Why a player feels it: at hour eight I would be doing my fourth fare and my third count of the week, waiting for a scene to fire on some state I cannot see. Fix: name eight to ten dusk jobs and tie each to a move by the Widow, Danny or Agar, so that no two weeks give the same job. Give the wind-down road two or three jobs of the same weight. Set a rule that no gap between scenes is longer than about twenty-five minutes of play.

2. The theme is said out loud too often. What is wrong: "Nobody ever asked me what I wanted" comes from Kevin, then Alison ("He asked me what I wanted"), then the Widow's "He never asked what it was worth" and "He never asked them either". Ron says "You never ask for owt" twice, and Ada and Sheila both make speeches about not asking. Where: scenes 3, 13, 14, 15 and 17, Sheila's kitchen, and the Straight Life ending. Why a player feels it: by the third time I would hear the writer, not the people. Fix: keep Kevin's line on the rank and Ron's "You never ask for owt" with its pay-off "You've just done it again". Cut the line from Alison's scene and from one of the Widow's two. Let the rest show it through behaviour, such as Ron climbing into the back seat without a word.

3. The climax happens at a counter, and the last day is errands. What is wrong: the biggest decisions in the game are a pencil, a form and three names. The Friday before it is one phone box, ringing people and driving to their doors. Where: section 8, Friday and five o'clock. Why a player feels it: if the last high point is paperwork, a GTA or RDR2 player will feel it go soft just when it should tighten. Fix: keep the inspection, since the frame asks for it, but make Friday a contest. Danny's lads hold the rank and the routes. Agar's agents come with a padlock at noon, and someone has to stay on the door. Getting Tapper and Shiner out from under the Widow's eye is a job done by hand, not a phone call. Each door the player reaches should cost him one he cannot reach in time. The form stays, signed by a man who fought his way to the counter.

4. Only one opponent has teeth. What is wrong: Danny's own scene ends with "That's the best thing anybody's done to me", and his last word is "Fair enough". Agar is a run of dated letters, one walk in the rain and a telephone call made off screen. Where: section 5, scene 10, scene 16, scene 18. There is also a slip in scene 5: Tom's line is that he lays no hands on a woman, yet the player can nudge his bonnet towards the Widow until she steps aside. Why a player feels it: there is not a moment in sixteen hours where I would be afraid for anybody. The Widow's quiet menace needs something louder beside it. Fix: give Danny a second turn that costs something, such as his lads taking car two, the evidence car, off the forecourt on the anniversary week, so it has to be got back before Ellis sees the wing. Give Agar one scene in person in the first movement, perhaps walking the yard with a surveyor while Tom is in it, so his calendar has a face. Cut the bonnet-edging option, or make it a press the game refuses to offer, so his line holds.

5. The mystery rests on one coincidence and a quiet reveal. What is wrong: Mickey's heart gives out at his desk at ten to eleven, on the very night of the job, with the men already waiting. The answer to "why did Ron sell himself" is that he was never actually threatened. Where: section 10, points 2 and 6; scene 14. Why a player feels it: a heart attack at that exact minute feels written. A reveal whose content is that nothing happened can deflate rather than turn, unless the player has already seen what the Widow's silence is worth. Fix: plant Mickey's heart early. June can say in Act I that his first turn came "last back end", and Father Walsh can mention his first spell on the ward. Before scene 14, let the player see the Widow keep someone else's secret when telling would have paid her, for example something about Darren or Kevin she could use and does not. Then "I'd as soon talk to the tide" is proven before it is said, and Ron's year of silence hurts instead of shrugging.

## 3. Would I single this story out to a friend?

No, not yet. If I did, it would be for Ron: the passenger seat, the buildings named by what they used to be, eleven miles an hour, and a story he finishes for the first time at the bollard with my hand on the brake. That relationship is close to Henry and Hans. But I could not promise a friend that the middle holds, and I would have to warn him that the big finish is a form. Fix the middle and put real pressure on the last day, and this becomes a yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron, the seat and the handbrake. Lesson one, lesson two and the third lesson down the row a year to the day, and the last drive where Ron can brake for himself, are the heart of the game.

2. The fare book as alibi and noose. "22.50. Car one. Office to Infirmary. One. No charge." beside "Night. Car two. Out and back. Eleven miles." This is a mystery solved with the hands by laying sheets side by side, paid off by a mild man with a propelling pencil. And Sheila's "15.10. Ferry landing. One. No charge.", echoed in the Suitcase ending, is quietly devastating.

3. The Widow as written. An opponent who never lies, never threatens, keeps every deal and is partly right is rare in any game. Her straight answer in the passenger seat, and the two widows in the back of the cab with the meter running, are scenes I would remember.
