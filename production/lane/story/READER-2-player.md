# LEDGER: a player's read of the story

Read on 20 November 2026, from STORY-TREATMENT.md as it stands, with the KCD2 review figures from 05-players-basics.md in mind. Written as a player who has finished GTA V, RDR2 and KCD2 and plays these games for the people in them.

The short version: the first week is the best thing here, and it is properly good. After that the treatment is a shape, not a story, and I would drift off somewhere in week three.

## 1. The first hour, as I imagine playing it

Minute 0, the ferry. Grey sky, one suitcase, people looking. A good quiet start with no cutscene to sit through. But Tom has a letter in his pocket and the treatment never says what it is. If I can read it and it's a solicitor's form letter, that's a wasted hook. If it's in Mickey's hand, even one line, I have a reason to walk up the quay before anyone has spoken to me.

Minute 1:30, Quay Street. Five people in fifteen seconds, each with "You'll be Mickey's nephew, then." I'd remember Ron ("boss") and Darren. June would slide straight past me, because "wanting nothing from the office" isn't a character, it's an absence.

Minute 1:45, Sheila. Hooked. "Come on, I'll show you round before you start looking lost" and "Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule, and it's mine now" tell me who she is in two lines. She's the best character in the file and she's on screen before minute two.

Minute 2:30, the first fare. Pauline giving directions by landmarks is lovely period flavour. But the drive goes to the priest's house and the priest has no story. The first passenger of the game should say one thing about Mickey that I'm still thinking about at minute ten. As written it's a driving lesson.

Minute 4:30, the locked door. "That's Mickey's office. It's been locked since he died, and I've the key. Not today." A great promise; I want in immediately. Then the money, and here I'd notice something blunt: Tom has £23.50 and Friday's rent is £18. I can already pay it. The "from having nothing" squeeze is fake unless something takes money off me or the rent is more than what's in my pocket. The night's cut should be exactly what makes the rent possible. That's the whole pull of crime in one sum, and right now the sum doesn't work.

Minute 5:30, Darren's £22. A small, clean first choice, and "somebody sees" is the right note.

Minute 7, Ron and the coat. This is where I'm properly in. "If you go tonight, boss, wear that. In the dark nobody looks twice at a coat." Never saying what's in the envelope is exactly right. Seven minutes to the first real crime choice is quicker than any of the three games I'm comparing it with.

Minute 9, the carry. If the constable really can turn round, the yard really is slow and dark, and the cab really has the firm's name on the roof, this is a moment I'd replay. Lit quay, dark yard or cab is a genuine choice with a cost for each. One worry: on my second go I will get searched on purpose to see what happens, and the treatment admits nobody knows what happens if the constable takes the envelope. That has to be written before anything in Act II.

Minute 11:30, the landing. "Mickey's?" "Right. Wednesday, same again." Spare and creepy. Perfect for now. By week four I'll want a face.

Minute 13, Tuesday morning. Darren says it to my face: "Heard you did Mickey's run." This is the moment the game proves it's watching me. KCD2 does this well and its critics rate it highest of anything. Second best moment of the hour.

Minutes 15 to 45, Tuesday to Thursday. Dense and mostly good. Lee standing on the quay with people stopping to watch is fine. Julie and her £9, mercy or shame, is the first time I'd feel something about a debtor. The cold-store packing shift worries me: if it's a minigame I'll resent it by the second shift; if it's one line and a fade, fine. Alison in the cab asking about the fire is a good scene that can't pay off yet, because the fire is empty.

Minute 39, Wednesday night. This is the one I'd tell a friend about. Ron brings the second envelope at eight. Ada's pot is on at nine: "I don't ask twice, mind." I sit with an old widow over tea with the envelope in my coat, watching the clock, slip out at half ten for the landing, and she sees from her window which way I go. That's a real choice between my two lives, with a witness, at night, in my own hands. It's GTA-grade retelling material without a gun in it.

Minute 45 onwards, Thursday. Somebody calls me "Nowak" for the first time. Ellis is on the street if I've been loud. Two dockers wait by car two. Rent tomorrow. The escalation is right.

One more thing about the first hour. Tom's lines are typed. So in the first ten minutes I will type "How did Mickey die?" to Sheila and "Who's the man at the landing?" to Ron, because that's what anyone would ask. The ruling says nobody doubts the heart. Fine, but then Sheila needs an answer worth hearing, and the treatment also has her hiding that she saw Ron arguing with a stranger two nights before. In a typing game the player pokes the obvious mystery straight away. Every obvious question needs an answer that is itself interesting, or the spell breaks in the first hour.

Not hooked: the priest fare, June, the packing shift, the rent that doesn't bite. Hooked: Sheila, the coat, the carry, Darren saying it back, Ada's tea.

## 2. Hour by hour through the acts

Hours 0 to 2, Act I. As above, the best stretch of the game. The week ends with Sheila coming in on her day off: "So which is it going to be?" That's a strong act break. But Friday and Saturday have nothing in them, and they sit right before the best question in the act. Two blank days is where a new player wanders off to the boxing club and forgets what they were doing.

Hours 2 to 5, the firm becomes mine. A third car, a first recruit, and, if Sheila trusts me, the locked office and Mickey's real book. That door has been teasing me since minute four, so it's the first big payoff. The problem: the book holds one page tying the fire to the outfit, and the fire has no content. So the reward for two hours of earning Sheila's trust is a page about something nobody has written. If that page is thin I'll feel what you feel when a locked chest has three coins in it.

Hours 5 to 9, the rivals arrive. Noticed, a visit, pressure. Some of this is good on paper. The Widow first hearing of me over my own cab radio, which anyone can listen to, is a great idea. Ellis searching the office with a night's notice is the best Act II scene the treatment has, even as one line: an evening hiding the real book while the street watches is a set piece. Agar's paper war could be brilliant if it's personal, a courteous old man taking my licence away across a table, and dreadful if it's a letter in a menu.

Hours 9 to 14, the sag. This is where I'd stop caring. Three rivals climbing the same five-rung ladder, every pressure answered pay, defy or counter, nine or more times over, with fares and the nightly count in between. That's a management sim with a crime coat on. In the KCD2 reviews sampled, story and characters were the most praised thing (about a third of reviews), while "boring, nothing to do for minutes" (about one in nine) and "the same loop over and over" (about one in fifteen) are exactly what this stretch walks into. Beats that "fire on what has happened, never on dates" also means a slow player gets fewer story beats, which is backwards. RDR2's middle works because every few hours someone in camp has a mission that is about them. Here nobody does; the treatment itself says Act II has not a single scene.

Worse: if I chose "wind it down" on Sunday, nothing says what Act II is for me. That's eleven to fourteen hours of nothing for the player who took the decent road. I'd quit.

Hours 14 to 16, the sit-down, with "one rival head", unnamed, no words. If it's whichever rival I pushed hardest, it needs writing three ways. The proposal that the men behind the landing hand me their end of the Hook and fade, so I become what Mickey was, is the best idea for Act II's shape. It needs a scene with a face in it.

Hours 16 to 19, Act III. The courteous letter wakes me back up. A VAT inspector bringing one item each morning is the freshest final boss I've seen in a crime game: I built an empire of night money through a minicab fare book, and what brings it down is a polite man from Customs and Excise. That's Al Capone's tax bill done properly British. Ellis's trade (give her a rival, and the street learns who talks) is a good, ugly choice. The last day, ringing round to see who picks up, is a beautiful idea: the campaign I played answers the phone. But the inspector has no name, no items and no lines, and the crewman who asks to take over has no name. Right now it's a frame. And for the player who never built a business, the treatment admits the inspection changes nothing: the climax is scenery.

Characters I'd care about: Sheila above everyone. Ron, if the yard argument goes somewhere. Ada, for one night. The Widow, on one line ("I buried a husband and kept his business. Draw your own conclusions"). Alison, if her two scenes ever exist, because "she never burns a source, but lies she finds out become the story" is a romance with teeth. Ellis, because asking while the case is answerable and telling once it isn't is a lovely way to show a copper closing in.

Characters I'd forget: June (nothing to hold), Father Walsh (lines, no story), Philip Danby (doesn't exist yet), Garbutt (a function, not a man), Cammack (the loud young crew boss is in every crime game ever made; "the recklessness a costume over calculation" is in the card but in no scene), the drivers Kevin and Terry, and the outfit. Darren I'd like for a week and then miss, because his story stops. Same with Ada.

The endings, and whether I'd want to see each:
- The Kingdom: I'd get this first time, because it's what playing a crime game well gives you. "Civil and no more" is a nice cold note. But it's a state, not a scene: a short walk round town and credits. After RDR2's final chapters, that will feel as if the game forgot to end.
- The Straight Life: only if the wind-down road has a story. Handing the firm on and watching three days to see if it holds is a good idea if I care who takes it. Right now the successor is nobody.
- Burn Both: no. It's the fail state, "what doing nothing produces". I'd see it by accident and reload, or watch it on YouTube.
- The rare better Kingdom: I'd love to earn it, but not enough to replay fifteen hours whose first two are fixed script.

Would it stay with me? Not as written. What stays after RDR2 is Arthur; after GTA V, Trevor and the three of them in one room; after KCD2, Henry and Hans. LEDGER's nearest pair is Sheila and Tom, and Tom is a blank. I'd remember the coat, the tea and the inspector's letter. I wouldn't remember who Tom was, because the treatment says itself that nobody knows.

## 3. Scores

- Hook: 8. The envelope at minute seven, the coat, the carry and Ada's tea make one of the tightest openings I've read; the fake rent squeeze and the empty priest fare cost the last points.
- Characters: 5. Sheila, Ron, Ada and the Widow are alive on the page; nearly everyone else is a function or stops after week one.
- Tom: 3. No want, no fear, no flaw, no voice, and every ending says where he stands rather than who he became; the naming ladder (new owner, Nowak, Tom, Tommy) is the only arc he has.
- Villains and threat: 5. The Widow and Agar have good lines and Ellis has a great manner, but none of them has a scene, Cammack is stock, and the outfit has no face.
- The middle: 3. Eleven to fourteen hours without one written scene, the same ladder three times, and nothing at all for the player who winds the firm down.
- Surprises: 2. There isn't one: the obvious mystery, Mickey's death, is ruled closed, and the fire everything hangs from is empty.
- The endings: 4. The VAT inspector climax is excellent; the endings themselves have no last scenes, and one road's climax is scenery.
- Side stories: 6. Fares with a wrinkle, debts that each teach something, a retired doctor who stitches and remembers, a stranger who returns a favour: KCD2's texture done small. KCD2 reviewers praised side quests that are whole stories, not errands (about one in seven), and three to five steps is on the short side for that. Scored on promise; the 32 drafts weren't in front of me.
- Humour and tone: 6. Grey port, the dock labour scheme just gone, dry lines that land; but nothing is actually funny yet, and with no pub there's no obvious home for banter. The cab radio, drivers ribbing each other with anyone listening, is the obvious place for it.
- Wanting to keep playing: 5. I'd tear through week one and the first look inside Mickey's office, then drift somewhere in week three.

Overall: 5 out of 10 as the design stands. The first week on its own is an 8.

## 4. Verdict

LEDGER's first week beats the openings of RDR2 and KCD2: an envelope, a coat and a widow's tea put me in the crime within seven minutes, and a VAT inspector is a final boss none of the three would dare. But GTA V had three men who wanted things, RDR2 had Arthur changing and KCD2 had Henry growing up, while LEDGER has a blank man walking through eleven unwritten hours of rivals climbing a ladder. As it stands I'd finish week one, tell a mate about Ada's window, and never see an ending.

## 5. The five things that would most make me care more

1. Give Tom a want, a line he won't cross, and a change.
- Use the letter in his pocket. Make it Mickey's, in his own hand, three lines written before he died: why him, a nephew Mickey never met, and one thing Mickey asks of him. Wanting to understand that letter is a want that never touches his past.
- Make his one rule canon (no hands on a woman, anyone of sixty or over, or the priest) and test it once in Act II and once in Act III, where keeping it costs something real: Rita Welburn holding the thing he needs, or Ada as the only witness against him. A line he won't cross is character.
- Or make the blankness the point, A Fistful of Dollars style: the stranger who plays three gangs off against each other. That only works if the rivals are louder, the plot turns, and the last scene gives the town's one-word verdict on him.

2. Decide what happened at the warehouse and who the outfit is, and make it bite Tom.
- A night watchman died in the fire, and Mickey's envelopes paid for the silence. Tom has been carrying that money since minute seven, and he learns it from Mickey's real book. That's the mid-game gut punch the story lacks.
- Tie it to the fare book. One of Mickey's cars drove the men to the warehouse and back that night, and because every fare goes in the book, the real book holds that fare. The founding mystery, Mickey's rule and the inspector's last item become one story.
- Give the outfit a face Tom already knows by day: the retired doctor who stitches without asking, or Brenda Laverack, his dawn fare to the fish dock. The surprise is that he's been driving and chatting to the other side for weeks.

3. Write Act II as scenes, make it shorter, and give the wind-down road a story.
- One written evening per rival that changes the board: the Widow's men stopping Kevin at the dock gate while Tom hears it on the radio; Agar's lunch where the licence goes across the tablecloth, apologised for; Cammack's lot setting car two alight under Ada's window.
- Name the guaranteed collision now: say, the evening Alison comes up to the flat for supper and Ron knocks with blood on his sleeve.
- Cut to four game weeks with a fixed midpoint (the office search with a night's notice, hiding the real book), let the rivals turn on each other so it isn't one ladder three times, and write the wind-down player's Act II: keeping a clean firm alive while three outfits lean on it is a story too.

4. Pay off what week one sets up, or cut it.
- Ron and the stranger in the yard. Keep the heart as ruled, but make the stranger the Widow's man, so her poaching Ron in Act II is the payoff and Tom must decide whether Ron sold Mickey before trusting him in a fight. Or the stranger came to tell Mickey his arrangement was finished, and the strain did the rest. Or cut it; in a typing game I'll ask about it in the first hour.
- Give Ada, Darren and June one more beat each: Ada's window as Ellis's witness or Tom's alibi in Act III; Darren beaten in Act II for something he said about Tom, the loss that makes the rivals personal; June as the one who takes the firm in the Straight Life.
- Commit to Alison and write her two scenes before anything else. Park Philip Danby until there's time.

5. Give each ending a last scene with one person in it, and give Act III a face.
- Last scenes. The Kingdom: Sheila gives him the key to Mickey's office for good, and the name she uses is the verdict, "Mr Nowak" or "Tom". The Straight Life: the successor's first night, Pauline calling cars for someone else, Tom riding in the back as a fare. Burn Both: ten past ten at the landing, nobody comes, Ellis's car at the end of the quay.
- Name the inspector and give him a habit and three good lines: a mild man with a flask of tea and a bad knee who never raises his voice and never misses a figure.
- Give the player who never built a business his own Act III: the outfit wants Mickey's arrangement back from the successor, or the inspector reads the successor's books and Tom has to stand behind them.

## 6. The three things I'd keep exactly

1. The first week's two nights. Ron and the coat ("In the dark nobody looks twice at a coat"), the envelope never explained, the carry past the constable, "Mickey's?" at the landing, Darren saying it back next morning, and Ada's tea on the night of the second envelope with her window watching. Don't add a cutscene, don't explain the envelope, don't move the timing.
2. Sheila Dunn as written: "New management", the fare book rule, the locked door ("Not today"), keeping his name back until she trusts him, and "So which is it going to be?" on her day off. The first time she says "Tom" should be the biggest moment in the game, and the design already has the bones of it.
3. The VAT inspector as the last act: the courteous letter, one item each morning, "I'll want the other book, Mr Nowak", and the last day's calls where whoever picks up is the campaign you played. A crime story that ends on a tax inspection is the most original idea in the file and very British. Don't swap it for a shootout.
