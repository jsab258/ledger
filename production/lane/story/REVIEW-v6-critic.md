# LEDGER, story bible version 6: a critic's review

## 1. Scores

**Overall: 7**

1. **Tom: 8.** The flaw is the best thing about him because it is played: every gift is one press, take or pay, and the hearing and the last Friday add up the answers. His Ledger voice ("The ham had a seat") makes him somebody without a word spoken. What holds him back is that "nothing marks which is which" makes the gift-or-hook lesson hard to learn before the final bill arrives.
2. **The cast: 7.** Sheila, Ron, Terry and Pauline are written for the hands and the ear, and Frank Cammack does a great deal with a few lines. But the bible says "fewer people, met more often" and then names more than forty, and too many of them hide a link to the fire or the club.
3. **The antagonists: 7.** Agar is the best of them: the glass door, the bank man saying the date three times, the lamp in the rain. Danny's arc, where a page takes away the one true thing he had, is genuinely sad. The Widow mostly works as a list of offers, and Ada, the real enemy after the midpoint, never gets a final-act scene where she stands to lose anything except a conversation.
4. **The central mystery: 7.** It has a real truth that changes earlier scenes (the tea, the letter, "new management"), and Sheila's own hand on the line for 18 November is a fine last turn. It is over-built, though: three layers of who-really-did-it, a coincidence in every household, and nearly every clue is a document someone happens to have kept.
5. **The opening and its retold scene: 8.** The first night is strong, the sums bite (rent, wages, a frozen account, and one carry is exactly the gap), and the failed carry ending with "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book is a scene people would tell their friends about. Act I is rich, but two scripted hours with this many introductions is near the limit for "slow opening".
6. **Structure and pace: 6.** The act shapes and the calendar dates are sound, and the rivals peak in turn. But the midpoint evening holds five big beats, and the last week has something like twelve, so the two places that matter most are the most crowded.
7. **The middle: 7.** Twenty scenes, two chases, dated pressure that pulls a slow player forward, and a Friday round that changes a door each week: much better than most open-world middles. The straight road's middle leans on forms, trustees and a bank account, and will feel like civic paperwork next to the keeper's road.
8. **Surprise against cliché: 6.** The sweet old widow who is secretly the boss, and the kindly dead benefactor who turns out to be the architect, are both familiar moves, and the bible signposts both. Sheila writing the line, Ron as the importer's collector, June filling three envelopes from her wage, and Terry standing at the window are the real surprises.
9. **The endings: 8.** Each one is earned by state, not picked from a menu, and has a last image that sticks: "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four"; "Ferry landing. One. No charge." Ron in a vehicle as the verdict on the friendship is excellent. The Kingdom's plain turn turns into a list of what happened to everyone.
10. **The side stories: 8.** Ivy and her daughter protecting each other from a thing both know is the most grown-up writing in the bible, Ron pawning the watch for the flowers is quietly devastating, and Karen's wardrobe going past the step twice is a real set piece. They touch the main line without being needed by it, which is exactly the job.
11. **Tone and humour: 7.** Pauline as the bar and the chorus works, and there are lines that are actually funny ("They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in"). Too much of the comedy is in single lines, and the darkness after the Fifth runs for long stretches with only the Ledger to lighten it.
12. **Story and play feeding each other: 8.** The lessons, the carry, the stakeout, the lock-up planned like a job, the search carry, the boot and the phone box all make the story something done with the hands. Talk is where consequences land, and nothing is on rails. The final Friday as timed fetch-and-carry is the weakest piece of this.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The mystery is over-built and the world is too small.**
What is wrong: almost every person turns out to be tied to the fire or the club by something hidden. Maureen is Ada's daughter-in-law; Ellis's mother is on the list; Alison lodges above Ada; Frank was paid secretly by Mickey; June makes up three envelopes; Ron collected the importer's rent, stood at Alan's drowning and pawned the watch; Coultas is one of the fire's men; the ironmonger writes everything down; Hal's camcorder catches the window and the car. Where: section 0 items 1, 3, 5 and 12; section 7 scenes 8, 11, 12 and 16; section 10. Why it hurts: past a certain point each new link stops being a revelation and starts feeling like the author filling in a chart. A reviewer will call it neat rather than true, and the paper trail (minute book, rent card, ironmonger's book, fare book, Mickey's page, Ron's mileage book) makes the dig feel more like reading than finding things out. Fix: keep the links that earn a scene and cut two or three that only add a thread. Ron keeps the drowning and the importer's rent (that is the "believe your friend" beat). Give the watch to someone else, or make it plain grief. Take Ellis's mother off the list, and let her drive for the fire because it happened two streets from where she grew up. Let Alison lodge somewhere else, so Ada's line "I like to know where the paper sleeps" becomes something Ada arranged and the player can find out. Replace the ironmonger's book with something the player sees with his own eyes: Darren's hands smelling of paraffin in the cab on the 2nd.

**2. The last week is overloaded and its climax is the thinnest thing in it.**
What is wrong: Act III packs in Reese, the ride-along, Agar under the lamp, the yard fight, the old books, Alison's proof, Ellis's trade, the boot, Sheila on the wall, the cut page, the phone box run, the rival on the pavement and Ada's table, in three to four hours. The emotional peak (Sheila on the wall) is Thursday night. Friday, the climax, is a run of written calls that open errands, and the rival stand-off is written as "settled there, with what Tom knows or with his hands or by walking round". Where: section 8, "The week" and Friday. Why it hurts: BAR's own record says a last act that hurries gets punished, and this is the GTA V failure in a different coat. The player who has lived with these people for fifteen hours ends on errands against a clock, and the one antagonist the whole second half has built up, Ada, is met only at a tea table after the verdict. Fix: move Agar in the rain out of the week, to the night after Garbutt's table, so it closes the middle. Write each rival's Friday stand-off as a proper short scene with its own choice. Make the phone box run three errands, not an open count, and send the last errand to Ada's door before five: what Tom says to her decides whether she backs Sheila's account or Mickey's lie in front of Reese. That puts the main antagonist in the climax, with something to lose, and keeps the verdict as the last word.

**3. The midpoint reveal is familiar and crammed into one evening.**
What is wrong: the book, the supper, the burned car, the tallyman at Ada's window and the second tea all happen on the night of the Fifth. The widow at the window who turns out to be the head of the outfit is a known twist, and the bible signposts it from week one: "what were you doing in my alley", "why twenty and not twenty-one", the door shut on the answer, the tea as an "interview". Where: section 6 Friday; section 7, "The midpoint". Why it hurts: genre-savvy players will guess Ada by the first weekend, and the BAR warning about the ending guessed in the first hour applies just as much to a midpoint. Five beats in one evening also means none of them gets room. Fix: open Mickey's office on the Saturday before, so the player sits with "The fire settled what the rent couldn't" for two days of play. Keep the fire, the window and the second tea together on the Fifth. Plant one honest bit of misdirection for players who are paying attention: a scene where Cyril gives orders to the Widow's men on the quay with Ada nowhere near, so the tallyman looks like he really is the boss. Then reward the player who has been watching the window: he can have seen Cyril go into Ada's front door on ask nights, and can turn up at the second tea already knowing.

**4. The Widow and Garbutt's table run on paper, not scenes.**
What is wrong: the Widow's threat is a run of priced offers, each "put once", and her two good scenes (the gate at six, the two widows) put her in the back of a car. Garbutt's table, the end of the act, is the player trading pages between three people over what the documents mean. Where: section 5, the Widow; section 7, scenes 6, 15 and 20. Why it hurts: a reviewer remembers antagonists by what they did in a room, and BAR asks for "different ways of coming at him". Offers are a way of coming at him, but only one, and a table of swapped documents is hard to follow and has no hands in it. Fix: give the Widow one scene where she does something herself. On the night of the stoppage, she is at the cold-store road with her men, and walks out into the headlights to stand in front of the bonnet. Make Garbutt's table one physical bargain: each rival has brought one thing that belongs to Tom (Kevin's copied sheets, the yard padlock key, Darren's marker), and the trade is objects on a table, the pages in his coat.

**5. The straight road reads as a civic checklist, and the gift-or-hook lesson comes too late to learn.**
What is wrong: the straight middle is forms, three trustees, a hall, money in a bank and a list, and its turning point rests on one woman walking out of a meeting. Separately, the flaw system says the player learns "the difference between a gift and a reason" by getting it wrong, but the hooks are mostly called in on the last Friday, when he cannot get it wrong again. Where: section 7, the straight ladder and scenes 3 (S), 9 (S) and 14 (S); section 3, "The flaw is played". Why it hurts: BAR names the road of the player who refuses the crime as a place where thinness is punished, and a lesson that only lands at the end is a twist, not something the player learns. Fix: give the straight road a hands set piece of its own: the society's first payment carried door to door on the Friday round, cash in a holdall with the Widow's men and the far bank's people both wanting it to fail. That echoes the first carry and pays it off. For the flaw, call in one hook in each half of Act II (Danny's fifty after the yellow cars, Agar's forgiven rent before the hearing), so the player is billed twice before the last Friday and comes to it knowing the price.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review

No, not yet. It is clearly above the genre average, and three or four scenes would get quoted in a review: the failed carry ending with his own name in the debt book, Ron's third lesson on the dock road, two widows in the back of a minicab with the meter running, and Sheila on the boarding-house wall saying "Nobody reads a fare book". Ron and Tom is the one relationship here that could be remembered the way Henry and Hans are. But the story's two big reveals are moves I have seen before and are signposted, its last act hurries through a dozen beats to end on errands, and the web of hidden connections is so tight that the town starts to feel built rather than lived in. A reviewer would praise the writing and the people and then say the plot tries too hard. Fix the last week and thin the coincidences, and the answer could become yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. **Sheila and the fare book.** "Every fare goes in the book" turns out to be her rule kept against Mickey's word, the line in her small upright hand, the letter she posted to put a reader in the chair, and her withholding of his name until "Night, Tom". This is the spine of the story and its best idea.
2. **Ron in the passenger seat.** A man who cannot drive, the map learned as a list of things that shut, rain by four, three lessons that stop being funny, the Bishop story finished at last, and the seat he is in at the end as the verdict. Protect every minute of it.
3. **Take it or pay.** Gifts and hooks that look alike, tallied by the town and called in at the hearing and on the last Friday. It turns a character flaw into play and makes the theme, that a name is a debt, something the player lives through rather than is told.
