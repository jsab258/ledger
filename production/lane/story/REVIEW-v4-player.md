# LEDGER, story bible v4: a player's review

## 1. Scores

**Overall: 7**

1. **Tom: 7.** The Ledger gives an unvoiced man a real voice ("The ham had a seat"), and the suitcase under the bed is a fear you can press a button on, which is clever. But his flaw is played as a run of take-or-pay prompts, and his change shows up mostly in the last two lines of each ending, not in the scenes along the way.
2. **The cast: 8.** Ron, Sheila, Terry and Pauline's tally, Reese with his flask, Frank Cammack signing first with his left hand: these are people I would talk about. Alison is the weak spot: a crusading reporter whose editor sits on the board she is investigating is a type I have met many times.
3. **The antagonists: 7.** Danny is the best rival: he is beating Tom fairly, he is innocent of the thing everyone blames him for, and his page takes his grudge away instead of healing it. The Widow prices instead of threatening, which is a good idea, but she has few scenes of her own, and once Ada turns out to be the real power she becomes a list of offers.
4. **The central mystery: 7.** It is built properly. Every layer changes what earlier scenes meant, and "That's the last one, Cyril" reads differently the second time. But the final truth arrives by reading two books side by side at a desk, and "the dead uncle who sent for you did it" is a familiar shape. I also never quite believed a man planning a fire would write the run down in his own hand, even with "every fare goes in the book".
5. **The opening and its retold scene: 8.** Day one moves fast: first fare, the badge, the priest's forty-one empty envelopes, Darren's debt, the carry at nine minutes. If the constable takes the envelope and Tom ends the week as "Nowak, T. £82" in his own debt book, that is a failure I would retell happily.
6. **Structure and pace: 7.** The calendar (the hearing, Bonfire Night, Remembrance, the anniversary, the letter) pulls a slow player forward, and the rivals take their peaks in turn, which gives the middle a real shape. But Act II packs twenty-odd scenes into ten to thirteen hours with little said about what fills the hours between them.
7. **The middle: 6.** The Fifth is a superb hinge, and Remembrance Sunday and the two widows in the cab are strong. Around them the keeper's road is thin (a rank, rent from the parade, a lesson to the chandler), and the second half turns into a queue of people confessing to Tom.
8. **Surprise against cliché: 6.** Ada with the tallyman standing cap in hand in her front room surprised me. But I picked Darren for car two the second time he said "I could do the landing", Danny's "That wasn't me" is the innocent-suspect line from a dozen films, and the benefactor as the guilty party is the last-act turn I would bet on.
9. **The endings: 8.** Ada's table in every ending is the right last conversation, and the last images are excellent: "41 out. T.N.", "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four", the Reckoning's "Nowak!", "Ferry landing. One. No charge." What lets them down is that the deciding moment before them is a man reading paperwork.
10. **The side stories: 8.** Ivy and her daughter each protecting the other from a thing both know is the best writing in the bible. Ron pawning the watch for the flowers, Karen's wardrobe going past Sylvia's step twice, and Norman's "nothing moves but me" all have real turns.
11. **Tone and humour: 7.** There are actual jokes: Brenda's crabs ("They don't fill forms in"), Kevin reporting himself at the lights while eating a sandwich outside the window, Pauline's tally, the tallyman's "I'll not hold it against you twice". The dark half is played quiet. But the funny material thins out after the Fifth, so the last six hours are mostly grief and paper.
12. **Story and play feeding each other: 8.** The offers count at the hearing and in the phone box, the passenger seat and the lessons, the lock-up as three different jobs depending on whether you saw Hal's tape: this is story that lives in the hands. Garbutt's table and the two-books evening are where it falls back on menus and reading.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The second half becomes a confession parade.**
What is wrong: from the Fifth on, nearly every turn arrives as someone sitting Tom down and telling him. Ada at the second tea, June in the corridor, Norman at his gate, Ada and Maureen in the cab, the importer at the plot, Garbutt fronted, Agar in the rain, Cyril at the table, Sheila on Friday morning, Les at the landing. Where: section 7, scenes 10 to 17 and the second tea; section 8, Tuesday to Friday. Why a player feels it: BAR.md punishes "long scenes, too much talk", and this is a game where the player acts with his hands. By the fourth I would be pressing to skip. Fix: turn at least four of these into things found or done. Agar brings no speech, just a bundle of Monday interest receipts in Mickey's hand that he wants back. Cyril's last line is cut: the player sees him take the book and turn to the October page. Sheila's cut page is found in a search the player makes (her handbag on the counter, the back of the fare-book drawer), and she speaks one line afterwards.

**2. The middle's hours are unaccounted for, and the keeper's road is thin.**
What is wrong: Act II is ten to thirteen hours. The straight road has a five-rung ladder with names and fights on every rung. The keeper's road has the ferry rank, the parade's rent and the chandler's lesson, and "the firm's rise" is otherwise a phrase. Where: section 7, "The debt", scenes 3K, 9K, 14K. Why a player feels it: this is exactly where KCD2 and RDR2 lose people (BAR.md's complaint about the loop at forty-two hours). The player who takes the business over, the obvious choice for a GTA player, gets the thinner game. Fix: give the keeper's road its own named ladder of five rungs to match the society's, each held by a person who comes back: a third car and Julie Kettlewell; the ferry rank; the launderette's till; the landing (Darren or a stranger); the gate's cab contract. Each rung opens a repeatable kind of job, and each is attacked by one rival on the calendar.

**3. Who burned car two is guessable, so the midpoint's mystery solves itself.**
What is wrong: Darren says "I could do the landing" in week one, again in week two, and again just before the Fifth, and Ada has already shown she finds people reasons. Danny's "That wasn't me" is the line every innocent suspect gets. Hal's tape then only confirms what an attentive player already thinks. Where: sections 4 and 7 (the "In these weeks" note, the morning after the Fifth, scene 12). Why a player feels it: the lock-up is meant to be the game's one planned job, with a real moral trap, and the trap only works if the player might believe Danny did it. Fix: drop Darren's second "landing" line and put a second plausible hand near the car. Kevin is perfect: he is already carrying for the gate on a promise and has a key to car two. Then Darren in the too-big coat is a real turn, not a tick.

**4. The flaw mechanic gives itself away.**
What is wrong: the bible says hooks "look the same" as gifts and the player learns the difference by getting it wrong. But every hook it lists comes from a rival or a fixer (Danny's fifty, the Widow's lift, Agar's rent, Garbutt's read, Ada's tea), and every pure gift comes from a friend. Any player will refuse the man in the car coat and accept the woman in the caff. Where: section 3, "The flaw is played". Why a player feels it: it becomes a karma meter with obvious answers, and Tom's one real inner struggle is reduced to reading who is offering. Fix: cross the lines. Make one rival's gift clean: Danny's fifty is never called in, and he is insulted if Tom pays it back. Make one friend's gift a hook: Sheila's £20 loan, or June's needle, comes back on the last Friday as a favour Tom does not want to do (June asking him to keep her three envelopes going, whatever he decided).

**5. The climax is paperwork.**
What is wrong: the truth lands in an evening of reading two books side by side; the last day is a run to fetch documents to a phone box by five; and Garbutt's table, the end of the middle, is a trade of pages. For a crime story the final act comes down to who holds which sheet of paper. Where: section 8, "The week before: the two books" and "Friday, the run"; section 7, scene 20. Why a player feels it: BAR.md's "exceptional" asks for a mystery that pays off at the climax, and reading a ledger, however clever, is the least physical way to land it. The best idea in Act III, the book in Reese's car boot on Thursday night, comes after the reveal, when it should be the reveal. Fix: move the discovery into Reese's boot. Tom reads the fare book for the first time properly by torchlight in the street outside the boarding house, finds Mickey's squeezed-in line for 18 November, and at the same moment holds the pen that could alter it. The reveal and the temptation land together, with his hands doing something.

## 3. Would I single this story out

No, not yet, though it is closer than most. I would tell a friend about scenes: Ada at her window with the tallyman standing in her front room while the car burns below; Frank Cammack calling "Go on, Tommy" in the third round; Ron driving the dock road at eleven miles an hour and asking to go round again. Ron and Tom could stand beside Henry and Hans, and the passenger seat as the verdict on the friendship is a lovely idea. But I would not yet tell a friend to play it for its story, because the middle on the keeper's road has no clear shape, the second half tells more than it lets me find, and I saw two of the three big turns coming. Fix the confession parade and the thin keeper's road and this becomes a yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. **Ron and the passenger seat.** He cannot drive, so he rides; the three lessons go from a bollard to eleven miles an hour to not funny; and every ending puts him in a vehicle, with which seat he is in as the verdict. It is built entirely for the hands.
2. **The window on the Fifth, and Ada's table at the end.** The tallyman cap in hand beside her chair, "You'd better come in, Tom", and the last Friday where Tom can tell her what Mickey did to her or let her die believing the fire was hers. She is the most memorable person in the bible.
3. **The fare book as alibi and noose, read by Reese.** A mild man who only wants to know what happened, reading aloud "Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak", and the plainest book in the office turning out to be the only one that never lied.
