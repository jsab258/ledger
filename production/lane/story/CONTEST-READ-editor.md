# Contest reading: the script editor

20 November 2026. A fresh Opus reader (a script editor for crime drama and story games), given BAR.md and the three treatments under shuffled labels and nothing else. For this reader: X was CONTEST-A (Twice), Y was CONTEST-B (No Charge), Z was CONTEST-C (The Passenger). The reader never saw the letters A, B, C. The text below is the reader's own.

## 1. Ranking

First: Y ("No Charge"). It has the only turn of the three that really changes what every earlier scene meant, it plays fair down to the pound, and its ending makes Tom's flaw the price of the climax.

Second: Z ("The Passenger"). It has the best relationship and the best last image of the three, but its turn is a familiar noble sacrifice, and its two biggest moves break the opponent's own rule and the frame.

Third: X ("Twice"). Handing the crime to the player as an ordinary fare is a brave, game-native idea, but the treatment warns the player so loudly that the midpoint can be guessed a week ahead, and the mystery is spent in week one.

## 3. Each treatment

X, best things

- The midpoint is a deed, not a revelation. It is one job with three ways in, and the third ("work it out") is pure systems play: fetch Darren, warn Danny, send the men back, or check the building is empty. Sheila writing the line the next morning, so that her honesty records his guilt, is the strongest single image in X.
- The hearing's full public bench, which nobody asked for, is a gift the player never got to refuse. It turns the "take it or pay" thread into a debt before he knows he owes one. Agar's "your uncle's friends light them for nothing, and I buy what is left" is a good bitter twist.

X, worst faults

- The plot depends on a crime nobody would commit this way (section 7, and again on Saturday 15 December). A club whose officer "never does anything himself" books an arson over an open cab radio that anyone with a scanner can hear, on its own account, on the busiest night of the year. It knows the job will be written into a fare book, and it books the next one while a Customs officer is reading those books. Ron's "not in a car with a radio in it" only shows that the treatment knows the radio is a risk.
- The mystery is told rather than uncovered (sections 5 to 7). Cyril tells the whole history at the burnt plot in week one. Then come Ron's "just don't take it", Darren's sleeping bag and five units stopping. A careful player sees the Fifth coming, and the bar punishes exactly that. What is left for Scene 13 (Mickey drove, someone was hurt) is small and easily inferred. There is also a remit fault in Act III, on Thursday. A journey given free carries no VAT, so Reese has no tax reason to care about the Blenkin account, and "what was carried" is not a VAT officer's question. Cyril as an opponent hardly moves.

Y, best things

- The turn itself (section 7, the count and the kitchen). The outfit does not exist and the bookkeeper is its keeper. The nineteen pounds, the wage packet into her bag, the £80 she knows without being told and "Who fills the box?" are all on screen beforehand. The player finds it with his hands: "The ham had a seat. £2" against "Rennison. Account. No charge." It reframes the first night as her con and her test. Agar as a decent man at war with a rumour is the best antagonist idea in any of the three.
- The Hook shares Tom's flaw. Ada's "A club is a thing you're in. A charity is a thing done to you" and the envelopes handed back raise the stakes on the ending. The Friday round driven backwards, with Tom asking at doors, makes the flaw pay off in action. The staged job with Tapper and Shiner (Scene 10) is the best-planned set piece across the three.

Y, worst faults

- The story undoes itself twice (Scene 13). After "there is no outfit" comes "nobody burned the warehouse" (Norman's heater). The second reveal is also muddled: a scorched wall in the watchman's hut does not explain a warehouse fire, so whose heater was where? Together the two take away the town's only real menace, leaving Danny's car fire as nearly all the violence in the game. A second "it was nothing" deflates the first.
- The finale is a whip-round (Act III, Friday). Neighbours fill a counter in time for the deadline, which is It's a Wonderful Life, and the bar punishes cliché. The procedure is wrong as well: no VAT officer in 1990 counts a pile of cash on a shop counter with his pencil and turns suppression into "carelessness" because it arrived by five. There is also a naming fault, in Scene 12 and present in all three treatments. Ada is "Walter Jensen's widow" and Maureen is "Jensen, the Widow", and two Jensen widows sit side by side with nobody remarking on it.

Z, best things

- Ron. The unfinished stories, the lessons at eleven miles an hour, "You never ask for owt", the empty seat after the Fifth, and "I can drive, boss. Near enough" all recast the comic relief as the secret. This is the only one of the three with a friendship people would talk about afterwards. The last input of the game is the handbrake from the passenger seat, which is a final image a review would quote.
- The flaw is turned into a mechanic. The Widow takes people away by asking what they want, and the player's only defence is to ask first (Kevin, Pauline, Terry). She is a lawful opponent who prices, keeps her deals and is often right. The "T. Nowak" box, where the man with no past is the one man with no alibi, is the boldest idea in the three.

Z, worst faults

- The Widow breaks her own law (Scene 14). Her defining trait is that she keeps every deal, and Ron's foremanship was bought with her silence. Telephoning Customs breaks that deal and throws away her hold over him for nothing. The plot needs the call, so the character pays for it. There is also a route fault in Scenes 9 and 13. Ron drove "with a man burning in the back", yet the 1989 line reads "landing to warehouse row and return", with no infirmary. Sheila also writes what a car did with no sheet and no driver.
- The ending breaks the frame (Act III, Friday). "You came to this town in October, Mr Nowak. The player answers, or does not" means someone asks about Tom's past, which the frame forbids. A VAT officer demanding a name for an unpaid journey that he himself says "is not a matter for Customs and Excise", on a form with exactly one box, is a contrivance. And underneath, the friend who seemed to sell out but was really protecting the firm is a turn viewers have seen many times.

## 4. Would I single one out?

Not yet. Y comes closest. Its turn is fair, surprising and changes the meaning of week one, and that alone would get a mention. What it still lacks:

- a relationship as remembered as Henry and Hans. Ron and Sheila serve the mystery but neither carries the game.
- menace that survives the second half, once both fires are explained away.
- a climax that the player's hands win, rather than the town's generosity.

Fix those and it is a 9.

## 5. What Y could take from X and Z without losing its shape

- From Z: Ron as the spine of the friendship. Keep his lessons and unfinished stories, and have "Twenty year I've put my arm in that box" land on a friend rather than an employee. Take Z's "teach Tom to ask" middle, Kevin's "She asked me what I wanted" and Pauline's Saturday. That trains the asking that Y's last Friday depends on, so the door-knocking is earned rather than sentimental. And take the handbrake from the passenger seat as the last input of the Straight Life.
- From X: the bookkeeper's virtue recording the player's own deed. If Tom stages or lights the answer in Scene 10, Sheila writes it into the book the next morning in the same words she used for Mickey, and Reese later reads it. Take the public bench at the hearing as an unasked gift, set against Agar's solicitor, so that the feared name both sinks him and saves him in one room. In the Kingdom, take the fare entered for the first time in a third hand.
- From both: keep one fire that really was lit, so that the "nothing behind the name" reveal stands against a real menace instead of a second "nothing".
