# LEDGER story bible v7: script editor's note

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7.

1. Tom: 8. The want (the postscript), the fear (the suitcase under the bed) and the flaw (he takes nothing, and it is fear) are all things the player does with one press, which is rare and right for an unvoiced lead. The Ledger gives him a dry, funny voice, and his last line shows the change in every ending.
2. The cast: 8. Sheila, Ron, Ada, June, Terry and Pauline are each somebody, and each turns. Philip is a chessboard and a heater with no want of his own, and Cyril, set up as the man who only wants the tally right, never has a scene where that pays off.
3. The antagonists: 7. Three different ways of coming at him (offers, letters with dates, beating him at his own trade), each with a personal reason and a page in the book, and Ada above them is the best of the lot. Agar's hired heavies are stock, the Widow has only two real scenes, and Danny's arc leans on the framed-rival turn.
4. The central mystery: 7. The truth (Mickey did to Ada what Ada did to Tom, and the bookkeeper wrote the one line he would not) changes what earlier scenes meant and pays off in front of the VAT man. But the player has the whole of it before the climax, and the two people who could simply say what happened are never asked.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The carry at minute nine is a proper hook, and the failed carry ending with "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book is the best single idea in week one. The first Monday is crowded and one sum in it does not add.
6. Structure and pace: 6. Act shapes are sound and the calendar pulls a slow player forward. But six of the biggest scenes fall between 3 and 11 November, the last fortnight of Act II carries three, and Act III's revelation is pre-empted by Act II.
7. The middle: 7. Two chases at once, rivals peaking in turn, a midpoint that takes Terry's hands and Alison's view of him, and a second half that turns on Ada. The weekly loop of fares, count and Friday round will be felt by week five, and the late-November stretch is thin exactly where reviewers punish a slowing main line.
8. Surprise against cliché: 6. The parts are familiar: the inheritance with a dark secret, the kindly old woman who runs the firm, the rival who did not do it, the editor in the big man's pocket, the detective who kept the closed file open, the convenient home video, the bosses' sit-down, settling it in the ring. They are assembled well, but too many are signposted.
9. The endings: 8. States, not a menu, each with a last scene and image, and Ron's seat in a vehicle as the verdict is excellent.
10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and her daughter shielding each other from what both know is the finest thing here; Lilies and the Tangs touch the main line well. The watch's turn is guessable and Karen's wardrobe is a sketch more than a story.
11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline on the radio, the Ledger lines ("The ham had a seat"), Ron at eleven miles an hour and the crabs on the ride-along are actually funny, and the dark scenes are kept quiet.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The offers system, paid off by who writes references at the hearing and who answers the phone box on the last Friday, is the climax testing the flaw through play, which is what the bar asks for. Two roads, many three-way choices and five end states are wider than "plain" admits.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The mystery is solved before it is revealed, and its witnesses are never asked.**
What is wrong: by the end of Act II the player has the importer's PAID rent card and Ron's confession that he put the money on Mickey's desk (scene 17), the ticket clerk saying Mickey met Coultas off the boat (scene 8), and Agar in the rain saying Mickey came to him "last September" offering the plot (scene 21). Together, that is Mickey's guilt. Sheila's kitchen (section 8) then only confirms it, plus her own line. Meanwhile Terry, who drove the watchman every Saturday and was given the night of the eighteenth off (section 10), and Les Coultas, the man who lit it, are never questioned in any scene.
Why it matters: the climax of the dig becomes a recap, and a player who thinks to ask Terry or Les finds the game has no answer.
Fix: cut Agar's date. In the rain he says only that Mickey "had an understanding" with him and the plot settled it; the September date lives only in the 1989 fare book. Make Terry a key: after the ward, his five words can become six ("I was give that night off"), which sends Tom to the fare book. Give Les one short scene across the water where he lies, so that the kitchen is the first time the lie breaks.

**2. Act II's calendar crowds then sags.**
What is wrong: the watchman (3 November), the inside of the office (4th), the Fifth, the ward (7th), the lock-up (10th) and Remembrance (11th) all fall in nine days. After the anniversary and the search, the last fortnight (19 November to 2 December) has Danny, Garbutt's table and Agar's visit.
Why it matters: the bar names a main line that slows in the middle and a hurried last act as the commonest complaints. This shape gives both: a rush round the Fifth, then two quiet weeks before Reese.
Fix: move the watchman and the inside of the office into the last week of October (the watchman goes home every Saturday, so nothing ties it to the 3rd). Put the Widow's offensive into late November as a scene: the night job to get Kevin's copied sheets out of the gate hut, already set up in scene 2, and the gate hiring Tom's men after Remembrance, shown.

**3. Stock crime-drama furniture, too heavily signposted.**
What is wrong: Ada is "the only person on Quay Street who does not look surprised"; the bible tells the player "One of those is true"; Danny says "That wasn't me" the next morning. Any British viewer of crime drama has the frame-up solved there and then. The editor on the Harbour Board, the sergeant with the closed file, Hal's camcorder first seen in scene 13, the sit-down and the bout are each familiar.
Why it matters: "a plot the player has seen before" is the punished thing, and the false flag is the act's main turn.
Fix: cut the "not surprised" beat and keep Ada at her window like everyone else, so the player can honestly believe Danny did it. Let Danny's lads boast louder (already half there). Show Hal filming the match on the first Saturday, so the tape is set up, not supplied. Give Alison's editor a smaller, more local reason to drop the fire (his brother-in-law holds the insurer's agency) rather than the board seat beside Agar.

**4. In a game about books balancing, the books do not balance.**
What is wrong: on Monday "one night's carry is exactly the gap", but the gap is £38 rent and the carry pays £15 (section 6). The Wednesday envelope is never explained, though the Monday one already holds the far bank's twenty pensions. Scene 15 (S) says "four rungs are standing" on a road that has three. Darren is told to put the envelope in Danny's lock-up, and someone has left a new can of paraffin inside, yet Tom needs a key cut by Wayne to get in (scene 13). At Garbutt's table Tom holds "Cyril's tally of what Alan Jensen took", with no scene in which he gets it. Car two has "forty miles" on it for a run from the landing to the warehouse row and back in a town Ron can walk.
Why it matters: players count in this game; the fare book is the hero. One wrong sum and the rest is doubted.
Fix: make the carry pay £15 twice and the Friday round's £8, or make the gap £30. Make Wednesday's envelope the row's rent going over to the tallyman. Correct "four" to "three". The envelope and the can are in the yellow car parked inside the lock-up, which Danny's lads leave unlocked; or Darren has the second key from Akers' old owner. Let Cyril hand Tom the tally in the two-widows aftermath, or let Maureen keep a copy. Make it fourteen miles, or route the run round by the coast road.

**5. Key hinges have no motive on the page.**
What is wrong: Maureen stood still while her husband drowned (sections 0 and 7, scene 16), and the two-widows scene rests on it with no reason given. Cyril, whose whole self is the tally being right, hears a paying man say he paid and sends him away. Sheila keeps Mickey's cut page unread for two months and produces it only on the Friday. Danny says "You can't touch her" of Ada when "the street never has it". Ada lets a reporter lodge over the table where the envelopes are made.
Why it matters: a blank at the hinge reads as convenience, and these are the people players will talk about.
Fix: one line each. Maureen: "He'd had it off me and all, for years." Cyril, at the landing near the end: "Mickey's word was the tally. I never counted him." Sheila brings the page because of what Tom did with her line on Thursday night: she would have burned it herself if he had burned the book. Danny learns it from his father's mouth on Tannery Row. Ada, to Tom: "Better a reporter upstairs than looking in from the street."

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. "Importer a/c. Paid" is not a fare, so it has no business in a fare book. Fix: make it Ron's Monday round, written as a fare: "Row round, R.K. Importer in."
2. Agar is called "the one living man who can be faced for the warehouse", but Les Coultas, Cyril and Ada are alive. Fix: "the one man with money in it".
3. Darren "sleeps in the firm's spare car", but the firm has two cars, both working, and he burns one without looking in the back. Fix: he sleeps in a dead third car in the yard, the one later painted as car three.
4. The unposted letters to June (first Saturday) are set up and never paid off. Fix: what Tom did with them decides whether she shows him the three envelopes on the ward or hides them.
5. Ron's mileage pocket book pays off on the last Friday with no set-up. Fix: he takes it out at the bollard in lesson one.
6. A failed carry puts twenty named cash packets in the police station, yet nobody comes to the office. Fix: Ellis calls the next morning, asks one question, and that becomes her first scene with Tom.
7. Ellis taking the 1989 book into evidence would not stop Customs assessing 1989. Fix: her trade is that the arson is hers and Reese keeps to the money, not that 1989 vanishes.
8. The importer has "come over for the day", but Mickey's page puts him in a town inland. Fix: one or the other.
9. The letter from Mickey is not a will; on what paper does Tom own the firm? Fix: a solicitor's letter in the same envelope, naming him as the will's legatee.

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review

No, not yet. The bones are better than most game stories: a friendship built in a passenger seat, a bookkeeper whose one act of honesty is the climax, a flaw paid for at the phone box, and a tea that was an interview. But the same reviewer would notice that the mystery is spent by the end of Act II, that the turn of the midpoint is the frame-up everyone has seen, and that in a game about counting, several sums are wrong. Fix the five points above and it is within reach of a yes.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. Sheila and the line she wrote. The fare book as the only book that never lied, the Monday runs and the free Saturdays in her small upright hand, "Nobody reads a fare book", and Reese reading it aloud. It is the story's heart and its best surprise.
2. Ron and Tom. The passenger seat, the map as a list of things that shut, the three lessons (the third not funny), the Bishop story finished on the dock road, and Ron's seat in a vehicle as the verdict in every ending. Players will remember it.
3. The offers. Take it or pay, with nothing marking gift from hook, paid off at the hearing and again at the phone box on the last Friday, where only those he let give him something answer. It makes Tom's flaw something the player lives, and lets the climax test exactly what the story built.
