# LEDGER story bible, version 1: a critic's review

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7

1. Tom: 6. The bible gives him a want, a need, a line, two fears and a change, all stated cleanly. But he is mostly defined by what others call him and what he chooses between, and the "need" ("a name is a debt") is a thesis rather than a struggle the player feels hour by hour.
2. The cast: 8. Sheila, Ron, Cyril, Norman Dent, Reese and Father Walsh are specific, period-true and each carries a secret that pays off. The weakness is breadth over depth: forty-odd named people and no single bond that the story is built around.
3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is excellent: she never threatens, she prices, and her history with Ada gives the two-widows cab ride real weight. Agar is a stock developer with a deadline and Cammack a stock local hothead, both lifted by good details (the false grudge, the apologies) rather than by their shape.
4. The central mystery: 6. The truth of the fire is solid, layered and documented in the fare book, which is a lovely device. But it is guessable from Alison's line in the first week, it is fully told by Ada at the midpoint, and after that the story is about hiding a truth rather than finding one.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The first night is brisk, grounded and full of plants (the badge, the forty-one empty envelopes, "they'll send a man"). The failed carry ending in "Nowak, T. £82" in the debt book is the best idea in the first hour, and the tea's second meaning on replay is earned.
6. Structure and pace: 7. Calendar days that pull a slow player forward, rivals peaking in turn and a dated climax week are a sound skeleton. The first half of Act II sags and Act III is crowded.
7. The middle: 6. From Bonfire Night to the hearing it is strong: the Fifth, Remembrance Sunday, the night of the book, the hearing. The three weeks before the Fifth are mostly first rungs and introductions, and the straight road is asserted rather than written.
8. Surprise against cliché: 6. Real freshness: a VAT officer as the climax, Norman's innocence, Danny's grudge resting on a falsehood, the mooring with no boat. Against it: inherited crime business from an unknown uncle, the sweet old neighbour who is the boss, a marina developer, and a grudge match settled in a boxing ring "like our dads would have".
9. The endings: 7. Every ending has a last scene, a last line and a last image in Sheila's hand, and "Ferry landing. One. No charge." is a superb echo. But the Kingdom's two turns differ by one line, and the verdict arrives as one of two sentences from Reese, computed off-screen.
10. The side stories: 8. The watch, Norman Dent, the wardrobe and the mooring each have a want, a turn the player will not see coming and a consequence the town keeps.
11. Tone and humour: 6. The radio as running chorus, the wardrobe twice past Sylvia's step and Reese's ride-along are good comic engines. The sample lines are mostly mild ("it's not the size of the haddock"), and there is little the bible promises will make a player actually laugh beyond three set pieces.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. Carries, rounds, the ride-along, the five calls from the phone box with counted coins, consequences landing in talk: this is genuinely systemic storytelling. Some of the biggest beats (the hearing, Remembrance Sunday, the two widows) are scenes where Tom mostly watches.

## 2. The five weakest points

### 1. No relationship the player will talk about afterwards

What is wrong: the bar asks for one bond as remembered as Henry and Hans. The bible spreads warmth thinly across Sheila (a judge more than a friend), Ron (a man who may leave), June (a mirror), and an optional "someone", Alison or Philip, who gets a supper, a chess game and a hiding place. Nobody is beside Tom through the whole game with their own arc bound to his.

Where: section 5; the "someone" in Act II scenes 4 and the midpoint supper; Ron in scene 10.

Why it matters: an unvoiced lead with an unsaid past needs a companion to play off, or he reads as a cursor. Henry has Hans; Tom has a cast of commentators.

Fix: make Ron the spine. He already brings the asks, walks the round, stands at Tom's shoulder and carries the watch, the yard and the gate. Put him in the passenger seat most nights with a running exchange (Ron talks, Tom picks one-word answers the player will come to love), let his foreman's pride be his arc, and make scene 10 the heartbreak it should be: the Widow's offer is the one moment the player risks losing him. Keep Sheila's naming as the quiet second bond. Cut the optional someone down to Philip only, and give him more.

### 2. The mystery is solved too early and too plainly

What is wrong: Alison tells the player on Wednesday of week one that a Mickey's cab waited at the landing on the fire night. Any player who has watched one crime drama will conclude Mickey drove the arsonists. At the midpoint Ada says it all aloud. After that there is no question left, only concealment, and the climax turns on whether a known line in a known book is found.

Where: Act I Wednesday (Alison in the cab); the midpoint's second tea; Act III Thursday.

Why it matters: the bar asks for a truth uncovered by play that changes earlier scenes and pays off at the climax. This one pays off at the climax but is uncovered by being told, and is guessed early.

Fix: hold one true layer back for Act III and make the player dig it out. The bible already owns it: Agar knew beforehand and profited, and Mickey's "settled" has a second meaning. Keep Ada's midpoint confession to her part only ("I found the reason"), let the player think the fire is closed, then let Reese's reading of the 1989 accounts, plus Les Coultas on Thursday night, show that the fire also paid Mickey's debt to Agar. That reframes Mickey from tragic loyal man to a man who burned a building partly to save his own lease, which reframes the letter and "God forgive me, that's why". Soften Alison's first-week line to the watchman's times, not the cab.

### 3. The first half of the middle and the straight road are underwritten

What is wrong: between Sunday of week one and Bonfire Night are roughly three weeks of game time and eight scenes, most of which are a rival's "noticed" or "visit" rung or a first offer. Pressure is introduced, not applied. The straight road through the second half is one paragraph that claims "the same number of scenes" without writing them.

Where: Act II scenes 1 to 8; "The straight road through the second half".

Why it matters: the bar's commonest complaints in KCD2 are a main line that slows after its first stretch and the same loop in the middle, said by players around hour forty. The bar also names the road of the player who refuses the crime as a place that must not be thin.

Fix: give each of weeks two to four one fixed turning event that costs something, in the way the Fifth does. Week two, Kevin's betrayal discovered on the rank. Week three, the gate stops Tom's drivers for a morning and the cold store wavers. Week four, Agar's first letter dates the hearing and Danny's fifty-pound note becomes a public insult. For the straight road, write its own set pieces: the first society meeting in the school hall going wrong when Ada's people walk out; a clean contract job sabotaged by the gate; a Friday when the honest takings fall £20 short and Tom must pick which of the forty-one goes without.

### 4. The rivals lean on familiar shapes

What is wrong: a property man ruined unless he can build a marina on dead docks by year's end, and a loud local lad with a dead-end grudge settled in a boxing ring before the whole town, are both shapes players have met many times. Ada as the sweet widow who is secretly the boss is itself a known twist.

Where: section 6, Cammack's "How it can end" and Act II scene 15; Agar's climb; section 5, Ada.

Why it matters: the bar's first punishment is "a plot the player has seen before". The ring in particular is the moment a reviewer writes "predictably".

Fix: keep the ring as an option but make the page the true end of Danny's story, and make it hurt him rather than heal him: learning that Mickey paid his father for six years, and that his father hid it, should break Danny's picture of himself, and he should do something unexpected with it (go to work for the Widow, or turn on Ada, who struck nobody off but presided over it). For Agar, lean on what is already unusual: he is the only rival who is courteous, sorry and broke. Give him one scene where he asks Tom for help, not leverage, before the telephone call.

### 5. Act III is crowded and leaves threads loose

What is wrong: in two to three hours the last week carries Reese's five items, the gate-hut break-in, the two widows, Ellis's trade, Alison's proof, the book at the boarding house, Les Coultas, Garbutt's favour, Ada wanting the book back, Cammack's noise and five calls. Several are set up and never closed: Garbutt needs a favour but the bible never says what; Les Coultas is "the only living man who can say who paid him" and no ending says what he did; Ada, the central relationship of the plot, has no face-to-face last scene in the Kingdom or Straight Life beyond a line from a back room.

Where: section 9, "The week before" and the five nights; section 10.

Why it matters: the bar names a hurried last act as the public complaint against GTA V, and an ending that feels computed rather than lived as a punishment.

Fix: move Les Coultas and Garbutt's favour into one thread (Garbutt needs Tom to keep Les quiet, because Les can name Garbutt too) and resolve it on Thursday night. Give Ada a last scene in every road, at her table, with the book either on it or not. Let Reese's verdict be preceded by one visible thing on the Friday morning that the player caused (a phone call answered on screen, a statement read), so the sentence confirms what the player saw rather than announcing a hidden score.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review

No. Not as it stands. I would praise it, and quote the fare book, Reese, Sheila's withheld name and the window. But the bar for singling a story out is that players remember the people, and this bible has a superb ensemble and no one bond at its heart; its mystery is guessed early and told at the midpoint; and its first middle stretch and its honest road are not yet written to the standard of its set pieces. Fix those and it could reach a 9.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. The fare book. "Every fare goes in the book" as first image, as the line that hangs Mickey, as Reese's evidence and as the last image of every ending, down to "Ferry landing. One. No charge." It is a whole theme carried by one object the player handles daily.
2. Ada and the window. The neighbour with one invitation who is the head of the firm, the two figures at the glass under a burning car, the dark window in the Kingdom and the watcher in the Suitcase. With the second tea and the two widows in the cab, it is the strongest strand in the story.
3. Failure as story. The searched envelope that puts "Nowak, T. £82" in the debt book, the licence suspended rather than the game ended, Reese the mild man who is sorry when the books do not add up: the bible turns every loss into a debt the town remembers.
