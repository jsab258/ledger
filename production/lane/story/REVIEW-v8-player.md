# LEDGER story bible, version 8: a player's review

## 1. Scores

**Overall: 7**

1. **Tom: 7.** The flaw is the best thing about him: a man who won't take a cup of tea, turned into a button you press all game, and it pays off at the hearing and in the phone box. His want and his fear are clear. Past the flaw, though, he mostly reacts to people who come to him, and the Kingdom change ("he has learned to find people reasons") is the only arc that really bites.
2. **The cast: 8.** Ron, Sheila, Ada, June, Darren and Terry are people I would talk about afterwards. Terry's five words, June filling three envelopes from her wage under the ward lamp and Frank Cammack signing first are all real moments. Philip, Lee, Wayne, Julie and half the regulars are names I would forget, and Alison is drawn more as a partner on the case than as somebody I'd fall for.
3. **The antagonists: 7.** Danny is the best of them. He beats Tom fairly in daylight, takes credit for a fire he didn't light, and then a page in a book takes away the one true thing he had. The Widow's "she prices, she never threatens" is a fresh idea, and the two widows in the cab is a great scene. Agar is mostly letters and a security firm until scene 21, and a developer buying up the dead docks for a marina is the most familiar villain in 1990 Britain.
4. **The central mystery: 6.** It has a truth that changes earlier scenes: Mickey lied to Ada, and Sheila wrote the line herself. Both reframe the first Sunday and "every fare goes in the book". But most of it is told to Tom by people confessing, and most of it has been told before the climax, so the Thursday night lands on something the player has largely pieced together already.
5. **The opening and its retold scene: 8.** The first night works: a carry with three honest routes, a constable who can turn round, and the failed version putting "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book. Ada's tea that turns out to be an interview is the scene I'd retell. Week one is dense, but you do something with your hands every few minutes.
6. **Structure and pace: 6.** The calendar (hearing, the Fifth, Remembrance, the anniversary, the letter) is a strong spine and pulls a slow player along. But Act II tries to run two ladders, three rivals, the dig, a weekly round and 21 scenes at once, and from Remembrance to Garbutt's table everything piles up in about three weeks of game time.
7. **The middle: 6.** The named scenes are good: Kevin on the rank, the gate at six, watching the watchman, the lock-up. Between them, the keeper's ladder rungs 3 to 5 are one-line ideas with no scene behind them, and the Friday round repeated seven times is exactly the loop the bar warns about, at hour twelve.
8. **Surprise against cliché: 6.** The sweet old widow who turns out to be the boss is a known trope, though revealing it on the first Sunday rather than saving it is clever. After that line ("Mickey found the men. I found the reason"), a car burned under her window while she watches is something I would pin on her straight away. The convenient camcorder tape is a cliché too.
9. **The endings: 7.** The last images are lovely: "Nowak, T. Round the Hook. £2. Paid." "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four." "Nowak!" from the constable. The Suitcase's "No charge". Ron in a vehicle as the verdict is beautiful. The weak spot is that the better and plainer versions of each ending hang on hidden states the player can't read, and the final face-off is with whichever rival happened to telephone.
10. **The side stories: 7.** The watch and Ivy Leadley are real stories with turns that hurt quietly; Ivy and her daughter each protecting the other is the best idea in the section. The wardrobe is a proper set piece. Lilies is thin, and there are only four of them for fifteen hours.
11. **Tone and humour: 7.** Pauline on the radio is a real chorus, and "They're crabs, love. They don't fill forms in" made me laugh. Tom's Ledger as the straight man is a good device. Some of the jokes read as written-funny (Brenda's postcard lines), and the comedy drops out almost completely after the Fifth.
12. **Story and play feeding each other: 8.** The offers mechanic, the fare book as both alibi and noose, the carry, the lock-up planned like a job, Reese riding along, the phone-box run where only people you let in will pick up: this is a story built for hands. It loses marks where the truth arrives through speeches rather than through play.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The mystery is spent before the climax, and mostly by confession.**
What is wrong: by Thursday night of Act III the player has heard from Coultas's tail that Mickey hired a man over the water (scene 8). Norman has said one of Mickey's cabs ran him (scene 10). Terry has said "I drove the watchman" (scene 12). The importer has said he paid (scene 17), and Ron has confirmed it. Agar has said outright, "I paid your uncle four thousand pounds to see that a plot came free" (scene 21). So Sheila's kitchen only adds two things: Mickey lied to Ada, and the line is in Sheila's hand. Then on Friday the June page tells us about the £4,000 a third time. Nearly every step forward is a person saying it out loud: Ada's first-Sunday speech, Darren "in one breath", the importer, Ron, Agar, Sheila at the sink.
Where: sections 6 (first Sunday), 7 (scenes 8, 10, 12, 17, 21), 8 (Thursday night, Friday).
Why a player feels it: the best moment in the game should be the player turning those 1989 pages and working it out himself. Instead he is ticking off a shape he already knows, and the bar asks for a truth "uncovered by play".
Fix: in scene 21 Agar asks for his pages and admits nothing, only "I did not ask how". Leave the £4,000 to the fare book's Harbour Board run and the June page. Turn two confessions into found things: let Ron's Monday collections appear as a pencilled sum in his mileage pocket book, and let the player match Norman's Saturdays to the fare book himself before anyone says a word. The kitchen should be where the pieces click, not where they are repeated.

**2. Ada's hand in the Fifth is visible from the moment it happens.**
What is wrong: the player has heard "Mickey found the men. I found the reason" in week one. Then car two burns under Ada's window, Ada is lit up at the glass with the tallyman behind her chair, and she calls him in that minute with an order to burn Danny's lock-up. A player who pokes at mysteries will suspect her before the fireworks finish. That empties the lock-up's dramatic irony, and Hal's tape becomes a confirmation, not a turn. Then the fire turns out to have the same shape (Mickey found Ada a reason), which is thematic, but it is the same trick twice.
Where: section 7, the midpoint (steps 2 to 4) and scene 13.
Why a player feels it: the midpoint is the game's biggest turn, and if it is guessed in thirty seconds the five days of planning feel like the game waiting for you to catch up.
Fix: burn the car somewhere else, on the rank by the fish market, away from her window. Let the order come the next morning through Cyril at the landing, not from her in person. Give Danny's guilt real weight: his lads did put the window in, there is a yellow-car driver near the rank, and Danny boasts. Keep Ada off the glass that night. The tape then shows her watching from somewhere she said she wasn't.

**3. The middle's machinery has no scenes behind half of it, and the Friday loop repeats.**
What is wrong: keeper's rungs 3 to 5 (the launderette, the cold store, a man at the landing) are each a line and a "turn" with no scene of their own. The Friday round comes back roughly seven times, with "one door different". Scenes 14 to 20 (Remembrance, the lesson or the hall, the gate and two widows, the anniversary, the search, Danny, Garbutt's table, Agar) all fall between 11 November and 1 December.
Where: section 7, the ladder lists and "What a week is"; scenes 14 to 21.
Why a player feels it: at hour ten to twelve the player is either doing the same round again or being rushed through six big scenes in a row. Both are on the bar's list of what gets punished.
Fix: give each keeper's rung one scene with a named person and a turn, the way the straight road's mooring and hall have them, or cut it down to three rungs. Make the round a full set piece only three times (the first, the week after the Fifth, the week of the anniversary) and a quick montage the rest of the time. Move the search or Danny's bout into the week after Remembrance so the late-November run has room to breathe.

**4. Convenient finds do the detective work.**
What is wrong: Hal's camcorder happens to swing to exactly Ada's window and exactly the arsonist's back. The page cut from Mickey's book has been lying in the letters drawer since the first Saturday. The importer happens to be standing on the plot on the anniversary.
Where: section 6 (Saturday), section 7 (scenes 13 and 17).
Why a player feels it: players forgive one lucky break. Three in the main line make the story feel arranged, and it takes away the feeling that you found things out.
Fix: make the tape something the player has to think to look for (Hal "films everything", heard about in week one), and make it unclear: a coat too big, no face. Then Ron has to name it as the coat Darren carries the envelope in, which also puts the friendship to work. Bring the importer in because Tom or Alison tracked him through the name on the June page, or through the insurer, so the anniversary meeting is something the player set up.

**5. The climax splits its attention, and its final face-off is arbitrary.**
What is wrong: the Friday run piles five coins, every hook called in, errands across the Hook, the landing, Ada's table and a rival in the doorway into one afternoon. The rival at the door is whoever telephoned Customs, settled "with what Tom knows or with his hands or by walking round". None of the three rivals is the person the story has been about; that is Mickey, dead, and Ada. And which version of an ending you get hangs on states the player can't see ("the street does not hold his rackets as fact").
Where: section 8, Friday; section 9, the better and plain turns.
Why a player feels it: a climax that is a checklist on a timer can feel like admin. A last fight with whichever rival is left feels like a leftover, not the sum of the man.
Fix: make Ada's table the very last door before the office, after the rival, so the emotional peak is the last thing you do. Write each rival's last stand as its own short scene, built on that rival's history with Tom. In Wednesday's and Thursday's Ledger entries, have Tom name in his own words the two or three things the better turn depends on (who would count him, what the street believes), so the result is seen coming.

## 3. Would I single this story out

No. Not yet, though it is close. I would tell a friend about Ron learning to drive at eleven miles an hour and finishing the Bishop story with the handbrake on. I'd tell them about the fare book where the truth was written in a bookkeeper's small upright hand, and about two widows in the back of a minicab with the meter running. Those are scenes as good as anything in the games I love. But I would be telling them about scenes, not about a story that pulled me through. The mystery is mostly told to me before I get to find it. Ada's big turn is guessable on the night. The middle has too much machinery and too few scenes. If the mystery came through play and the midpoint were hidden properly, this would be a yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. **Ron and Tom.** The passenger seat, the three lessons, Friday's number nine on the quay wall, the gate offer put kindly, the importer's "big fellow" making the player decide whether to believe his friend, and which seat Ron is in at every ending. This is the Henry and Hans of the game.
2. **The fare book, Sheila and her line.** "Every fare goes in the book", paid off by a line she wrote against Mickey's word and a year of waiting for a reader, then "Mr Nowak", then "Tom". It is the spine, the twist and the last image all at once.
3. **The offers.** Take it or pay, with nothing marking which gift has a hook, paid off at the hearing and in the phone box, where only people you let in will pick up. It is Tom's character as a mechanic, and nothing else I have played does it.
