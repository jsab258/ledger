# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 5: a player's review

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8 out of 10.

1. Tom: 7. The flaw is in the buttons, take or pay and the question, and the suitcase under the bed is a lovely small fear I would feel every time I walked past it. His want is thinner than his flaw, though; I know what he will not do far better than what he is after, so he is a good shape rather than a somebody.

2. The cast: 8. Ron is the reason I would keep playing: the passenger seat, the buildings named by what they were, the knees at his ears, the stories that stop a sentence later each time. Sheila, Ada, Pauline, Terry, Shiner and Reese all sound like people; a few of the twenty-one are only names.

3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is the best thing in the bible after Ron: she never threatens, keeps every deal, asks the question first and stands in the road with her hands in her pockets. Agar is good in his one minicab scene and otherwise a run of letters; Danny is a set of dusk jobs with a father bolted on late; Ellis says one line three times.

4. The central mystery: 8. Finding the eleven miles with a steering wheel and a dial, then finding seven, then the bollard, is the best idea here, and a seat that has not moved for a year is a clue I can touch. It drops a point because one of Ada's lines gives the answer away five hours early (point two below).

5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The ferry with £23.50, the carry with a constable who can turn round, and Sheila writing "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book in her own hand is a first half hour I would tell someone about. The first Tuesday is heavy with tea and explaining.

6. Structure and pace: 7. The three acts and three movements sit well, and the Fifth lands in the right place. Act III crams a great deal of talk into two weeks against a rule of few short scenes.

7. The middle: 7. The week table is the right answer to the same-loop complaint: the round shrinking from twenty doors to eighteen is a clock I would feel in my stomach. But four of the six dusk jobs are Danny's, and the two side roads (asking Ron first, winding the firm down) get a paragraph and a table where the main road gets scenes.

8. Surprise against cliché: 7. Pointing the player at Ron and making him wrong while every clue he found stays true is a proper trick. "The quiet trusted one did it" is a move I have seen before, though, and it is flagged too loudly to land as a surprise for anyone paying attention.

9. The endings: 8. The last images are earned and specific: Sheila in the back of car one paying £2 and counting it twice; "Ron drove"; the constable calling "Nowak!" on the first night again. Too much of which one you get rests on one signature on the last Friday and on a question the game never prompts.

10. The side stories: 7. The watch and Ivy Leadley are proper stories with turns that touch the main line, and Ivy and her daughter shielding each other is the most grown-up thing in the bible. One Way is a light errand with a name, and the umbrella is three beats rather than a story.

11. Tone and humour: 7. The crabs ("They don't fill forms in"), Terry's fifth word, Shiner breaking orders by the Tangs' and Darren selling a Customs officer a radio would make me laugh. Most of it is radio, so the lonely fares after the Fifth will be long and grey.

12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The handbrake, the dial, the seat, the take-or-pay offers and the Sunday lessons all carry story through my hands, and the last input being one you should leave alone is clever. The typed question is the risky piece, and the best ending hangs on it.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The question is a master key, and the best ending is hidden behind it.
What is wrong: asking someone what they want opens Kevin, Pauline, Ron, Danny and Sheila, and each time out comes a tidy true answer that fixes the scene. Then the best ending (section 9, the Kingdom's better turn) needs me to ask Sheila in her kitchen (section 8, Wednesday night) when the game deliberately does not offer it.
Why a player would feel it: by the third tidy answer I would stop feeling it as a risk and start treating it as the "solve" button. A player who never types it in that kitchen loses the best ending to something he did not know was there, and feels cheated, not taught.
Fix: let the question cost and sometimes fail. Kevin's answer costs Tom his best driver either way; Pauline laughs and will not say; Danny tells a lie the first time and the truth only on Ropery Lane. Keep a page in the Ledger of who Tom has asked and who has asked him. In the kitchen, give Sheila a held silence with her back turned, the same beat Ron gave on the quay wall, so the player who has learned the verb feels the gap without a prompt.

2. Ada names Sheila five hours early, and Sheila's own walk-round line is not true.
What is wrong: at the Fifth (section 7, the window) Ada says Sheila passed first go in 1961 "and she's never sat behind a wheel since". Section 10 boasts that Sheila has never said a false thing, but at the walk-round (section 6, scene 3) she says Mickey "had it where he wanted it", which Kevin later shows is not so.
Why a player would feel it: a seat set for a small person, a dead man on a ward and the only woman in the firm with a licence, all by hour five: I would guess Sheila on the Fifth and spend scenes 8 to 15 watching Tom be slower than me. And if her first line misled me, the promise that every line of hers holds up falls over.
Fix: give Ada a list instead of one name: Mickey taught half the street, Julie's mother, Kevin, Sheila, Pauline failed three times, Ada gave up. Hold the "never since" part for June, after scene 12. Change Sheila's line to something true that still steers me wrong: "Car two was Mickey's own. The seat sticks. Kevin's given up."

3. The roads off the main road are thin.
What is wrong: the player who asks Ron first (section 7, "If the player asked first" and "If Ron never left") and the player who winds the firm down (section 11) get a paragraph and a table. The bar says the road of the player who refuses the crime must not be thin.
Why a player would feel it: those are exactly the players the game says it rewards for being decent. If my reward is the same clues with less feeling, the game did not expect me.
Fix: write each road's Empty Seat as scenes. With Ron kept, Shiner's bragging becomes the job: Tom and Ron have to keep Shiner quiet without Ron's coat, and scene 13 becomes stopping a man talking rather than staging two strangers. On the wind-down road, make Edith Stockill's finger on eleven miles a full scene and let the hall fight on Bonfire Night be its own set piece.

4. Act III talks too much for a game played with the hands.
What is wrong: in section 8, Agar's speech in the rain, Reese's three speeches, Sheila's long confession, the Widow pricing aloud at the gate, Ellis's coda and four possible Friday rides, all in two weeks. Thursday's phone box with five coins is named and never shown.
Why a player would feel it: this is the GTA V complaint, a last act that hurries and then sits me down to listen. After nine hours of driving, I want the end in the car.
Fix: cut Agar to three lines and the hat left on the seat. Give Reese one line on Monday and keep his words for the ride. Split Sheila's confession: half in the kitchen, the rest said in car two on the Friday, at the bollard, with my hands off the handbrake. Show the phone box as a short run of doors.

5. Danny and Ellis are thinner than the Widow.
What is wrong: Danny (section 5 and the week table) is the freephone, a brick, a stolen car and a crash, then a father who talked to Customs in 1984, handed over by asking. Ellis (scenes 9 and 17, section 8) repeats "while a thing's answerable" and has no scene that is hers.
Why a player would feel it: one great opponent and two pressures is the bible's plan, but I would still feel Danny as a nuisance on a timer and Ellis as a voice at the end.
Fix: put Frank Cammack on screen early, in the Tangs' queue in week one, ignored by the gate, so the Widow's "Ask him" in scene 6 lands on a face. Give one of Danny's dusk jobs to Agar or the Widow. Join Ellis to Ivy's story so the sergeant's own scene is the rainy fare home, and her last-Friday choice reads off what Tom did there.

## 3. Would I single this out to a friend?

Yes. Not for the whole plot but for three things nobody else is doing: teaching a fifty-eight-year-old docker to drive at eleven miles an hour, finding out what happened on a dial in a car at night, and a bookkeeper who has waited a year for somebody to read two lines. I would tell a friend about Ron the way people tell you about Hans. I would say it with a warning that the last act talks and that a sharp player will guess the driver early.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron, the passenger seat and the three lessons, paying off with whatever seat he is in at the end. That is the game.

2. The eleven miles: the fare book lines, driving it and getting seven, the bollard with the firm's paint, and Reese's "Mileage is a truthful thing".

3. Sheila's year: "Every fare goes in the book", "Mr Nowak" held at arm's length, the licence kept up for thirty years, and the handbrake left alone as the last input.
