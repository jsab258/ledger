# LEDGER, story bible version 3: a critic's review

## 1. Scores

**Overall: 8**

1. **Tom: 7.** The want (his own name said with warmth) and the fear (the packed suitcase) are clear and played with the hands, and the Ledger gives an unvoiced man a dry, likeable voice. The flaw is clever but risks becoming a checklist ("accept every gift"), and his change is shown mostly in the last two lines of each ending, not across the middle.
2. **The cast: 9.** Sheila, Ron, Pauline, Reese and Father Walsh are people I would remember a year later, and the regulars are met often enough to become a town. Ron in the passenger seat is the game's Henry and Hans, and it is built from things the player does, not things he is told.
3. **The antagonists: 7.** Agar is excellent (the glass door, the rain, the wife who thinks they are moving to be near her sister), and the Widow's ladder of fair offers is a fresh way to be threatened. But there are too many of them, Danny's best material is taken off him by his father, and all three rivals are settled before the climax.
4. **The central mystery: 7.** The true book being the plain one is a genuinely good idea, and it does change what earlier scenes meant. But the evidence points at Mickey early and loudly, and the truth is then confirmed six times in five days, which turns a revelation into a recap.
5. **The opening and its retold scene: 8.** The carry at minute nine, with the failed version ending in "Nowak, T. £82" in the debt book, is a scene players will tell each other about. Ada's tea becoming an interview on a second run is a fine replay gift.
6. **Structure and pace: 7.** The calendar of real days and dated letters gives the open middle a spine and pulls a slow player forward, and the rivals peak in turn. The midpoint night carries five big beats at once and the last week is busier than a three to four hour act can hold.
7. **The middle: 7.** Strong set pieces (the hearing, the lock-up, Remembrance Sunday, the two widows) but the ten-hour stretch between them is "the game as designed", unspecified, and that is exactly where KCD2 drew its loop complaints. The straight road is thinner and loses its stakes in Act III.
8. **Surprise against cliché: 6.** The sweet old lady who runs it all, the dead benefactor who was the villain, and the dead man's confession on a cut page are all shapes a reviewer has seen. The fare book and the VAT man are what lift it.
9. **The endings: 8.** Each has a last scene and a last image written in two books, and Ron's seat as the verdict on the friendship is the best idea in the bible. The climax itself is a phone box and a man closing a notebook, which is watched more than played.
10. **The side stories: 8.** Ivy and her daughter protecting each other from the same truth, Karen's wardrobe, Hal's tape and Ron's pawned watch are real stories with turns. A couple (the mooring, the Tangs) are plot plumbing dressed as side stories.
11. **Tone and humour: 8.** Actually funny: "The ham had a seat", rain by four, the crabs in the Customs officer's cab, Pauline's hour off. The dark scenes are kept quiet and the light ones placed so there is somewhere to fall from.
12. **Story and play feeding each other: 8.** The small offers paying off at the hearing and the phone box, the driving lessons, the round with faces and the failed carry are all story carried by systems. The last Friday, the most important hour of the game, is the least playable.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The last truth is guessable by the midpoint and then told too many times.**
What is wrong: the bible wants the player to believe Mickey was "a decent man caught in the middle" until the two books. But by scene 8 Garbutt says he placed the brothers with Mickey; by scene 10 the watchman says Mickey's cab moved him; Agar has already said "Mickey and I had an understanding"; and the spine in section 1 tells us the plainest book is the only true one. A player who has read any crime fiction will name Mickey at scene 10. Then Act III confirms it through the two books on Monday evening, Agar on Tuesday, Reese and Les on Wednesday, Reese again on Thursday and Sheila's cut page on Friday.
Where: section 7, scenes 8, 10 and 17; section 8 throughout.
Why it is felt: the bar names "an ending guessed in the first hour" as the commonest story complaint. A twist that is suspected for eight hours and then repeated six times lands as confirmation, not shock.
Fix: muddy the trail in the middle so a second answer looks as likely. Let the watchman's lifts read as Ada's arrangement (she has the watch seat, after all) and let Garbutt believe Agar paid for the brothers. Then let the two books be the single moment the truth lands, and cut its echoes: Les's "old girl who'd want to think it was her idea" goes, Reese marks the pages without comment, and Sheila's cut page says less (see point 5).

**2. The rivals are finished before the climax, and the climax is paperwork the player watches.**
What is wrong: Danny, the Widow and Agar each close in scene 22, the sit-down that ends Act II. In Act III they are reduced to one telephone call that brings the inspector and, for Agar, one night visit. Friday is the phone box and then "the player watches what he caused". For a game that says hands first, the last hour is talk and spectating.
Where: section 7, scene 22; section 8, "Why the inspector comes" and Friday; section 9.
Why it is felt: the bar names GTA V's thin antagonists and hurried last act. A reviewer will say the villains left the stage early and the finale is a tax audit with a slideshow of consequences.
Fix: keep one rival live into the last week, whoever came out of Act II hurt and standing, and give them Friday night, not just a phone call: the Widow's men across the cold-store road, Danny's lads at the yard, or Agar's security firm at the mooring while Reese still has the books. Then the last press is something Tom does on the quay before the morning verdict, and the Reese scene becomes the reckoning of it.

**3. The straight road loses its stakes in the last act.**
What is wrong: Act III's danger is chiefly Tom's own wash (cause 2) and the temptation to alter or burn the books. A player who wound the club down has no wash. What does the inspection threaten him with? Mickey's free Saturdays are Mickey's crime, not his, and the bible never says what an honest new owner loses if Reese finds them.
Where: section 8, causes 1 to 3 and "Five mornings"; section 9, The Straight Life.
Why it is felt: the bar demands no thin stretch "on the road of the player who refuses the crime". A refuser will feel the last week happening to someone else.
Fix: give the straight road its own cost in the books. The society's bank account and its first clean money went through the firm's till (the cold-store shift, Sheila's twenty-pound loan), so a fare book that does not add blocks the society's registration and the forty-one's first payout. Reese can sink the lawful thing the player built. That makes the honest player's Friday as tense as the keeper's.

**4. Ada carries one secret too many, and the town is too tightly knotted.**
What is wrong: Ada is the hidden head, gave the word on the fire, showed her son's gang his theft before he drowned, let her room to the reporter to watch her, sold her house to fund the club and is in arrears. Around her, Maureen is her daughter-in-law, Alison lodges above her, Ellis's mother is on the list, Frank Cammack was struck off by Ada's son, and Garbutt also found the nephew. Every thread ties to every other.
Where: section 0 items 1, 3 and 12; section 4; section 10, layers two and four.
Why it is felt: the harmless old woman who secretly runs everything is a stock reveal, and the small-world links start to feel authored rather than lived. A reviewer will call it neat.
Fix: cut two links. Let Alison lodge above the chandler's and find her view of Mickey's door by her own choice, and let the sale of Ada's house stay unsaid (her arrears letter can be the club's, not her own). Ada keeps her window, her table and her son, which is more than enough.

**5. The dead man's written confession explains what the player should have earned.**
What is wrong: Sheila's kept page reads "Told nobody about the importer. Not Ada. Not the book. The fare book knows. God forgive me, the lad will have to." It names the fare book as the answer and states Mickey's guilt in so many words, on the last Friday, after the player has already found it.
Where: section 8, Friday; section 10, layer six.
Why it is felt: the confession note left by the dead is one of the oldest devices in crime writing, and here it is redundant. It also tidies away the one ambiguity the bible says it wants: whether Mickey meant the letter sent.
Fix: make the cut page a sum, in Mickey's style: initials, the importer's name, a figure that matches the rent card, and one word, "paid", underlined twice. The player who found the truth reads it as confession; the player who did not reads a number. Leave "God forgive me" unwritten.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review

No, not yet, though it is close. The friendship with Ron, Sheila withholding his name, the fare book as alibi and noose, the VAT man with his flask and his bad knee: those are things I would quote, and the small-offers mechanic that decides who stands up for Tom at the hearing and who answers the phone box is a smarter marriage of story and system than most games manage. But the two things a reviewer weighs hardest in a crime story, the turn and the climax, are its weakest parts. The turn (the kindly uncle did it) is visible from the middle and then repeated, and the climax is a man listening to an inspector's verdict after the rivals have already gone home. KCD2 and RDR2 earn their praise in their last chapters; this one is strongest in its first week and its middle set pieces. Fix the trail, keep one rival live into the last night, and give the honest road something to lose, and I would change my answer.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. **Ron and Tom, written for the hands.** The passenger seat, the map learned as a list of things that shut, the three driving lessons (the third "not funny"), number nine on the quay wall, and Ron's seat in the closing vehicle as the verdict on the whole friendship. "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four" is the best last line in the bible.
2. **The fare book as the true book, read by a Customs officer.** "Every fare goes in the book" at minute 1:45, paid off by "Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak." It is original, period-true, and makes the dullest object in the office the centre of the mystery. Reese, who only wants to know what happened, is the right man to open it.
3. **The small offers and the people who answer.** Grace's tea, Sheila's sandwich, Ron's supper: take it or pay. A player who paid his way with everybody sits through a silent hearing and stands in a phone box with five coins and nobody to ring. It turns the flaw into a consequence the player built himself, and it should survive every revision.
