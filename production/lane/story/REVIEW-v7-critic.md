# LEDGER, story bible version 7: a critic's review

## 1. Scores

**Overall: 8**

1. **Tom: 7.** The flaw, a man who will take nothing, is the best idea in the bible: it is a mechanic, it has no safe answer, and it pays off at the hearing and the phone box. But his change is shown mostly through what other people call him and two lines in a notebook, and at the climax his agency is mostly choosing what to do with pieces of paper.
2. **The cast: 8.** Sheila, Ron, Ada, Terry, Pauline and Reese are people I would remember, each with one hard edge. The regulars and a handful of the middle names (Lee, Kevin, Wayne, Hal, Edith) blur, and there are more of them than 15 to 20 hours can make known.
3. **The antagonists: 8.** Three rivals who come at different things in different ways, each with a personal reason and a page in the book, plus Ada, who is the real one and is good to him first. Danny's arc is the strongest; the Widow prices rather than acts and gets few scenes in person; Agar lives mostly in letters until the rain.
4. **The central mystery: 8.** It has a real truth, found with the hands, and it changes earlier scenes: the watchman's free lifts, "that's the last one, Cyril", the letter's "people are owed". But the full solution is a chain of loans, interest, lies and lifts that a player will struggle to hold in his head at the moment it lands.
5. **The opening and its retold scene: 9.** The first week is the best stretch: a money gap that one night's carry exactly fills, a carry with honest routes, a failed carry that writes his own name in the debt book, and the tea that becomes a different scene once the first Sunday shows whose table it is.
6. **Structure and pace: 7.** The middle has a deliberate shape (paper, street, people, one table) and the calendar pulls a slow player forward. Act III asks two to three hours to carry Reese's week, the kitchen, Ellis's trade, the cut page, the phone box, every hook, Ada's table and a rival in person; that is the hurried last act the bar warns about.
7. **The middle: 7.** Strong named scenes (the hearing, the ward, the lock-up and the tape, Remembrance, two widows in a cab) and both roads have rungs worked with the hands. Between them the weekly loop of fares, a dusk job, the count and the Friday round is sketched in one paragraph, and "one door different each week" will not carry ten hours alone.
8. **Surprise against cliché: 7.** Ada and Sheila are genuine surprises. The other turns are familiar shapes: the framed rival who is innocent, the eager lackey who lit the fire, the beloved dead patron who was the guilty man.
9. **The endings: 8.** Each is a state the player built, each has a last scene and a last image, and the fare-book and Ledger lines are excellent ("Stayed."; "Ferry landing. One. No charge."). Ron in a vehicle as the verdict on the friendship is the best device here. The better Kingdom turn hangs on a long list of conditions the player cannot read during play.
10. **The side stories: 8.** Ivy and her daughter, each protecting the other from a thing both know, is the finest piece of writing in the bible; the watch and the lilies tie neatly into the main line. Five whole stories is thin for the length, and the Tangs' turn (Danny stops it with a sentence) is the weakest.
11. **Tone and humour: 7.** Pauline's radio, the Ledger as straight man and the crabs on the ride-along are actually funny on paper. Almost all of it sits before the Fifth, so the back half is one long quiet note with little relief.
12. **Story and play feeding each other: 9.** The offers, the fare book as alibi and noose, the driving lessons with the roles swapped, the carry with a tail, a hearing built from the town's memory: this is the rare bible where the systems are the story. The one place it fails is the climax's key document, which is handed over rather than found.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The solution is accountancy, not revelation.**
What is wrong: the truth of the fire is Mickey borrowing £4,000 from Agar on a handshake, hiring two men over the water, promising Agar the plot, taking the importer's rent and paying it to Agar as interest, writing a false line in the real book, telling Ada the man refused, booking the watchman's lifts through Sheila, giving Terry the night off and driving the run himself.
Where: section 8, Thursday night in Sheila's kitchen; section 10, point 5.
Why it hurts: a player meets this as four fare-book lines and a speech at the sink, at the end of 15 hours. A reviewer who has to draw a diagram to explain your twist will call it convoluted. The great reveals (the one this story is reaching for) are one sentence long.
Fix: make it one lie. Cut the Agar loan and the interest. The importer paid; after the gate stopped paying in, Mickey quietly kept that rent to fill the forty-one, needed the hole hidden, and told Ada the man had refused so the club would burn him. Agar stays the man who bought the plot cheap and knew why. Then the kitchen adds exactly one new fact to what the player already holds: "Importer a/c. Paid." in Mickey's own hand.

**2. Act III is crushed, and its key paper is withheld.**
What is wrong: the last two or three hours hold the whole Reese week, the kitchen, Ellis's trade, the cut page, the phone-box run, every hook called in, Ada's table and the surviving rival in person. And the page that settles everything has been in Sheila's keeping since the night Mickey died. She never read it and simply produces it on Friday morning.
Where: section 8, Friday; section 10, point 6.
Why it hurts: a crowded last act is the complaint the bar names against GTA V. A decisive document that a character held all game, never opened, feels like the writer's hand, and it takes the last find away from the player in a game that is otherwise strict about finding things by play.
Fix: put the cut page in the bundle of unposted letters to June found on the first Saturday. Opened, it means nothing yet (a name, a town, a sum); given to June unopened, she brings it to the ward in scene 12 and asks what it is. Either way the player carries it from week one, and the kitchen is when he understands it. Move the rival's last stand to Wednesday night, so Friday is the run, Ada's table and Reese.

**3. The midpoint's turns are signposted.**
What is wrong: Danny says "That wasn't me" the next morning, and an experienced player reads a framed rival's denial as true straight away. Darren "offers, too eagerly" to make a noise during the planning of the lock-up job. Together they make the tape a confirmation, not a shock.
Where: section 5, Danny's turn; section 7, the midpoint and scene 13.
Why it hurts: the bar says a familiar plot sinks a story faster than anything. "The obvious suspect didn't do it, the hanger-on did" is one of the most familiar shapes there is.
Fix: invert Danny. He does not deny it. His lads put the window in a week earlier, a burned car is good for business, and he lets the street think it was him; he even smiles about it at the Tangs' counter. The player then has every reason to hit the lock-up. The tape shows Danny lied in the other direction, and the question becomes why a man would claim a crime that maimed a driver. Take "too eagerly" out of Darren's offer.

**4. Every secret is the same secret.**
What is wrong: Alan Jensen drowned while seven people stood still; Norman let a detective believe a lie for a year; Cyril sent the importer away and told nobody; Ron put the rent on the desk and never said a word; Frank hid his payments from his son; Ivy and her daughter each pretend; Terry "saw nothing, on purpose"; Sheila wrote a line and waited a year. Most of these come out as confessions spoken to Tom.
Where: scenes 10, 12, 16, 17, 19, 21, the kitchen; side story 2.
Why it hurts: the theme holds together, but by the sixth "I never said owt" the note has gone dull, and the back half turns into a run of monologues in a game that promises few short scenes.
Fix: keep the four that matter most (Sheila, Ron at the anniversary, the two widows, Ivy). Cut Terry's night off from the solution. Turn two confessions into things the player finds: Ron's silence about the rent shown by the rent card and his own mileage pocket book rather than a speech; Norman's truth seen from the parked cab, not told at his gate.

**5. Alison is a function after the midpoint.**
What is wrong: before the Fifth she has three good scenes with work for the hands and a trade of truths. After it she has a decision about what to file, a proof on Wednesday night, and a place in each ending (a desk, the back row). The romance that is meant to be "the someone worth being honest for" is thinner than the friendship with Ron by a long way.
Where: section 4, "Alison and Tom"; scenes 17 and 18; section 9.
Why it hurts: players remember relationships made in play. If the love interest stops doing things, the honesty she is supposed to cost him has nothing to bite on, and the endings that ask "did she stay" will not land.
Fix: give her one job with her hands after the midpoint: getting into the Harbour Board's minute room at night for the original sale papers, with Tom as lookout and the cab as getaway, inside the week of the search. Let what he told her truly or falsely decide whether she covers for him when Norman's torch finds them. That one scene turns her choice on the anniversary from a conversation into a debt.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review

No, not as it stands, though it is close enough that the gap is the five points above rather than anything fundamental. On the evidence of this bible I would praise the story warmly inside a review: the fare book that turns out to be the only honest book, Ron in the passenger seat learning to drive at eleven miles an hour, and a widow's tea that turns out to have been a job interview are the kind of things readers remember. I would not yet make it the headline reason to buy the game, because the solution at its heart is too intricate to land in one blow, the last act tries to settle everything in one Friday, and the midpoint's turns are shapes an experienced player has seen before. Simplify the lie, let the player carry the cut page from week one, invert Danny, and this is a story I would single out.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. **Ron and Tom.** The passenger seat, the map learned as a list of things that shut, the three lessons, the Bishop story finished with the handbrake on, the pocket book of mileage, and the last image of which seat Ron is in. This is the Henry and Hans of the game, and it is built for the hands.
2. **The fare book and Sheila.** "Every fare goes in the book," the plainest book in the office as alibi and noose, a woman who wrote down a fare against her employer's word and waited a year for anyone to read it, a VAT man who rides a route because mileage is truthful, and "Night, Tom" as the reward. Keep it exactly.
3. **The gift and the hook.** Every free thing is one press, take it or pay, with no marker of which is which, and the town counts it at the hearing and in the phone box. It makes Tom's flaw playable, it makes Ada's tea the deepest hook in the game, and it is the idea a reviewer would quote.
