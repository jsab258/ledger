# LEDGER, story bible version 4: a critic's review

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8

1. Tom: 8. The gift-or-hook system turns a flaw into something the player does with one press, and the suitcase, the Ledger's dry voice and the name ladder give an unvoiced man with no past a real want and a visible change.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Pauline, Terry, Reese and Ivy Leadley are people I would remember and quote; Alison is the weak link: her scenes run on a formula (one true thing traded each way) and she exists mostly to carry the dig.
3. The antagonists: 7. Danny is the best written rival, a man beating Tom fairly with dirty money and then losing the one true thing he had. The Widow is a price list with a good line, and her personal story is told for her in someone else's cab.
4. The central mystery: 7. The truth genuinely recolours earlier scenes ("That's the last one, Cyril", the free Saturdays, the letter), and the fare book as alibi and noose is a superb device. But it has too many layers, the decisive motive is small, and most of it reaches the player through confessions rather than through his hands.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The first night is quick, physical and specific, and a failed carry ending with "Nowak, T. £82" in the debt book is the kind of thing players tell each other about. The first nine minutes introduce too many names in passing;
6. Structure and pace: 6. The calendar spine (the hearing, Bonfire Night, Remembrance, the anniversary, Reese's letter) is excellent and pulls a slow player forward. But Act III front-loads its truth on the Monday evening and then spends the climax on errands, and the last week stacks five more revelations in talk.
7. The middle: 6. Strong named scenes, but ten to thirteen hours hang on about twenty of them, the days between are unwritten, and three of four dig beats are reading, watching or walking.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. The sweet widow who runs the outfit and the benefactor who was guilty all along are both shapes a seasoned player knows. What saves it is the execution: the books, the mechanism of "finding someone a reason", and a truth that arrives through a VAT inspection.
9. The endings: 8. Each has a last scene and a last image that sum the man ("Nowak's?", "Stayed", "Nowak!" from the wrong side, a date with nothing under it), and Ron's seat in a vehicle as the verdict on the friendship is exactly the RDR2 lesson. The Kingdom and the Straight Life each split into turns that may be hard for a player to read from his own play.
10. The side stories: 8. Ivy and her daughter protecting each other from a thing both know is as good as anything in KCD2's side content; Ron pawning the watch for the flowers and Karen's wardrobe are whole stories with real turns.
11. Tone and humour: 8. Pauline's radio as the game's bar is an inspired substitute, the crabs on Reese's ride-along are a real set piece. The light material all sits before the Fifth, so the last third may be grim for longer than the bible admits.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The offers feeding the hearing and the phone box, the driving lessons using the car's handling with the roles swapped, the carry as the first lesson in what the envelope is: this is a bible written for hands. It slips where the turns are delivered by a person sitting down and explaining.

## 2. The five weakest points

### 1. Too much truth, told by too many confessions

What is wrong. The secrets come in layers (Ada behind Cyril, Ada behind Darren, Alan's drowning, Frank's striking off, Agar's debt, the Coultas brothers, Mickey behind Ada, Sheila's line, the forty-second name), and most land as someone speaking plainly at length: the second tea, Darren on the pavement in one breath, the two widows, the priest at his table, Agar on the doorstep, Cyril at Ada's table, Les at the landing, Sheila on Friday morning.

Where. Section 7 midpoint and scenes 12, 13, 16 and 17; section 8, Tuesday to Friday; section 10.

Why a player would feel it. The bible says this is not a story-first game, yet the last week alone has Agar, Cyril, Les, Ellis and Sheila each adding a fact before Ada's table. KCD2 players punish "too much talk" more than a thin plot, and a reviewer will say the mystery is solved by a queue of people who decide to tell him.

Fix. Cut Alan's drowning as a hidden crime: the two widows' cab can carry the grievance as a grudge, not a revelation. Turn two confessions into objects. Agar's handshake and the interest payments should be found in the fare book's Monday column and Edith's books, not told on the doorstep; Rule of thumb for the last week: no more than one new fact per night, and the player should have his hand on it first.

### 2. Mickey's motive is small, and the twist's shape is used up before it arrives

What is wrong. The final truth is that Mickey burned the warehouse to clear a £4,000 handshake debt to Agar, which Agar then did not clear. That is thin for a man who kept forty-one families for thirty years. Worse, the bible's central mechanism, a person behind the hand who "finds somebody a reason", is revealed twice before the end (Ada behind Cyril on the Fifth, Ada behind Darren in scene 12), so by the time the player asks who found Ada's reason, the bible has taught him the answer is one more layer up, and the only person left up there is the man who sent for him.

Where. Section 0 item 5; section 8, "The two books"; section 10 item 5; scene 12's closing line, "He begins to wonder who found hers", which all but tells the player.

Why a player would feel it. A reviewer who guesses the ending in Act II calls the plot predictable, the commonest story complaint in the bar. A small motive also makes the uncle's guilt a device rather than the sum of the man.

Fix. Make the debt the club's. Mickey borrowed the £4,000 from Agar in the summer of 1989, after the scheme ended and the gate stopped paying, to keep the forty-one envelopes full. The fire is then what the forty-one were bought with, the theme turned on its head. Then cut scene 12's last sentence and let Cyril genuinely run the far bank on his own judgement, so the pattern is not a ladder the player can climb in his head.

### 3. The climax is a clerical run, and the mystery has already paid off

What is wrong. The truth lands on the Monday evening of the last week, when the player reads the two books side by side. The Friday that should be the climax is a phone box, five coins and written calls opening fetch-and-carry jobs against the clock, ending with Reese closing his notebook. The straight player's week is thinner still: he washed nothing, so most of Reese's items are not about him, and his "hardest call" is a single choice to go to Ellis.

Where. Section 8, "The week before" and "Friday, the run"; the last paragraph of section 8.

Why a player would feel it. The bar asks for a mystery that pays off at the climax. Here the climax is errands, and the best reveal is done in an evening of reading four days earlier.

Fix. Hold one decisive piece of the truth back for Friday and make it something the run must fetch. The cut page could be the fourth thing Reese needs, in Sheila's keeping, so the last errand is the walk to her, under the rival's hands, and the page is read at the counter with Reese present. For the straight road, give Reese his own items: the society's bank book, the mooring sale and Edith's ledger, attacked by the rival, so the honest Friday is as physical as the keeper's.

### 4. The middle is a list of excellent scenes with nothing written between them

What is wrong. Act II is ten to thirteen hours across six weeks. It has about twenty written scenes, and the keeper's "ladder is the firm's rise" is scene 3 and scene 9 and little else. The dig's four jobs are a walk at low tide, a page-by-page search of minute books, watching a man leave his post, and meeting a stranger at a plot; only one involves the player's hands doing anything that could go wrong.

Where. Section 7, opening paragraphs and scenes 3, 4, 7, 9 and 10.

Why a player would feel it. The bar says the "same loop" complaint comes at a median of 42 hours and is about the middle; RDR2's slow middle is the public record. A search of archive volumes reads well in a bible and plays as a menu.

Fix. Write the week's loop as a story in itself: what Monday, Wednesday and Friday mean on each road, how each changes after the hearing, after the Fifth and after Remembrance, and which regular's line changes as the firm rises (Lily Tang's "Same as last week?" is the right model; it needs six more). Make the minute books a night job: Edith tells Tom which volume, and getting into the harbour office after hours to copy it is the carry.

### 5. Too many convenient witnesses

What is wrong. The case is held together by people and things that happen to be in exactly the right place: Hal's camcorder swinging off the jetty to frame Ada and the tallyman at the window and then the arsonist at the car; Cyril standing in Ada's room at the precise moment the whole Hook is looking at her window; Gordon holding a parcel of returned letters and having pulled Alan out of the water; Mickey, who planned the fire with care, writing the run in the firm's day book in his own hand.

Where. Section 7, the midpoint (the window) and scene 12 (the tape); section 6, Saturday; section 8, "The two books".

Why a player would feel it. Each is defensible alone; together the town feels staged, and a reviewer will name the camcorder as the moment the story reached for a gadget.

Fix. Make the tape partial: it shows the coat too big for him and the can, and nothing of the window; the window at the midpoint stays as the player's own sight, which is enough. Put the paraffin in a book instead, the ironmonger's day book with a name and a date, which fits a story about books. And give Mickey's last fare-book line intent: let the cut page or the letter show he wrote it so that one day it would be found, which turns a convenience into his confession.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review

No, not yet, though it is close. The fare book, Mr Reese, Ron's lessons and Ada's two teas are better ideas than most crime games ever have. But what stands between this bible and the review I would want to write is exactly what the bar punishes: a middle that is a list of strong scenes with the connecting weeks unwritten, a final truth whose shape the player can see coming, and a last act that tells more than it lets the player find and ends on errands. Fix the first three points above and I would say yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron Kirby and the passenger seat. A docker who cannot drive, three lessons that start as slapstick and stop being funny, and the friendship judged in every ending by which seat he sits in and whether he looks across.
2. The fare book as the only book that never lied, and Reese as the man who reads it. A mild Customs officer with a flask, the crabs, and "Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak" is a fresh engine for a crime story.
3. The gift and the hook. Every offer as one press, read back at the hearing and the phone box, marries flaw and system and makes an unvoiced man into somebody.
