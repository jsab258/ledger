# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 2: a player's review

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7 out of 10. It's good, and in places better than good. The friendship and the handbrake are things I would remember. But the two big turns reach me as speeches, the menace is gentle all the way through, and Act III is mostly people talking at a counter.

1. Tom: 8. For a lead who never speaks, his flaw is properly played. Paying for tea that was free, the packed suitcase and "You've unpacked" give me a man without a word of backstory. His change runs through what I press, and that is the right way round.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Ada, Pauline and Terry's four words a shift are people I would talk about afterwards. Julie and Garbutt are thin.
3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is the best thing here after Ron: she never lies, she prices everything, and she stands in the road with her hands in her pockets. Danny turns into a sort of mate, Agar is mostly letters until scene 18, and none of the three ever frightens me.
4. The central mystery: 7. The truth is human and fair. Nobody burned anything on purpose, he came back, and the person Ron was hiding from was Tom. But I suspect "Ron drove" well before the game says it, and the two real turns are explained to me rather than found. One clue is also left hanging, the brass tally in Ellis's file.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The ferry, £23.50, the carry with the constable, and "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book. Lesson one at the bollard is a scene I would tell a friend about.
6. Structure and pace: 7. The three movements around the Fifth are well shaped, and the weekly table stops the weeks from feeling copied. The dates slip, though: on 18 November Ron says "She's had me a month" after thirteen days, and the scene numbers and the table don't agree on which week the job falls in. Act III also slows to a talk week.
7. The middle: 7. The Empty Seat is a real idea, with Ron across the road picking men every morning. But a week is still fares, a dusk job, a count and Friday, six times over, and the "ask Ron first" branch empties the middle of its own engine.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. It cleverly refuses the hidden boss, the guilty uncle and the crooked landlord. Underneath sits a familiar British picture, though: dying docks, the noble thieves' club for pensioners, the wise widow at her window and the decent copper who will not ask.
9. The endings: 8. Every one has a last scene and a last image, and Ron's seat as the verdict is excellent. "Ron drove." and "Got it, boss" would land. The plain Kingdom, where Mickey is framed and Ron comes back out of guilt, is properly bitter.
10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and her daughter, each shielding the other from what both know, is the best of the four. Alison's turn is good, and Karen's wardrobe is a proper set piece. The watch is a twist, not a story.
11. Tone and humour: 7. "The ham had a seat", the crabs that do not fill in forms, and "Tell Pauline it don't count" made me smile on the page. Nothing here is as funny as KCD2's best set pieces or the funny half of GTA.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The handbrake meaning one thing for the whole game, and the last input being one you can leave alone, is the best idea in the bible. The take-or-pay press risks turning into a rule I have solved, and the typed question is a big ask of a system.

## 2. The five weakest points

### 1. The two real turns are told, not found

What is wrong: the bible's own turns are "he came back" and "nobody was blackmailing anybody". The second arrives in scene 14 because the Widow simply tells me. The first arrives in scene 15 as Ron's longest speech in the game. Shiner's slip in scene 10 is also a confession on cue. My hands confirm none of it.

Where: section 7, scenes 10, 14 and 15. Section 10's "uncovered by play" is not met.

Why a player feels it: in Red Dead the worst truths land because you have already ridden through them. Here I sit and listen. The best payoff becomes a cutscene, and the bible says it has none.

Fix: make the eleven miles something I drive. In the scene 9 count, give me the mileage and let me drive the row, the bollard, the row again, Ropery Lane and the yard until the numbers add up. Then the "came back" truth is mine before Ron says it, and his speech in scene 15 can drop to a few lines that confirm it. Have the Widow answer "Who is there he'd mind knowing?" only if I bring her the tally or the Bishop's words. Settle Ellis's tally as Tapper's, so the last clue closes.

### 2. Asking Ron early breaks half of Act II

What is wrong: if I ask Ron what he wants before the Fifth, he turns the Widow down and stays. But scene 13 has him opening the cab door as her foreman. Scene 15 has him picking men at the gate and saying "She's had me a month". Scene 16's list of who stands beside Tom at the door assumes he left. All the bible says about this branch is that "its ache is that the seat is full".

Where: section 7, "If the player asked first", and scenes 13, 15 and 16.

Why a player feels it: this is the choice the bible most wants me to make, and making it would leave me playing scenes written for someone else.

Fix: write this branch's own versions of the three scenes. Scene 13 has Tapper on the door, and Ada asks the Widow what losing Ron cost her. Scene 15 is the anniversary lesson started from the rank, with Ron silent in the seat until the row. His question becomes "You asked me once. Ask me now." Cut "a month" in either case.

### 3. Nobody is frightening and nothing truly hurts

What is wrong: Danny apologises with a joke, Agar apologises before every blow, and the Widow keeps every deal. The worst a player can lose is a tax bill and a firm. Nobody named is hurt in the present, and violence and menace are the owner's own page items. The one physical standoff, on the cold-store road, is a scene where my line means I can only wait.

Where: section 5, all three opponents, and Act II scenes 5, 6 and 16.

Why a player feels it: this comes from GTA and Red Dead, where a quiet story still has a moment you feel in your stomach. Here the dread never builds. By the third movement I would know that everyone is decent underneath, and stop worrying.

Fix: inside the frame (a car hurts, nobody dies), give Danny one escalation that costs a named person. Julie's car is run into the dock wall on a night fare, and she is in plaster when Garbutt offers her the van. Let the Widow's price in Act III, if I dealt crooked, be Terry.

### 4. Act III is a week at a counter

What is wrong: Monday is Reese reading. Tuesday is a comic ride-along. Wednesday is old books and two marks. Thursday is Reese's longest speech, then Sheila's kitchen. Four of the six days are talk, in a game that says it is played with the hands and has few short scenes.

Where: section 8, "The week", Monday to Thursday.

Why a player feels it: just when it should go fastest, the game slows and talks. That's the complaint about RDR2's middle and GTA's last act, moved to the end.

Fix: put Monday and Wednesday together as one scene. Cut Reese's Thursday to four lines: the two entries, the four thousand pounds, the form, the copy to the police. Start Friday's scramble on Thursday night, so leaving Sheila's kitchen leads straight into driving and the phone box. Keep the crabs.

### 5. Take or pay becomes a rule I have solved

What is wrong: taking is rewarded on the page (the hearing, the last Friday's doors, the Straight Life). The hooks are claimed but never shown biting: Danny's fifty-pound note and the Widow's lift lead nowhere written. By hour three I would know to take everything, and the flaw stops being mine.

Where: section 3, "The offers"; Act II scene 2 and Danny's fifty.

Why a player feels it: once the rule is solved, every gift is a menu choice and not a feeling.

Fix: before the hearing, let two taken gifts visibly cost something. Danny's fifty comes back as "you took rent" when he parks on Mickey's rank, and the street repeats it. The Widow's lift is why Ron sits in the back in scene 2.

## 3. Would I single it out to a friend?

No, not as it stands, though it is close. I would mention Ron, the driving lessons and the handbrake as the one thing in it that feels like nothing else, and the Widow as a better opponent than most. But I would not say "play it for the story" while the big turns arrive as speeches, nothing ever puts me in danger, and the last act talks more than it moves. Fix the first two weak points and ask me again.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Ron, the lessons and the handbrake. "I go, you stop" set up at a bollard, paid off a year later on the same road, and the last input being one I can leave alone. "Got it, boss" is the game's last image, and it is earned.
2. The fare book and Sheila. The two lines from one night, one an alibi and one a noose, and then "15.10. Ferry landing. One. No charge." in her kitchen. Her withholding his name until she finds something out for herself is the best-written person here.
3. The Widow who never lies. "Yes. You didn't ask before", and the two widows in the back of the cab with the meter running: "Nothing. That's what I can't get past." An opponent who wins by asking is new, and it is Tom's flaw turned round.
