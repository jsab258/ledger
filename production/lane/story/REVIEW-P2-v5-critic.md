# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 5: a critic's review

This is a small, cold, carefully built story about a bookkeeper, a docker and a fare book, and its best scenes would sit beside the quieter hours of Red Dead Redemption 2. It is not yet the whole thing.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 8.

1. Tom: 7. The flaw (he takes nothing and asks nothing, because a man who owes nothing can pick up his suitcase) is the right flaw and it is played through two real inputs, the offer and the question. But on the page he is still mostly the sum of what others say to him; the Ledger, his only voice, gets two sample lines in sixteen sections.
2. The cast: 9. Sheila, Ron, Ada, Pauline and Terry are people I would talk about afterwards, and Ron and Tom in the passenger seat is a friendship with a real chance of being remembered the way Henry and Hans are. The Bishop, given perhaps four lines, is unforgettable.
3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is the best opponent I have read in a design document this year: lawful, honest, generous, and dangerous because of it. Danny is a set of escalations with a sentimental key turned in him at the end, and Agar is letters until the last act.
4. The central mystery: 8. A truth found by play, on a dial and in a seat runner, that changes what minute two meant. It is fair. Its one weakness is that it can be solved too early by an attentive player (see below).
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The ferry, the walk-round, the priest's forty-one empty envelopes and the carry are a strong first evening, and the first driving lesson is a natural second retold scene. The carry itself is referred to rather than shown here, so I am scoring a promise.
6. Structure and pace: 6. Act II is well shaped around the Fifth. Act III crams a yard siege, a ride-along, a confession, a phone box, a gate scene, an eleven-mile drive, a charge and an ending into two and a half hours, which is exactly where GTA V was punished for hurrying.
7. The middle: 7. The Empty Seat movement is the best stretch in the bible. But five of the six weekly dusk jobs are set off by Danny, and the branch where Ron never leaves is three sentences long.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. The story knows the obvious suspect is a cliché and uses him as bait, which is clever. But "the one person nobody suspects did it" is also a pattern, and the clue that points to her is placed too loudly.
9. The endings: 8. Every ending has a last image, a fare-book line and a Ledger line, Ron's seat is the verdict, and no ending lets the crime become nothing. "Ron drove" and "a date, and nothing under it" are as good as closing lines get.
10. The side stories: 7. Ivy Leadley and her daughter protecting each other from the same known thing is a whole story with a turn. The watch is good. Karen Grummitt is an errand with a wardrobe on it.
11. Tone and humour: 8. The radio as the bar is a genuinely good idea, and the crabs, Terry's fifth word and two old dockers on a day out are funny in the way set pieces are funny, not just lines. The dark scenes are left alone.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. Driving the eleven miles to learn the truth, teaching Ron the skill he later lies with, and a final input whose right use is to do nothing are the best fits of story to hands I have seen in a while. The question mechanic, on which the best ending rests, is not yet designed.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The best ending rests on an input nobody has designed. What is wrong: the better turn of The Kingdom needs Tom to ask Sheila what she wants in her kitchen, and the bible says "the game does not offer the question; the player has to have learned to ask it". Earlier it says the question is "never a prompt" and asked "in his own words". Where: section 3 (the question), section 8 (Wednesday night, Sheila's kitchen). Why a player would feel it: a typed parser will fail on someone's phrasing at the most important moment in the game; a hidden input means most players never find the best ending and feel it was locked behind a secret, not earned. Fix, inside the frame: make "ask what they want" a verb the Ledger gains the morning after the Widow asks Tom at the gate, written in his hand ("She asked. Nobody has."), usable on any named person, never suggested, and with its cost shown (an answer owed). The player then knows the tool exists; whether he thinks to use it on Sheila is the test.

2. The decisive clue is too loud and too early. What is wrong: at the Fifth, Ada says Mickey got "Sheila Dunn through first go in nineteen sixty-one, and she's never sat behind a wheel since". Where: the midpoint, beat four; and section 15's table. Why a player would feel it: naming the one office member who can drive, at the halfway mark of a mystery about a driver, is a spotlight. A sharp player has the answer five hours before scene 15, and the Ron misdirection then reads as the game being slower than him. The bible also lists the licence renewed "every three years since", which is wrong for Britain after 1976, when licences began to run to seventy; a period-minded player will notice. Fix: keep Ada's tea, but have her list three or four people Mickey taught on that dock road, Sheila among them in passing and Julie Kettlewell's mother or Pauline's failed attempts beside her, so the line is a record, not a pointer. Move the "never sat behind a wheel since" remark to scene 10, when Sheila looks at the tally and goes on writing, where it rewards the player who is already thinking. Make the licence one renewed to seventy in the 1970s and kept in a drawer.

3. The last act is a queue of beats. What is wrong: two weeks, two and a half hours, eight beats, four branches of who signs, two closes for Burn Both, and Ellis's charge after Reese's verdict. Where: section 8 throughout, especially Thursday and Friday. Why a player would feel it: the story has been patient for twelve hours and then hurries; the phone box with five coins is an abstraction where every earlier beat was a scene. Fix: give Act III three hours by taking half an hour from the Seat movement, which is the longest. Cut the phone box as a menu and fold it into the Thursday drives: the people Tom once let do him a turn are on his route and speak when he stops. Give the Widow her own dawn and Danny his own lane, not both in one beat.

4. The player who plays the flaw well gets a thinner game. What is wrong: if Tom asks Ron what he wants before the Fifth, Ron never takes the coat, and the bible's whole account of the next five hours is a short paragraph and a line ("The movement plays with the seat full, and it is harder"). The wind-down road in section 11 is a table of six weeks, better, but still a sketch beside the main road. Where: the midpoint ("If the player asked first"), the end of the Empty Seat movement, and section 11. Why a player would feel it: the bar asks that no road be thin, including the road of the player who refuses the crime; here the reward for growth is losing the best set of scenes in the game (the coat, the effigy, two widows in the back) with nothing equal in their place. Fix: write the asked-first movement as scenes. Ron on the door, saying nothing, while Shiner brags unchecked: let the Tangs' queue name the wrong person, Kevin, and let Ron sit in the passenger seat while Kevin is sacked for it.
5. Danny drives the middle on his own. What is wrong: the weekly table gives Danny five of six dusk jobs; the Widow has one; Agar has none until Act III. Danny's arc then ends on a father who talked to a customs man in 1984, a softening that arrives late and from nowhere. Where: the table at the head of Act II, scene 6, scene 17, Thursday at Ropery Lane. Why a player would feel it: one opponent setting the same kind of job each week is the repeated loop players punish in the middle. Fix: give Agar the 19 November dusk job (his surveyor measuring the yard by night, Tom moving the cars), give the Widow the 26 November one, and plant Frank Cammack early: an old man at the back of the gate's sixty, never picked, whom Ron will not look at, seen in week two and named only on the last Thursday.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review?

No, not yet, though it is close. I would single out scenes: Sheila's kitchen, the eleven miles on a dial, Ron braking at the mouth of the row, the bench of dockers, the handbrake left alone. But a review judges the fifteen hours, and on the page the last act hurries, the middle leans on one opponent, the best branch is a sketch and the best ending depends on a mechanic that does not yet exist. Fix those four and this is a yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. Sheila Dunn, whole: a woman who has never said a false thing, a year of good mornings, "New management", the name withheld, a seat still where she left it, and the answer "to have it read, and to write my own name to it".2. The eleven miles as play. The truth is found with a steering wheel and a mileometer, taught to Ron on Sunday mornings, lied with or told straight on the last Friday, with an officer of the department that was robbed in the back. Nobody else is doing this.
3. Ron in the passenger seat as the verdict of every ending, and the Widow who never lies, never threatens and asks Tom what he wants before anyone else does. One friendship and one opponent, both built from the same theme, is rare.
