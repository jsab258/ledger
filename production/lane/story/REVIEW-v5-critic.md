# LEDGER, story bible version 5: a critic's review

## 1. Scores

**Overall: 8**

1. **Tom: 8.** Tom has a want (his own name said with warmth) and a flaw that you play rather than read about: every gift is one press, take it or pay. It is the best idea in the bible for a silent lead. His notebook voice is dry and funny, and his last entries show the change. He loses a point because his "need" is stated more than it is dramatised, and in the last week he is mostly a man answering other people's revelations.
2. **The cast: 9.** Sheila, Ron, Ada, Reese, June and Terry are people you would talk about afterwards. Ron and Tom have a real chance of being remembered the way Henry and Hans are, because the friendship is built from things you do: the passenger seat, the lessons, number nine on the quay wall. Alison is the thinnest of the principals and reads more like a dig partner than a love interest.
3. **The antagonists: 8.** Four different ways of coming at him (offers, letters, fair competition, reasons) is exactly what the bar asks for. Agar in the rain and the two widows in the cab are first-rate scenes. In the last act, though, the rivals shrink to obstacles on an errand map.
4. **The central mystery: 8.** It has a real truth. It is uncovered with the hands (the minute books, the watchman, the boot), and it changes earlier scenes: the letter, "that's the last one, Cyril", the first tea. It pays off at the climax. It is also overbuilt, and too much of it arrives as writing on a page.
5. **The opening and its retold scene: 8.** The first night is tense and specific. The failed carry ends with "Nowak, T. £80" in the debt book, which is a superb way to teach the stakes. Monday asks a player to meet about a dozen named people in eleven minutes of beats, and that is a lot.
6. **Structure and pace: 7.** Using the calendar as the town's clock is smart, and so is giving the rivals turns. But the scenes bunch up between 30 October and 18 November, while the last fortnight of Act II is thin. Then the final week crams four revelations into forty-eight hours.
7. **The middle: 6.** The named scenes are strong. What sits between them is a loop of fares, a rung, the count and Friday, which the bible names but does not write. Several rungs on both ladders have no scene, no person and no turn. That is where the 42-hour "same loop" complaint will come from.
8. **Surprise against cliché: 7.** The VAT inspection, the fare book as the honest witness, and Sheila's line are genuinely new. Against them: the hidden hand behind the hidden hand, an obviously guilty rival who turns out innocent, an eager lackey who lit the fire, a developer with a marina, and an editor who sits on the Board. All of that is familiar.
9. **The endings: 8.** Ada's table in every ending, the last image split between the fare book and the notebook, "Ron takes his test in March", and "Nowak!" all land. The thresholds beneath them ("Ellis's case is answerable", "the street does not hold his rackets as fact") are not ones a player can see from his own play.
10. **The side stories: 8.** Ivy and her daughter protecting each other from a thing both know is the best of them, and keeping it between three people is a brave choice. Ron's pawned watch and Karen's wardrobe are proper stories with turns. Two of the seven (Lilies, the Tangs) are thinner and lean on the main plot.
11. **Tone and humour: 7.** Pauline on the radio, the ham with its own seat, and the crabs on Reese's ride-along are funny, and the dark scenes are kept quiet. But much of the comedy is described rather than written, and the set pieces are few for twenty hours.
12. **Story and play feeding each other: 8.** The offers feed the hearing and the phone box. The lessons feed the mileage book. Every scene is a job with talk where it lands. The weak spots are where the bible promises freedom (every turn can be found early) without writing what happens when a player does find one early.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The truth is overbuilt and arrives as reading.**
*What is wrong.* The truth has too many layers: Ada runs the club; Mickey's debt to Agar; Garbutt and the Coultas brothers; the watchman's lifts; the importer who paid; Ron's silence; Sheila's line; the cut page; Alan Jensen's drowning; Frank Cammack's secret pension. And the key turns arrive as writing on paper: the real book at the midpoint, Danny's page, the minute book, the rent card, the 1989 fare book, the cut page.
*Where.* Sections 7 (scenes 7, 8, 17, 19 and the midpoint), 8 (Tuesday to Friday) and 10.
*Why it would be felt.* In the last four days a player gets Agar's confession, the boot, Sheila's confession, the cut page and Ada's table in a row: "too much talk" and "rushed" at once, in the hours that matter most, in a game that says it is not story-first.
*Fix.* Cut one strand: drop Garbutt and the Coultas brothers, or move Alan's drowning wholly into a side story. Turn two page-reads into deeds: let Tom drive Mickey's old Monday route with Ron and find the Harbour Board doorman who remembers the cab. Move Sheila's confession to Thursday night, so Friday is only the run.

**2. The same trick, played three times.**
*What is wrong.* "She found somebody a reason" is the hinge of the midpoint (Ada with Darren). Then it is the hinge of the climax (Mickey with Ada), and the bible says so: "He did to Ada what Ada did to Tom." Agar's "I did not say how" is a third version. On top of that, Darren as the arsonist is signposted by the bible's own table ("I could do the landing"), and Danny as the innocent obvious suspect is a stock turn.
*Where.* Section 7, the midpoint and scene 12; section 8, Thursday night; section 14.
*Why it would be felt.* A reviewer will say the last twist is the midpoint twist with a dead man's name on it. Once a player has learned the pattern, "somebody behind the somebody" stops surprising him and starts to feel like a rule.
*Fix.* Give Mickey a different sin. Keep the debt and the lifts, but let him have let Ada believe the fire was hers to keep her paying the forty-one out of guilt: guilt as a currency, not a second "reason". And let Darren be found first through something he does (the radios from the lock-up, the too-big coat seen on him at the round) rather than through the setup line.

**3. The middle is a loop with empty rungs.**
*What is wrong.* Act II is ten to thirteen hours. The keeper's ladder has five rungs, and the cold-store contract and "a man of his own at the landing" have no scene, no person and no turn. The straight ladder's "money in a bank" is the mooring, but its trustees are never cast beyond Philip. And from 19 November to 2 December only scenes 18 to 20 happen.
*Where.* Section 7, the opening list and "What a week is"; scenes 9 and 14.
*Why it would be felt.* This is the same-loop complaint: a player in week five, between the anniversary and Garbutt's table, has fares and a count.
*Fix.* Give every rung a named person with a want and a small turn, as scene 9 does with Lily Tang. For the cold store, the foreman's son is one of the Widow's eleven. For the landing, you choose who replaces Cyril, and Cyril has an opinion about it. For the trustees, Edith and Gordon each ask something of him before they will sign. Then move one written scene, for example the bout or Kevin's return, into the week of 19 November.

**4. The "no rails" promise breaks against the fixed reveal.**
*What is wrong.* Section 15 says the fare books sit on a shelf behind Sheila from the first day, and that every turn can be found early. But Ron's confession at the anniversary, Reese's Thursday line and Sheila's Friday all assume the 1989 book has not been read. There is also a continuity hole: if the player takes Ellis's trade on Thursday at midday, she seizes the 1989 books, and then they cannot be in Reese's boot that night.
*Where.* Section 8, Thursday; section 15, "Nothing is on rails".
*Why it would be felt.* A curious player reads the book in week two and either spoils the climax or finds it plays as if he had not: the town knowing what it could not know.
*Fix.* Keep the 1989 books in the locked office with the real book until the midpoint, which fits Sheila's character. Then write the early-reader branch: if Tom reads the 18 November line before Reese does, Sheila's confession comes when he asks her, and Thursday becomes Reese finding the line already known to Tom, which raises the stakes of Friday. Make Ellis's seizure happen after the boot, or have her seize only the copy Reese took. Either way, write which.

**5. The rivals and Ada slip the hook at the end.**
*What is wrong.* In Act III the Widow and Danny become obstacles at the gate hut and the end of Quay Street. Ada, who had Terry maimed, gets "He found me a reason", and the bible says "it is enough". The ending thresholds that decide all this are hidden.
*Where.* Section 8, "Friday, the run"; section 9, "Ada's table" and the turns of each ending.
*Why it would be felt.* Terry's hands deserve more than a line, and a player who never saw "Ellis's case answerable" on any screen will feel the ending was decided for him.
*Fix.* Give the rival who rang Customs one written face-to-face on Friday, inside his errand, where the hook he holds is named. At Ada's table, let Terry be in the room if Tom brought him, or have his five words read back, so that "enough" is something the player decides and not the bible. Put each ending's conditions in the notebook's Open list as plain lines ("The street believes: …", "Ellis can prove: …"), so the player watches them move.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review

Yes. It has two things most open-world crime stories lack: a friendship built with the hands (teaching a fifty-eight-year-old docker to drive, the empty passenger seat), and a climax that is a polite man from Customs reading a fare book. I have not seen the second before, and it pays off the theme cleanly enough to carry a paragraph of any review. Sheila's line, left for a year for "somebody who'd look", is a turn players would talk about. The caveat: the middle as written is a promise, not yet a story, and the last week is overloaded. Fix those and this sits near the stories I compare it with; leave them and a player reaches the brilliant Friday tired.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. **Ron and Tom, built from the passenger seat.** The lessons, number nine on the quay wall, the Widow's offer of the gate, the empty seat Pauline names on air, and the last image of Ron in a vehicle. This is the Henry and Hans of the game.
2. **The fare book and Sheila.** The book that never lied, the line she wrote herself, the year she waited, "Mr Nowak" and then "Tom", and Reese reading "Somebody drove a long way for nothing." This is the spine, and it pays off every set-up in section 14.
3. **The offers.** Take it or pay, with no marker of which gift has a hook, and the debt coming due at the hearing and in the phone box. It turns a silent lead's flaw into play, and it makes the endings feel earned.
