# LEDGER story bible, version 3: script editor's note

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7

1. Tom: 7. The want (his own name said with warmth), the flaw (he will take nothing) and the fear (the suitcase) are clear and, unusually, played as mechanics. But his change is a meter filled by play; the flaw never costs him something in the moment, only at a tally later.
2. The cast: 8. Sheila, Ron, Ada, Pauline, Norman, Hal and Ivy are specific and distinct. Alison is the weak one: sharp, then gone after the midpoint.
3. The antagonists: 7. Danny is excellent, Agar in the rain is the best single scene in the bible, and the Widow's ladder of offers is a fresh way to threaten. Agar's hired men at the mooring are generic, and the promised three-way play between the rivals is asserted, never staged.
4. The central mystery: 7. It has a truth, it is found by play, and it turns the kind uncle into the author, which recolours the letter, night seven and the second tea. The machinery underneath it leaks (see point 1), and a careful player holds the answer two weeks before the climax that is meant to reveal it.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The failed carry ending in "Nowak, T. £82" will be retold; the priest's forty-one empty envelopes is a perfect line. Week one is dense with talk, and Tuesday to Thursday is a list, not a shape.
6. Structure and pace: 6. The calendar pulling a slow player forward is right, and the staggered rival peaks give the middle a profile. Act III is overloaded: the same truth is confirmed five times in five days, and every loose end arrives in the same week by convenience.
7. The middle: 7. The Fifth is a real midpoint and the second half has strong scenes (two widows, Remembrance, the lock-up). The straight road is thin next to the keeper's road and its arithmetic does not work.
8. Surprise against cliché: 6. The VAT man as nemesis, Danny's page and Agar's ruin are fresh. The two main turns, the sweet old lady who runs it and the dead benefactor who did it, are familiar shapes, and the first is signposted hard.
9. The endings: 7. The last images are lovely ("41 out. T.N.", "Stayed", "Ferry landing. One. No charge.") and Ron's seat as the verdict on the friendship is a fine idea. The mechanism that decides the climax, Reese weighing whoever walks in, does not hold, and the love interest has no last scene.
10. The side stories: 8. Ivy and her daughter protecting each other from the same knowledge is the best writing in the bible; Karen, Hal and the mooring are proper stories. The Tangs' is thinner.
11. Tone and humour: 7. Pauline as the bar works, the crabs at the ride-along are genuinely funny, and the Ledger has a voice. Much of it depends on delivery the bible can only promise.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 7. The small offers paying off at the hearing and the phone box is exactly what the bar asks for. But the mystery's key beats are document searches no listed system carries, and the climax is decided by talk, against the bible's own rule.

## 2. The five weakest points

**1. The fare book cannot be the secret it needs to be, and Sheila must have seen it.**
What is wrong: the bible's last truth is that Mickey wrote the free Saturday runs and the fire-night run (twenty to midnight, car two, two fares, no charge) in the fare book, "where nobody would look". But Sheila looks. She spots Kevin's short mileage "in the book before anyone says it"; for eleven weeks in 1989 she would have totalled a free Saturday run, then a no-charge job from the landing on the fire night. Either she knew, or she is not who the bible says she is. Separately, the fare book proves the importer was a cab account customer, not that he paid his rent to the club; Reese's "a good payer, right up to the week of his fire" proves nothing about protection money.
Where: section 8, "The week before: the two books"; section 10, layer five; section 4, Sheila.
Why it matters: a sharp player asks "why did Sheila never notice?" at the exact moment the story wants awe.
Fix: make Sheila's blindness a chosen one, and make it the last turn. The Saturday runs are in her hand: Mickey told her they were a kindness to Norman Dent's wife, and she wrote "no charge" without asking. The fire-night run is the only one in Mickey's hand, added after she had gone home. On the last Friday she says she has known for a year that one line was not hers, and that is why she kept the cut page unread. Her withholding of his name then has a second reason under it, without changing a line of her as written. And have the importer settle his rent and his cab account together, monthly, on the one account card, so the fare book's "paid" really is the rent.

**2. The climax is decided by character witnesses, and the truth is told five times over.**
What is wrong: on the last Friday Reese "takes a word" from whoever comes in and then closes his notebook as "in order". A VAT officer does not settle suppressed takings on a neighbour's say-so, and the bible's own rule is "talk decides nothing alone". Meanwhile Act III confirms Mickey's guilt six times: the two books, Agar, Reese twice, Les and the cut page, and the dead man's last word adds nothing new.
Where: section 8, Tuesday to Friday.
Why it matters: the climax re-tests whether people like Tom, which the hearing already tested, and the final revelation lands as a repeat.
Fix: each call from the phone box must produce a piece of paper that answers one item Reese has named: Edith's folder (the society's books or the round's receipts, showing where the cash went); Ron's mileage notes from the passenger seat; Kevin's original sheets; Ellis's note on the envelope taken on night one; Christine Pickering's copy of the register entry. Reese is persuaded by documents, and who will bring them is the campaign Tom played. Cut Les's confession down to motive only (who paid him, nothing about the "old girl"), and make the cut page new: the importer's real name, where he went, and the sum Mickey reckoned he owed him. "People are owed" then pays off literally, and the endings gain a choice: whether Tom pays the one man Mickey robbed.

**3. The straight road is thin and does not add up.**
What is wrong: the road for the player who refuses the crime has about four scenes of its own (June's five lines, the names, Sheila's £20, the meeting) against a full keeper's ladder. Its arithmetic is impossible: forty-one at £4 is £164 a week of clean money from a firm whose honest takings "just cover the wages", which then loses car two and Terry. Each rival is said to go for a rung, never shown doing it, and the promise that the rivals move against one another has no scene.
Where: section 7, scenes 1, 3(S), 9(S), 14(S); section 5, "The three together".
Why it matters: a player who refuses the crime will feel he picked the lesser game.
Fix: endow the society with capital, not takings. The mooring (side story 9) is the obvious source: sell the right of way to Agar or the Widow for a lump sum, and that sale is the bank-account rung. Agar going for that sale, the Widow going for the list, and Cammack going for the cab money that pays the trustees' costs gives each rival a rung in a written scene. Then stage one three-way scene through Garbutt where a page of Mickey's book can be sold to one rival against another.

**4. Alison is set up as the love story and given no pay-off.**
What is wrong: four scenes before the midpoint, a supper on the Fifth, then one question in the last week. She has no ending beat, appears only as an option ("Alison, or Ron") in one plain turn, and her editor on the Harbour Board is never resolved.
Where: section 4, "Alison and Tom"; section 9.
Why it matters: cut from two romances to one "whole" one, she is still half a romance.
Fix: give her a turn of her own after the midpoint: the night she must choose between her editor and the story, and asks Tom to decide what she prints about Mickey. Give her a last scene in every close, staying or leaving by which drawer Tom ended up in, with what she printed in the last image.

**5. The Ada turn is guessable, and the dig is ahead of the climax.**
What is wrong: an old woman at a window who sees everything, takes an envelope "like the rest", asks about her alley, lodges the reporter and invites the stranger to tea will be read by many players as the boss by the end of week one. And the anniversary card gives away Mickey's authorship on 18 November.
Where: section 6 (Tuesday, Friday round, the tea); section 7, scene 17.
Why it matters: the bar's commonest complaint is a plot guessed early.
Fix: give Cyril one public scene in which he overrules Ada's wishes to her face (he cuts Winifred's envelope over her objection, in Ron's hearing), so his command is seen. Offer a second plausible head, such as Garbutt. And let the importer's card at the anniversary prove only that he paid somebody, unsigned; the initials M.S. are found only when the fare book's account page is set beside it in Act III.

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Ellis's inspector closed the fire "as the owner's own work", yet the importer "took the insurance". No insurer pays a man the police think burned his own premises. Fix: the file was closed as undetermined; the street, not the police, says it was the owner.
2. Mickey owed Agar £4,000 "against the yard", but "nothing had been put in writing". If Agar held a charge he would enforce it on the estate, not object to licences. Fix: a handshake loan, unsecured, which is why Agar needs the book.
3. Where did the importer's rent go after Mickey wrote "refused"? Fix: it went to Agar as interest; say so on the cut page.
4. The importer told the tallyman he had paid; Cyril never passes it on and nobody asks him. Fix: a short Act III scene where Cyril admits he chose Mickey's word over a stranger's.
5. June: works nights at the town's infirmary, yet "goes back over the water"; Mickey posted her letters from the far bank and they came back "not known". And "nobody knows why she has not gone home" is never paid. Fix: say she lives across the water and works at its hospital; she stays because of the will and the three envelopes she is paying.
6. Men at the stoppage stand in front of a car "that cannot kill them and know it". That is the game's rule talking. Fix: they stand because they know Tom's line and his name.
7. A 1990 member of the public could not read prices paid from a land register. Fix: the Harbour Board's own minute consenting to the transfer, with the consideration recorded, which Christine keeps.
8. A civic Harbour Board wreath at a Catholic priest's chapel is odd for a British Remembrance Sunday. Fix: the service is at the dockside memorial, with Father Walsh reading the Hook's names by custom.
9. Les and Agar both arrive in inspection week by coincidence. Fix: both come because the Customs letter is street talk; say so.
10. "Agar is his, held by two pages", though Agar is ruined by 31 December. Fix: Tom holds Agar's Harbour Board vote, not his money.

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review

No. Not yet. I would praise Ron in the passenger seat, the failed carry, Agar in the rain and a VAT inspector as the last antagonist. But a story is singled out when its mystery is airtight and its climax tests what the player built; here the fare book's secret has a visible hole, the last week repeats its revelation, and the climax turns on goodwill rather than on what Tom did with the books. Fix the first two weakest points and this moves to an 8 and close to the line.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. Ron and Tom: the passenger seat, the three driving lessons (the third "not funny"), the empty seat on air, and his seat in the last vehicle as the verdict. It is the relationship people will talk about.
2. The flaw played as a mechanic: every small thing offered, take it or pay, coming back at the hearing and in the phone box. It is character made of play, and it is rare.
3. The fare book as alibi and noose, and the failed carry that puts "Nowak, T. £82" in the debt book in the first hour. The plainest book being the only true one is the story's best idea and its spine.
