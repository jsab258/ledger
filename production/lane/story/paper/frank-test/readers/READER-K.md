Mean 7.1. Nothing reaches 9: no scene has the scale or the physical cost of the reference bar, and every one dilutes its main beat with a menu.

**01.** Strongest: the timed fare on an open channel, ending backwards down the tenfoot ("PAULINE: One fifty-two, by mine. ... Well."), and Sheila's "I'm writing down that it was there." Weakest: it is a taxi tutorial with a stopwatch, and Ron's flat hand does the steering thinking. The action carries the meaning: Frank earns his name by driving.

**02.** Strongest: Darren's "By Christmas. He never said which." and the envelope's pink carbon. Weakest: the want comes as a speech ("And when they say Mickey's, they'll mean me."), a stock ambition aria told to a man who answers "Envelope still wants carrying." The player's choice (carry, refuse, look inside) carries mechanics more than meaning tonight. The meaning is deferred.

**05.** Strongest: the hatchback on the coping, keys on the bare third hook, "Car three. Two pound forty to the quay." Danny's real delight ("He's moved my motor.") makes the enemy likeable. Weakest: Ron's beckoning palms make it half Ron's deed, and the scene admits it ("Was it Ron?"). The action carries the meaning completely.

**08.** Strongest: the pen turning its back on Ron one man at a time ("By the time the leaf is on its stop, fourteen backs are to the road."), and the packers' upholstery banter. Weakest: six routes (pay, bluff, push, send, go round, walk) flatten it. Frank is a bystander to Stathers and Ron ("There's one Frank on this gate"). The meaning lives only on the "Send Ron" route.

**10.** Strongest: the shot taken for a rocket ("Somebody says 'Ooh.' Nothing breaks."), Ron telling the buildings who they are, and the "Stop" word that turns a getaway into a rescue along the coping. Weakest: the gun-under-fireworks device is stock, and the old man is a stranger to the player when he falls. The action carries the meaning, through "Stop" and the drive.

**12.** Strongest: "Handbrake's yours, boss." The car strains against a brake Ron set, and only the player's release moves it: the cleanest join of hand and meaning in the set. Weakest: the question rests on a book line this selection never shows, and "Book says a man can put in for his test after five" is muddled. The action carries the meaning.

**14.** Strongest: Ron spelling it right ("Nowak. N, O, W, A, K."), "Hang that on my hook, boss.", and the lights-out reverse counting bollards. Weakest: it is three scenes in a coat (the fetch, the confession, buying a way out) with prices and options, and the loyal man taking the fall is a familiar turn. The action carries the meaning, though the menu crowds it.

**15.** Strongest: the cork, "Eight foot four, less five foot six, less me.", then the arm between wing and post. Darren's pound. Weakest: a crowded roll-call of half-met names, Kevin's mileage grievance arriving cold, and the staged unscrewed bulb. The action carries the meaning when the player corks the gate and steps into the gap.

**17.** Strongest: car two put behind the hearse as the family car, the young man saying "Cammack's?" to a coffin, the Man in Black's "Seven. ... Three firms, and one car of family." Weakest: the tea becomes a trading table (paper, man, load, torch) with Agar as a cipher. The procession carries the meaning. The tea is mechanics.

**19.** Strongest: Fewson's "No. I want to see what you do." turning Danny's week-one line on him, and car two on the same coping. Weakest: a finale fist-fight over a book, outcomes in a list, Danny's turn arriving from offstage, and the real reckoning (Customs at five) not here. The action carries mechanics more than meaning.

## 3. The three weakest scenes

1. **19 The quay at four.** The one change: make Danny's last move a deed the player sees him prepare across the act, and let the fight's stake be Ron's line, not the book. Danny should offer to hand Ellis the eleven nights *instead of* Ron, so beating him costs Ron something. As written it is a mirror of car three with fists.
2. **17 The room at Grace's.** The one change: cut the tea to the torch and one thing on the cloth. The procession is the scene. The trading of paper, man and load reads as a negotiation menu, and Agar never becomes a man.
3. **02 The ask on the rank.** The one change: cut the speech and let Frank's want come out as an act on the rank, for instance refusing the envelope's terms to the man at the landing in person, with Darren's line kept as the reply. "And when they say Mickey's, they'll mean me" tells what 05 then shows.

## 4. Voices

Ron is unmistakable ("Aye.", "Nowt coming.", "Coming up on the chandler's."). So is Danny: playful, "nephew", "I've had most things.". Pauline is dry radio wit. The trouble is that almost everyone shares one author's register: short, dry, numerical, a line with a sting at the end. "It's brass. It'll keep." (Ron), "I've waited longer for worse." (Lee), "That's us level. I didn't enjoy it." (the Widow), "Mine's Fridays." (Terry). Frank's counting habit has spread to the whole cast (Sheila's sums, Danny's "Eleven nights", Stathers's "Five pound a car").

Two pairs sound alike:
- **Sheila and Pauline.** Two deadpan women behind one counter ("Is it. I've never counted." against "Board's for cars. Book's for fares.").
- **Kevin and Terry.** Both old drivers whose lines would swap without notice ("Six year." / "Mine's Fridays. It's always been Fridays.").

The Widow and Garbutt also share a measured, settled courtesy.

## 5. Clichés, stock lines and turns seen coming

- 02: the "by Christmas I'll run this town" speech under a street lamp.
- 10: the gunshot taken for a firework. The dying old man asking the firm's name. Nobody knows his name ("Nobody on the Hook ever has."), seen coming.
- 14: the loyal old retainer taking the fall for the boss, seen coming from 12's "It's my two letters at the end of that line." Ron spelling Nowak right is foreseeable once the misspelling runs, though it still lands.
- 14: the bent detective's "Files lose things. Two hundred." and Ellis's "I write everything down; that's what you're paying for."
- 17: rival heads dividing ground at a funeral, seated by rank.
- 19: the lieutenant turning on his boss with the boss's own line, flagged in 05 ("in the yard, or on the quay at the end, he is there with this morning on him"). The finale fist-fight at the water's edge.
- 01 and 02: the button tic announced rather than discovered ("the player will learn what it means").
- 14: "You've took the one thing I had to give." is a touch on the nose.

## 6. Rule breaks and confusion

No breaks found: no alcohol (Stathers brings "a flask of tea"), no gambling, no children, nobody asks Frank's past ("Both names are mine" hints at it, and nobody follows it up).

Confusing to a reader without the plan:
- The Widow is Maureen Jensen. These ten scenes only let a reader infer it.
- Two Franks (Stathers and Nowak). It is used on purpose but costs a beat.
- Unexplained terms: "Frank holds the third rung", "the day of looking", Garbutt's three jobs, "Haddock's dear", "Mickey's watch", "Frank signed first", "the brass key he never gave back", "Lily Tang counts out one of number nine", the ghost name under CAR 2.
- The Widow's four cases. Taken from her in 10, they "came to my door on Thursday night" in 17. The return is never placed.
- The driving test. "Book says a man can put in for his test after five" is not a British rule. It needs to be a clearly fictional instructor's book or cut.
- Ron driving alone on L-plates. The text marks it ("The book says he may not be."), which is good, but in 14 the constable writes down only the string on the boot.

## 8. Distance from the bar

Line by line the writing is close to the bar: observed, economical, funny, and full of objects that carry weight (the third hook, the tally, the handbrake, the torch, the cap). Lesson five and the night between would sit near RDR2's quieter best. What keeps the set at seven to eight, not nine to ten, is that the story's heaviest deeds belong to Ron, not Frank. Ron walks the gate, drives the Landing, sets the brake, walks into the station and writes the statement. Frank's part in those moments is mostly to pick a line from a list or decide which of Ron's sacrifices runs. Frank's own deeds are skilled driving feats, often steered by Ron's hand. Arthur's diagnosis happens in Arthur's body, from Arthur's collection. Here the diagnosis happens to Ron. Second to that is diffusion: five to seven variants per scene and some thirty named townsfolk thin every peak.

## 9. The people, by their own deeds

1. **Ron Kirby.** He makes the two scenes players will remember longest: the handbrake he will not release and the statement he walks in to make. He also walks the gate leaf open in front of the pen and drives the Landing with four lessons.
2. **Frank Nowak.** He has more retellable deeds than anyone: backwards down the tenfoot, Danny's car on the edge, corking the gate, the arm in the gap, the blind reverse along nine bollards, the family car behind the hearse. But they are feats more than choices with a cost, and several are guided by Ron.
3. **Danny Cammack.** The hatchback across the rank, reading the day book on the counter, laughing at his own car on the edge, taking the book to the coping. A rival who acts every time he appears.
4. **Kevin Needham.** He calls the settle in the yard with car one's key and orders "Lift her out." It is the one middle-week revolt that comes from a man's own grievance.
5. **The old man from the landing.** One deed, unforgettable: a pencil torch in a gunman's eyes and "Not on here."
6. **Craig Fewson.** The seat run back two notches, "No. I want to see what you do.", and driving off in Danny's car. A small arc, carried entirely by deeds.
7. **Sheila Dunn.** She writes things down ("cash introduced, £23.50") and that matters to the plot, but it is record, not deed. Her one push is the rent.
8. **The Widow (Maureen Jensen).** She drives nothing in these scenes. Her acts are offstage or by proxy (Stathers's gate, the van driver sent away), and at the table her loudest move is setting a milk jug on a sheet of paper.
