**01.** Strongest: the backwards run down the tenfoot, Ron's flat hand, and Sheila's "I'm not taking it. I'm writing down that it was there," which pays off on the last Saturday. Weakest: eleven named people in ten minutes, and "Both names are mine" is too written. The hands carry the meaning: the two minutes are his promise, kept or broken on an open channel.

**02.** Strongest: the envelope, a re-stuck electricity-board envelope holding the carbon that later convicts someone. Weakest: the speech. "And when they say Mickey's, they'll mean me" is a want *announced*, the hands idle. Darren's "He never said which" rescues it. The player only chooses from a menu; the mechanics carry it, not the meaning.

**05.** Strongest: Ron beckoning the car to the coping, "Whoa," the wheels turning over the water, and the keys going on the bare hook as CAR 3. Danny's laughter is real and earns his return. Weakest: Sheila's fare sum ("sixteen at ten pence. Two pound forty") is cute. Frank risks little bodily. The action carries the meaning outright.

**08.** Strongest: tally 39 held out across Frank, and "Send Ron", where "fourteen backs are to the road". That price is paid by another man, chosen by the player. The packers' "Mickey had upholstery" is the best comedy here. Weakest: the five-way branch spreads it thin; the fight route is a stub. The meaning is in the choice, less in the hands.

**10.** Strongest: "Along the quay two hundred faces go up to the sky to see where it will break. Somebody says 'Ooh.' Nothing breaks." Then Ron, alone at eleven miles an hour: "That's the ticket window." Weakest: the old man is a shape we have barely met, and the taped revolver is stock. "Stop", the carry and the drive on the coping carry the meaning.

**12.** Strongest: "Handbrake's yours, boss." He put the brake on and will not take it off, so the player's release *is* the answer. Weakest: a reader cannot tell what the line in the book says ("If the book has the envelope… If the book has a fare… If the book has Sheila's line"). The scene's force rests on a bookkeeping state the player may not hold. The hands carry it.

**14.** Strongest: Ron spelling "Nowak. N, O, W, A, K", and "Hang that on my hook, boss," the player made to take the cap or leave the arm out. Weakest: the night becomes a shop: Rita, Ombler, Wardell, Ellis at "eight hundred pounds", each a price list. The dark wall run is a repeat of 01's reverse. The deeds carry the meaning; the bribe menu carries mechanics.

**15.** Strongest: corking the gate with car two, then the arm between wing and post: "There is a sound." Darren's pound and Terry's "Mine's Fridays." Weakest: Kevin's grievance ("forty-two mile a week") arrives unplanted in these pages, and Kevin's rule needs explaining before it bites. The body carries the meaning.

**17.** Strongest: the one-armed coat. "Leave him. He can ask." / "Mrs Jensen. Would you." / "That's one." Also the car behind the hearse, and "Our friend from the landing". Weakest: the middle is a token exchange (paper, man, load, torch), three heads handed a dish each, and Agar and the Widow are thin. The coat is in the hands; the cloth is a menu.

**19.** Strongest: Fewson's "No. I want to see what you do," Danny's own line turned on him, and "Leave my mirror alone" as Fewson drives off. Weakest: the climax is a fist fight over a ledger, with "Eleven nights with R.K." an abstraction in place of a person. "You'd not. Mickey'd not" is muddy. Mostly mechanics: a fist fight with a menu after it.

## 2. The fifteen principles

## 3. The three weakest scenes, and the change that gains most

1. **02 The ask on the rank.** Take away the speech and give the want to his hands. Let Frank do one thing on the rank that only a man who means to run the Hook would do with the envelope, and let Darren's joke land on the deed. "Thirty years. Two cars, a rent book, and this. Not for me." is the player being told the game's goal by its lead.
2. **19 The quay at four.** Replace the fist fight's stake (a count of initials in a book) with a deed only Frank would do, at the cost of something he built. For example, he puts car two, book and all, into the dock in front of Ellis's windscreen, the car three trick turned on himself. The rhyme with week one would then be the climax, not an echo.
3. **17 The room at Grace's.** Cut the three-token bargaining to one object: the torch. Every head wants it, and the paper, the man and the load become what each offers for it. The coat beat proves the scene is best when one small physical thing decides who stands where.

## 4. Voices

Ron, Danny and Pauline would be known with names hidden. Ron has "Aye", "nowt" and "Coming up on…". Danny has "nephew" and runs on. Pauline has deadpan radio counting. Frank is recognisable by his arithmetic ("Eight foot four, less five foot six, less me"). The rest share one dry, clipped Northern register, where everybody has a neat last word.

Alike:
- Sheila and the Widow: "I'll not tell the landlord" against "That was never ordered. I'll say so once… and not again."
- Gordon and Stathers: "Aye. They all do. It's got his name on it" against "I know what you're called. There's one Frank on this gate."

Kevin and Terry also blur.

## 5. Clichés, stock lines, turns seen coming

- 02: the "by Christmas I'll run this town" declaration.
- 10: the gunshot taken for a firework. The dying stranger's last word is the firm's name ("Mickey's?"). The L-plate learner who must drive in the emergency is seen coming from the moment the plates appear.
- 10, 17: the old docker's code: "Not on here. Thirty year. Not once."
- 14: the loyal old hand takes the fall and will not name names. The bent detective with a price: "I write everything down; that's what you're paying for." "Nobody on the Hook has spelt it right before without being told" is foreseeable once the misspelling joke had run three times.
- 14 and 01: the reverse run down a narrow way, done twice.
- 17: the funeral where the crime heads sit at one table.
- 19: the henchman turning on his boss with the boss's own line. It is well done, but signalled since 05's seat and mirror. "I've wanted to since the hatchback" is stock.
- 05 and 19: a car on the quay edge, twice.

## 6. Rule breaks and confusions

No breach of the content rules found. Food at the funeral tea, no drink. Tobacco only. "The lad" means a young man. Dates check: the Saturday display on 10 November 1990 sits before Remembrance Sunday, and Saturday 1 December 1990 is right.

Confusing to a reader without the plan:
- "the third rung"; "the three jobs Garbutt set"; "Haddock's dear"; "a firm with no night money"; "Mickey's watch redeemed"; "Lily Tang counts out one of number nine. 'One.'"; the brass key's history (scene 06 is unseen); who the Man in Black is.
- That the Widow is Maureen Jensen has to be inferred from 08 and 17.
- What the day-book line actually says, and who wrote which version, across 02, 12, 14 and 19. The game's spine depends on it.
- Two Franks at the gate (deliberate, but it costs clarity).
- The goal is "By Christmas", but the game ends on 1 December.
- Wardell, Ombler, Agar and Ellis arrive with no introduction in these pages, which is expected in a selection.

## 7. Recognition

- *The Long Good Friday*: a British port gangster's ambition and the old order turning on him. Distant, in mood only.
- *The Godfather*: the funeral table of heads, and the loyal man who keeps silent. Distant.
- *Mafia: The City of Lost Heaven*: a cab man drawn into an outfit. Close at premise level only.
- *The Italian Job*: a vehicle hanging over an edge. A loose visual echo.

The gunshot under fireworks is a common film device, not one source. Nothing is copied.

## 8. Distance from the bar

These are good scenes, with three (05, 10, 14) a good shipped crime game would be proud of. At their best moments ("Nothing breaks"; "Handbrake's yours, boss"; "Hang that on my hook, boss") they brush the bar's tenderness. They are still a mark or two short of Red Dead Redemption 2's best. What stands most in the way is that the story's spine is a line of ink. Its heaviest pressure runs through the day book (who brought the envelope, "eleven nights with R.K.", which version Ron was told), and the reader cannot hold that state and the player cannot feel it in his hands. Arthur's scenes rest on a body and a person. Here the climax and the breach are about paperwork. The branching menus (bribes, tokens, five ways through a gate) dilute the single strong act each scene already has.

## 9. The people, by their own deeds

1. **Frank**: the backwards tenfoot, Danny's car on the coping, the cork in his own gate, the arm in the gap, the car behind the hearse. Retellable every time, though mostly feats of driving.
2. **Ron**: walks into the station unasked and signs "I MAKE THIS STATEMENT OF MY OWN FREE WILL". Brings the L-plate car to the brow alone. Opens the gate and loses the pen.
3. **Danny**: parks across the rank, sits on the counter reading the book, takes car two to the edge with the book.
4. **Kevin**: calls the settle and orders "Lift her out", the one supporting man who starts a set piece himself.
5. **The old man**: takes the gunman's sleeve with a pencil torch ("Not on here"). One deed, and it carries two scenes.
6. **Fewson**: "No. I want to see what you do," then drives off in Danny's car. Small and late, but his own.
7. **Pauline**: counts the two minutes on an open channel, which makes the opening a public bet.
8. **Sheila**: writes "cash introduced, £23.50". Her deed is a record, not an act.
9. **The Widow**: a milk jug set on Agar's paper and an exile ordered offstage. The quietest of the named powers, and it shows.
