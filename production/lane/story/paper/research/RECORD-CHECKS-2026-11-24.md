## Ron on remand: yes, with two corrections

- The offence. Being "in any way knowingly concerned in any fraudulent evasion" of duty on goods is an offence under the Customs and Excise Management Act 1979, s.170(2), punishable on summary conviction by up to 6 months or three times the value of the goods, and on indictment, as enacted, by "imprisonment for a term not exceeding 2 years". The Finance Act 1988, s.12, substituted "7 years" for "2 years" in s.170(3)(b) for offences committed after that Act was passed, so in 1990 the maximum on indictment was 7 years. It is imprisonable either way, so the Bail Act's full grounds apply. [s.170 as enacted, read 24 Nov 2026: https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1979/2/section/170/enacted/data.pdf; Finance Act 1988 s.12 as enacted, read 24 Nov 2026: https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/39/section/12/enacted]
- Correction 1: it is a Customs case, not only a police one. Proceedings for a customs offence "shall not be instituted except by order of the Commissioners", and magistrates' proceedings run "in the name of an officer" of Customs (s.145(1) and (2)); a court may still deal with a man already detained (s.145(6)). Read from the 1992 text of Part XI, so the 1990 wording is inferred unchanged. [https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1979/2/part/XI/1992-12-01/data.pdf, read 24 Nov 2026] A caveat: the story's charge is conspiracy to evade duty, which is charged under the Criminal Law Act 1977, s.1 (from memory, not read), not under s.170 itself, and s.145 covers offences under the customs Acts; so what was read does not settle whether Customs or the police would lay a conspiracy charge. In practice Customs brought duty cases (judgement). The owner's wording of the charge stays. In the story: DS Ellis takes Ron's statement and holds him; a Customs officer, whose department brings duty cases, lays the charge. The same department sends the VAT letter that opens Act III, which ties the two.
- Bail. A court "need not" grant bail to a man charged with an imprisonable offence where there are "substantial grounds for believing" he would "interfere with witnesses or otherwise obstruct the course of justice, whether in relation to himself or any other person" (Bail Act 1976, Schedule 1, Part I, para 2). Para 9 adds the seriousness of the offence, his "associations" and "the strength of the evidence". [https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1976/63/schedule/1/enacted?view=plain, read 24 Nov 2026] A man who confesses to years of a smuggling arrangement and says he will not name who took the goods at the landing is a fair case for refusal on the "any other person" limb, with the men at the landing still at large. The Act does not name refusing to name others as a ground; the reading is ours (judgement, not verified by a case). A 58-year-old docker of fixed address and no record could just as fairly get bail; the frame should not say it was certain.
- How long at a time. Remand in custody was for at most eight clear days at a time (the project's custody research, from the Magistrates' Courts Act 1980, s.128). The 28-day remand (s.128A, from the Criminal Justice Act 1988, s.155) applied only "in an area specified in the order" made by Parliament, and was widened only from 2 December 1991. [s.128A, 1993 text, read 24 Nov 2026: https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1980/43/section/128A/1993-09-20?view=plain; s.155: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/33/section/155/enacted] So in Meridian in 1990: arrested and charged on the Saturday of the night between (17 November), kept in the police cells over the weekend, before the magistrates at their first sitting on Monday 19 November, remanded for eight clear days, to be brought back by Wednesday 28 November, and remanded again then, past the close on Saturday 1 December. One remand hearing falls in week 7.
- How long men waited, and visits. The average time on remand before the court's finding was "about 53 days for males" (provisional 1989 figures), so two game weeks is short of the usual. Unconvicted men had "brief daily visits ... limited to 15 minutes"; from 1990 governors were given discretion to allow longer, less frequent visits instead. [HC Deb 3 May 1990 vol 171 c658W, read 24 Nov 2026: https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written_answers/1990/may/03/remand-prisoners] Scene 18's fifteen minutes on any weekday matches. Crowding was a live complaint in 1990: an MP said remand men were sometimes kept in cells "up to 23 hours a day" and "forced to share cells with three or more others"; the minister disputed it. [HC Deb 14 June 1990 vol 174 cc451-2: https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1990/jun/14/remand-prisoners]
- Own clothes. Unconvicted men wearing their own clothes is in the 1999 Prison Rules (rule 23); the 1964 rules in force in 1990 were not reached. Unverified for 1990; scene 18 keeps it as likely.

## A minicab on a street rank: no, and that is useful

- A car "standing or plying for hire in any street" is treated as a hackney carriage (Town Police Clauses Act 1847, s.38), and driving, standing or plying for hire without a hackney licence is an offence (s.45; the fine was raised by later Acts, the 1990 level not confirmed). [1991 text, read 24 Nov 2026: https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/10-11/89/crossheading/hackney-carriages/1991-02-01/data.pdf] A minister in 1984: "plying for hire other than by a licensed hackney carriage is an offence." [HC Deb 27 Feb 1984 c6W: https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1984/feb/27/minicabs] Letting any car other than a hackney wait on a hackney stand is a separate offence under the 1976 Act, s.64 (read from a summary of the text, not verbatim: unverified wording).
- Where licensing applied. Private hire licensing applied only where the district council had adopted Part II of the Local Government (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1976 (s.45(2)). [2009 text, read 24 Nov 2026: https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1976/57/part/II/2009-06-01/data.pdf] Canon has a council that renews Mickey's licence and can hold a hearing, so Meridian has adopted it (decided by this lane).
- Its own door: uncertain. Nothing read forbids a minicab waiting off the street for jobs booked through it. But a car standing for hire "in any street" is caught by s.38, and the courts' test for plying for hire, endorsed in Rose v Welbeck Motors (1962), was whether the vehicle was "on view" and the owner or driver expressly or impliedly invited the public to use it (a solicitors' summary, read 24 Nov 2026: https://www.bsbsolicitors.co.uk/?p=1767; the judgment itself not read). A car waiting at the kerb outside a cab office with its name in the window could be plying for hire if anybody could walk up and take it. So Mickey's rank is lawful only as long as nobody is taken off it without a booking, and it is a grey place in law that the council chose not to test while Agar renewed the licence unasked. Canon's rank stays. every booking must be written in the operator's record "before the commencement of each journey" (s.56(2)), and the operator must produce it on request. The council may suspend or revoke a vehicle licence for "any offence under, or non-compliance with" the 1847 Act or Part II "by the operator or driver; or ... any other reasonable cause" (s.60), with appeal to the magistrates; drivers' and operators' licences likewise (ss.61 and 62, read from a summary).
- So: Mickey's "rank" is the firm's own standing at its door, where cars wait for jobs Pauline books: lawful only while every job is booked before it starts, and even then a grey place that a council with a reason could test. The ferry rank is a hackney stand. A minicab on it, taking the boat queue, is plying for hire: an offence each time, and a reason the council can use to suspend Mickey's licence. Winning the ferry rank is a crime, which suits a crime game, and every fare taken there has to go in the book as phoned in, which is the line in the book in small.
