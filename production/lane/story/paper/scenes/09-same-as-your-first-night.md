# 09. Same as your first night

- Where it sits: Act IIa, week three: the first wash. The line from week one comes back as the firm's habit, and the player sees the red book.
- What wakes it: for the first time there is money in the tin that no fare explains (the first round's first collection, a run's cut, a front's share); it is evening; Sheila is cashing up; Frank is at the counter.
- Who must be present: Sheila Dunn. Ron Kirby, on the door, seen through the glass. Fallback: none. If the player keeps away, the money sits in the tin unbanked and Sheila asks on each evening that follows.

Frank is trying to get thirty-five pounds of night money turned into fares. He will give up the say over whose name stands beside it, which he did not know was his to give.

## Staging

The office, a little after six. Dark outside, the strip light on, the street lamp over the rank making a second, yellower light on the glass. Ron's back is against the wall by the door.

On the counter the tin stands open. Beside it are two piles. One is the day's takings in Sheila's bank bags. The other is thirty-five pounds, three fives and two tens, folded once, a little damp, which came in at dusk in a man's cap.

The day book is open at today. Clipped to a board are the cars' mileage sheets, each morning's and each night's reading in the drivers' own pencil.

Sheila's handbag is on the desk behind her, open. Her knitting is in it.

The camera is across the counter, as it was on the first morning, and stands in the same place on purpose. When the book is turned round it reads over Frank's hands.

## The scene

Sheila bags the last of the fares, cranks the machine, pins the slip. Then she looks at the second pile for a while without touching it.

SHEILA: That's not fares.

She pulls the mileage board toward her and runs her pencil down it.

SHEILA: Kevin drives forty-two mile a week that nobody pays for. He must like the view. (She lets that lie.) It gives me room. The book will hide ten pounds and four fifths of what the cars really drove. Past that I'm not keeping accounts, love, I'm writing you a reference.

She draws the pile across with the flat of her pencil, counts it by eye, and unscrews her pen. It stops above the page.

SHEILA: Thirty-five I can do. Same as your first night?

She does not wait to be asked what she means. She turns the day book back, a week's sheets at a time, to the first Monday, runs her finger down to the foot of the page and turns the book round on the counter to face him.

The camera reads the line. It is in her hand, in ink a little browner than today's: the words she read back to him across this counter, and he said yes to.

What she says next depends on what is there.

1. The line is a fare: Office to ferry landing and return. Waited. Cash. £2.80.

SHEILA: Waiting. It's the only thing a cab earns stood still. I can lay thirty-five pound of waiting across four cars and a week, and nobody's going to count the minutes.

FRANK: Same.

She turns back to today and writes seven lines, a car and a night to each, saying them under her breath as she goes: landing and return, waited; Fairview and return, waited. She puts the pile in the tin.

SHEILA: And the tin's got it this time. I'll not want your pocket.

2. The line is the envelope: One packet, by hand. Mickey's account. No charge.

SHEILA: I can't bank no charge. Your first night's no good to me, love.

She waits, pen up.

FRANK (the player picks one, or speaks his own): "Then write them as fares." / "Write it as it was." / "Keep it out of the book."

To the first:

SHEILA: Then it's a different hand from that one, and it starts tonight. Anybody reading down the page'll see the day it changed.

She writes them. To the second:

SHEILA: A round. In a fare book. (She writes it, small.) Collected, by hand, Quay Street. Thirty-five pounds. No fare. It's your book.

To the third, she folds the notes into an old cocoa tin from under the counter. "Then it's cash, and it stops cash. You can't pay the rent with it and you can't pay the cars."

3. The line is hers, written behind her hand on the first morning. He has not seen it until now: Office to ferry landing. Collection, a/c M. Suddaby. Settled.

SHEILA: You'd nothing to say, so I put it to Mickey. A dead man's account is the safest place in any book. Nobody can ask him.

She taps the cloth-backed debt book.

SHEILA: There's a hundred and fifty-six pound in there nobody's going to pay. So thirty-five of it's just been paid. Old debts recovered. Same as your first night?

On a plain yes she writes it so. On anything else: "Then say it how you want it," and he may choose as in the second case.

When the day book is done she reaches behind her without looking and takes a second book out of the handbag, from under the knitting. It is red, the kind the newsagent sells, and it has a ribbon. She opens it at the ribbon, writes one line quickly, and blots it.

The telephone rings. Pauline goes at six, except on the old firm's nights. Sheila turns in her chair to the desk and lifts it.

SHEILA: Mickey's. (A pause.) No, love, we're a taxi.

The red book lies open on the counter, facing nobody. The camera is free to go to it. The player can look.

It is ruled by hand, in three columns with no printed heads. Somebody has written the heads in once, on the first page, long ago: Night. What it was. Brought in by. The line she has just written is the last on the page. Above it are the nights of the last three weeks, one line each, in plain words: Landing, envelope. Landing, envelope. £15, Drewery. Landing, envelope. Tonight's reads Round, Quay Street, £35.

Down the third column, beside every one of them, in her small upright hand, is the same name. R. Kirby. R. Kirby. R. Kirby.

The top line of the page is the first Monday. Landing. Envelope. R. Kirby. It is the same night as the line he has just been shown in the other book, written as it was.

If the player turns back one leaf, the ink goes brown and the column changes. For as far back as the pages go, the name beside every night is M.S.

Sheila puts the telephone down. She sees where he is looking. She does not hurry. She closes the red book on its ribbon, puts it into the handbag, and draws the knitting over it.

SHEILA: That's mine.

FRANK (the player picks one, or speaks his own): "Why's it his name?" / "Put mine." / nothing.

To the first:

SHEILA: Because he brought it. I write who brought it in. That's all that column's for.

To the second:

SHEILA: There's no column for who it was brought to, love. Mickey never needed one. He went himself. (She screws the cap on her pen.) You want your name in there, you go and fetch it yourself.

If he asks who has seen it:

SHEILA: Me. And now you.

She shuts the tin. Outside, Ron shifts his weight from one foot to the other and is still again.

SHEILA: Sunday I'll read you the week. I always do it Sundays.

## What the player can do

- Say yes: the same as the first night. Sign: his own line, turned round for him to read. What follows: his first answer is now the firm's way of writing, and every wash after this is written like it.
- Change it. Sign: in the second case she says outright that the page will show the day it changed; in the first and third he has to ask. What follows: two hands in one book, with a date between them. In week five that date is the second thing DS Ellis puts her finger on.
- Keep it out of the book. What follows: cash in a cocoa tin, good for men's cuts and for nothing with a receipt.
- Look at the red book while she is on the telephone. Hands: look; one key turns a leaf back. Sign: she has never before left it open. What follows: nothing is said unless he speaks. He has now seen the first night twice in one evening, and seen whose name is beside it.
- Not look. She shuts the book and says, "That's mine," to nobody.
- Go and fetch it himself, from now on. This is the only way the third column ever changes. Any night job Frank does alone, with Ron sent home, goes down as F.N. Any job Ron brings to him, rides on or carries goes down as R. Kirby. Alone, Frank has nobody at his shoulder.

## What the town remembers

- Nobody saw this but Sheila. Ron, through the glass, saw the two of them at the counter over a book for longer than cashing up takes, and asks nothing.
- On Sunday Sheila reads the week back across the counter, as she said, and from this week she says the third column too: "Monday, the landing, Ron. Wednesday, the round, Ron. Friday, the gate, you." It is the only score the game ever shows him, and it is read aloud once a week by a woman with a pen.
- The line he read tonight is the line DS Ellis reads aloud in week five. The player has now seen it a second time, and knows there is a truer book than the one on the counter, and whose handbag it lives in.
- Ron's name down the column is why Ron stops the car in lesson five and asks what is in the book (scene 12). He does not know about the red one. He knows he has been the man who brought it in.
- Kevin's forty-two miles are The long way round. Sheila has now said it in front of the owner, and will see whether he reads his own book.
- In the last week, if the line is written true, it is this book the firm's books open on.

## Variants by path

- A firm with no night money. The scene wakes instead on the first Sunday reading after Frank has seen Mickey's locked office: Sheila opens the red book to enter nothing, and the phone rings. He sees the brown pages of M.S., and then three lines of R. Kirby for the nights of the envelope, and then white paper. "That's mine. It stops there, if it stops."
- The arrangement ended on night one. The page has one envelope line, a message carried, R. Kirby; the rest is whatever came after.
- Frank has already been going alone. Some lines read F.N. Sheila, shutting the book: "You've been fetching your own. I'd noticed."
