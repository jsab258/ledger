# 11. The gate: Ron cornered

- Where it sits: Act IIb, week five: the Widow's go at one of Frank's men. One of the week's three, and the only one by day.
- What wakes it: the Landing has been played; Garbutt has said the week's aim; Ron has carried tally 39 in his pocket since week three; it is the morning call at the pen. The word reaches Frank over the radio.
- Who must be present: Ron Kirby, Frank Stathers, the men of the pen. Kevin Needham on the air. Fallback for Stathers: Mark Billany reads the list, and reads it worse. If the Widow is Frank's ally this scene does not wake; Danny's go is at Ron instead, at the ferry rank, and is a different card.

Frank is trying to get Ron out of the pen with the docks looking on. He will give up a day's money and an owner's dignity, or the last of Ron's standing with his own, or nothing, and hear about it.

## Staging

The pen, twenty to seven. First light, grey, and rain coming in off the water at a slant.

There were fourteen men in it a fortnight ago. This morning there are nine, and one fruit boat on four berth that wants six.

Ron is the ninth. He is at the back rail, under the open half, where the tin roof does not reach: donkey jacket, cap, hands in his pockets, rain running off the peak. It is where he stood for thirty-six years. The other eight have left a clear yard on each side of him.

Nothing keeps him there. The pen has a wicket on the latch and no lock. What keeps him is the rule, and everybody present knows it: a man holding a tally stands the call until he is called or the list is shut.

At the stand by the gate is Stathers, white hat, board, the wire loop. Beside him a post with a row of hooks, most of them empty.

The gate's near leaf is shut across the road again. Lee Drewery, or whoever Frank put on the gate, is sitting on the kerb outside it with his cap in his hands and a split lip. Two cars of packers are stopped behind.

The camera comes in on the car's radio first, then is wide and still: the pen, the stand, the road. It does not go close on Ron's face at any point in this scene.

## The scene

It starts wherever Frank is, on the air.

KEVIN (radio): Car one. I'm at the dock gate and it's shut on me again. And they've got Ron stood in the pen.

PAULINE: Stood in the what?

KEVIN: The pen, Pauline. With his cap on. Like it was nineteen seventy.

A pause on the channel, which the whole Hook is listening to.

PAULINE: He's not asked for anybody. (Another.) He wouldn't.

The player has it from there. If he goes, he arrives as the hooter sounds a quarter to seven.

Stathers reads from the board. He does not raise his voice; he has never needed to.

STATHERS: Four berth. Six.

He calls six surnames, each with its number. Each man called comes to the stand, takes a tally off the loop, and goes through the wicket toward the ship without looking back. It takes a minute. Three men are left in the pen, and they are standing in the rain a yard clear of the fourth.

Stathers does not shut the board. He lays it flat on the stand and puts his hand on it.

STATHERS: List's open.

He calls nobody. He looks at his watch, and then at the weather.

ONE OF THE THREE (low, to the rail in front of him): Hand it him back, Ron. Let us get home.

Ron does not turn his head.

STATHERS (to the pen, as to a meeting): Kirby's carried a tally of mine a fortnight. Man with a tally stands the call. Or he comes up here and hangs it on that hook, and tells this pen whose he is. (He finds Frank, at the road.) Morning, Mr Nowack. You've a car wants to come through, I think.

Ron has seen Frank. He looks at the far wall.

What Frank does now is the scene.

Call him. FRANK (the player picks one, or calls a name of his own). The pen has its own rule for this as well, and Stathers is bound by it: any man with work may call a man off the stand, by his surname.

- "Kirby." Said as a foreman says it. Every head in the pen comes up. Ron takes his hands out of his pockets. He walks the length of the pen to the wicket, lifts the latch, and comes out to the road. He stops in front of Frank.

RON: Boss.

- "Mr Kirby." Nobody in that pen has been called Mister in his life. There is a silence with rain in it. Then one of the three, without turning round: "Go on, then. Mister Kirby." It is not said to hurt. Ron comes out the same way.
- "Ron."

STATHERS: That's not a call. I've three Rons.

He waits for a proper one.

After a call that stands:

STATHERS: You call a man off my stand, you pay his day. Down. In his hand. Thirty-eight pound.

FRANK: Thirty-eight pound. Count it, Mr Stathers.

He counts it into Ron's palm in front of three men who were not called. Ron has to take it; that is the rule too. He closes his hand on it and does not look at it.

STATHERS: And a man called for another firm hangs his tally up.

Ron takes number 39 out of his coat. He turns it over once. He walks to the post and hangs it on a hook himself, and it swings there.

STATHERS: List's shut.

The three go home. The two gate men walk the leaf open.

Stand with him. Hands: walk. Frank lifts the latch of the wicket and goes in, past the three, to the back rail, and stops a yard from Ron, in the clear space, in the good overcoat.

STATHERS: You've no tally, Mr Nowack.

Nobody else says anything. A man without a tally may stand; he is called last. Frank is standing where the casuals stand.

The player stands. It rains. He can speak to Ron.

RON (to the wall, each once): You've a firm to see to. / There's two cars sat at that gate. / It's my stand.

He does not say go, and he does not say stay.

One of the three reaches a packet across the gap without looking. "You'll want one. It's a long stand."

The packers get out of the cars and come through the wicket on foot for their clock. They pass the rail.

THE ELDEST: Is that him? In the pen?

THE YOUNGEST: There's nowt of him and now he's on the casual.

It goes on for as long as a foreman can leave a ship short of him, which is until the hatches are off. Then Stathers picks up the board.

STATHERS: I've a boat to work. (He looks at the two of them along the rail.) I've had this gate twenty year and I've not seen an owner stand his own man's call. (To Ron.) Mickey never did.

RON: No.

STATHERS: List's shut. Keep it, Ron. It's brass.

He goes through to the ship. One of the three, leaving, nods to Ron. It is the first time since the fifth morning.

Go in swinging. Hands: over the rail, or at the two men on the leaf. It is fists, by the gate, in the rain. Ron comes off the back rail at the first blow and is at Frank's shoulder, and then at his back, as on the ferry rank in week two, if it came to fists there. This time the men in front of him are men he stood beside, and two of the three in the pen come out against him because it is their gate. Ron hits them. Stathers does not fight. When it is over, if Frank is standing, the leaf is open and Lee is on his feet. Stathers walks over to Ron, who is breathing hard, and holds out his hand, palm up. Ron puts the tally in it.

STATHERS: That's that, then.

If Frank is not standing, they are both put out through the wicket onto the road, and the leaf stays shut.

Send somebody. If Danny is on the board he goes gladly, and makes a turn of it from the roadside with his hands round his mouth: "Kirby! Thirty-nine! Your taxi's here!" The pen laughs. Ron comes out, because a call is a call. Danny pays the day from a roll and tells Frank what he is owed. The first man cannot make a call that Stathers will hear, and gets the other half of Lee's lip.

Do nothing. Stathers keeps the list open until the hatches are off, and shuts it. Ron stands the whole of it. Then he walks out through the wicket and up the dock road in the rain. He is on the door at Mickey's by ten, wet through, and hangs his coat on its hook, and says "Boss," and nothing else.

## What the player can do

- Call him: by which name is the choice. Sign: Stathers has just said the rule to the whole pen. What follows: Ron is out without a blow; thirty-eight pounds; the tally is hung up by his own hand, at Frank's call and not by any word of Ron's.
- Stand with him. Sign: the clear yard each side of Ron. What follows: the morning's cars are late, and Tharratt counts it; the town has the new owner stood on the casual in the rain; Ron keeps the tally and his place, and one man nods.
- Fight. Sign: Lee's lip, and the men on the leaf watching Frank's hands. What follows: the gate by force, or the road; Ron has hit men of the pen, in the pen, and there is no nod after that.
- Send somebody, or nobody. Sign: Pauline's "He wouldn't." What follows: as written, and the telling reaches Frank within the day, to his face.
- Pay, or not. Thirty-eight pounds is from his pocket. Short, he cannot make the call stand. "Then it's not work you're calling him to, Mr Nowack. It's a favour. I don't shut a list for a favour."

## What the town remembers

- The pen tells it, this time, because this time it was done by its rules. "Called him out like a ganger." "Called him Mister. In the pen. I thought Stathers'd swallow his board." "Stood there an hour next to him in a ninety-pound coat and never said owt." Or: "Nobody come."
- Brenda, by dinner, if he stood: "I hear you've been looking for work."
- Stathers tells Maureen Jensen which it was. If Frank stood: "He stands where his men stand." She says that is not a man you tax; it is a man you wait for. If he called: "He bought him off my hook for thirty-eight pound." She has the tally sent to her and keeps it.
- Ron. Called: he keeps the day's money in his pocket and does not break it; it is counted out of his pockets at the station, or, if he walks, it pays for both of number nine. Stood with: he says nothing about it, ever; in lesson five he names the pen for the first time. Fought for: his knuckles are split on the wheel. Left: he turns the tally over on the door all week where Frank can see it.
- The night between reads this morning. Ron will let a cheaper way be done in his name only if Frank came for him here: if he called, or stood, or fought.

## Variants by path

- In week three Frank sent Ron to open the gate. The eight have their backs to him, and have had since. If Frank stands with him, the nod at the end is the first face Ron has been shown in a fortnight.
- No man on the gate (a firm with no night money, or nobody of his on the gate). No leaf is shut. Stathers: "Nothing to do with your cars, Mr Nowack. This is between a man and his number."
