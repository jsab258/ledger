**01.** Strongest: the choice between the grave and the fare is made with the hands, and Ron's quiet admission pays it off: "Nobody said which boat. I'd have walked down." Weakest: Pauline's patter is too busy. It plays like a sitcom switchboard ("He can spell. Ten seconds."). The backwards run down the tenfoot is a stunt. The action carries the meaning: he picks a stranger's fare over his uncle's grave.

**02.** Strongest: the old envelope that nobody ever bought ("He never bought an envelope in his life") and "Left alone." Weakest: the want comes as a speech under a lamp: "By Christmas, Ron. Mickey's runs the Hook. And when they say Mickey's, they'll mean me." It is a standing scene and a dialogue menu. Only the mechanics are in the player's hands. The meaning is told.

**05.** Strongest: Danny's "Sit down, Craig. I want to see what he does." Then the keys go on the bare third hook and Frank chalks "Car three". Weakest: Ron guiding with his hands repeats scene 01's gimmick, and the edge of the quay is a set-up that waits for the finale. The deed carries the meaning, because the town retells it. But the meaning is mostly swagger.

**08.** Strongest: "Send Ron". The pen turns its back on him: "By the time the leaf is on its stop, fourteen backs are to the road." The tally numbered 39 is a fine object. Weakest: five labelled routes (Pay, Bluff, Push, Send, Go round) read like a menu, and the two-Franks joke is laboured. The action carries meaning, because Frank spends Ron's standing with one order.

**10.** Strongest: the old man's "Not on here. Thirty year." Then "Next of kin? Mickey's. Quay Street." and "Put me down as family." Weakest: a gunshot mistaken for a firework ("Somebody says 'Ooh.'") is stock, and the tea-things plan is cute. The action carries meaning: saying "Stop" costs two cases and means turning back for a man.

**12.** Strongest: "Handbrake's yours, boss." Whatever Frank has just said, true or false, he drives away on it himself. Weakest: the scene hangs on a ledger line the player must remember from unseen scenes ("The game holds the line that stands in the day book and reads his answer against it"). The release of the brake is the meaning, and it is the best use of the hands in the set.

**14.** Strongest: "Hang that on my hook, boss." Frank drives home and says "Coming up on." and does not finish it. Weakest: it is overloaded. A padlock key, a lightless reverse run, a pawned watch at 2 a.m. and a bent detective all come in one night, and the watch is a convenient reveal. The pen on the counter carries the meaning in the hand.

**15.** Strongest: Frank corks his own gate with car two and holds the brake until the door breaks his arm: "That's the one I change gear with. Keys. On the board." Weakest: the bouncing car is stagey, and the roll-call by name runs long. Kevin's grievance is better than Frank's answer to it. The action carries meaning, because his body pays for not asking anyone.

**17.** Strongest: car two behind the hearse, all forty-one doors open, and "Cammack's?" answered with "Mickey's." Weakest: the tea is three bosses bidding over a symbolic torch in speeches, and the Man in Black's marks out of ten are whimsy. The drive carries meaning. The torch choice is only a menu.

**19.** Strongest: Fewson's reversal: "No. I want to see what you do." Weakest: the climax is a fist fight over a ledger, and almost every beat is a callback ("Leave my mirror alone", the seat run back two notches, the car on the edge), so it plays as a reprise. Ron only stands at the bonnet. The fight is mechanics. The meaning sits in "what Danny is worth", a single line.

## 2. The fifteen principles

- **P1. Want and need: 1.** The want is loud and plain: "By Christmas, Ron. Mickey's runs the Hook." But the need is not buried. Frank says it himself, more than once: "Nobody came down for mine"; "Five weeks I've wanted somebody to." In the middle scenes he is defending the rank, the gate and the yard, not visibly taking the Hook. Burying the need would raise it.
- **P2. Lead loudest: 1.** Frank acts and is at risk in the Landing, the night between, the yard and the quay. Car three is his week-one deed, and Danny answers it. By the test that is a pass. But the heaviest moments in 08, 12 and 14 are Ron's deeds, and Frank reacts: "Ron has walked into the station by himself." In 10 the heaviest deed is the old man's. Frank's lines are measured, while Danny and Pauline are louder.
- **P3. The bond: 2.** Frank and Ron share every scene, mostly in cars: lessons, the brow, the hearse. There is strain at the gate (the tally) and at lesson five (the lie), and the breach is acted, with the pen at nine. The last word is not in these scenes, and in 19 Ron is passive ("He says nothing").
- **P4. Few people: 1.** Ron, Danny, the old man, Fewson and Kevin each have a filmable deed. Stathers, Garbutt, Agar, Ellis, Wardell, Rita, Grace, Pauline, Sheila and the Widow crowd round them. Sheila and the Widow have no deed in these scenes that pulls against Frank. The carried cast is at or past the limit.
- **P5. Opponent: 2.** Danny is there in week one (05). He gains ground in view: the white hatchback on the rank, his lads on the landing, Fewson's roll, the CABS card. He wants the same thing ("Top line. Half. I'm not greedy, I'm just first."), and Frank faces him in person on the quay (19).
- **P6. Stakes: 2.** The ladder climbs: a fare and the rent, the rank, Ron's place at the gate, a man's life, Ron's trust, liberty in the cells, Frank's arm, the licence and the yard, then the book and Ron's freedom. Frank's body and liberty are both at risk in play, and the firm can be lost ("Or we'll have it in motors").
- **P7. Early deed returns: 2.** The envelope carried on night one goes in the day book with R.K. in the column. It comes back as the search, Ron's charge and Danny's blackmail: "Eleven nights with R.K. at the end of the line."
- **P8. Climbing middle: 2.** Every scene has different ground: the gate at dawn, the fireworks on the landing, a railway crossing, the cells, the yard, a funeral. The Landing at the midpoint brings guns in ("From tonight, the other side may bring one"). Some motifs repeat too often: three reverse runs, two held-input brakes, Ron's hand signals twice.
- **P9. Clarity and pull: 1.** Every scene says plainly "Frank is trying to…". But the book's lines, the carbon, "Sheila's line", "third line, no arrow", the top line and the board order all need explaining first.
- **P10. Last act: 1.** Only 19 can be seen. It is not one of the three heaviest scenes (10, 14 and 15 are). Its pieces were planted early (the edge, the brass key, Fewson's line), but the climax is "what Danny is worth", which answers week one's question only in part.
- **P11. One question, unsaid: 1.** The question is "whose are you?". The book and the town's memory keep its score. But it is said nearly aloud: "I never asked who you were… I ask whose." and "Nobody's. That were the job."
- **P12. A world ending: 1.** It is well shown in places: "three cranes with nothing hanging from them", "the timber ship has gone… the late boat is no longer on the timetable", the pen, the tallies, L-plates. Several scenes have no sign of loss, and the end of that loss cannot be seen here.
- **P13. Losses prepared: 2.** Losing Ron (to prison, or his silence) is prepared in every scene. The player's choices touch it: the truth or a lie at the white board, then the pen.
- **P14. Comedy: 2.** It is present in every scene and comes from character: "Mickey had upholstery"; "Not the stapler"; "He can spell". Running jokes pay off: "Two minutes… real ones" comes back in 15, "Leave my mirror alone" in 19, and the spelling of Nowak ends with Ron spelling it right in 14.
- **P15. Choices return: 1.** Choices clearly come back: the Billany cap, how Fewson was treated, sending Ron (the pen's backs on Remembrance Sunday), truth or lie, the torch. No side story is visible, and the ending is only implied to read the record.

**Total: 22 / 30.**

## 3. The three weakest scenes, and the one change for each

1. **19, the quay at four.** Replace the fist fight with a choice in the hands at the edge. Car two hangs over the water with the book on the dash. Frank can take the handbrake off and lose both, Mickey's car and thirty-one years of book with Ron's eleven lines in it. Or he can fight for it. The handbrake motif then pays off in the climax, and the price is something he built.
2. **02, the ask on the rank.** Cut the speech to two lines and play the carry. Ron walks him down in the rain, and Frank states the want to the old man at the ticket window, who answers "Mickey's?". Then the want lands as a deed done in front of a witness, not a declaration under a lamp.
3. **17, the room at Grace's.** Move the torch decision from the tea table to the overhang, when the hearse stops at the ticket window. Keeping it, or handing it to Danny's young man in front of three firms, becomes a public act in the street rather than a bid round a cloth.

## 4. Voices

Most lines can be told apart with the names hidden. Ron is unmistakable ("Aye", "Nowt", "Coming up on…"). So is Danny ("nephew", pleased, "I've had most things"), and so is Agar ("One would expect…"). **Sheila and Pauline** sound alike: both are dry, both say "love", and both run a counter ("He's nowhere to be" / "Which she'll not be"). Stathers, Gordon and the old man share one gruff northern register too ("Aye. They all do." / "Brow's greasy at low water.").

## 5. Clichés, stock lines and turns seen coming

- 10: a gunshot taken for a firework ("Somebody says 'Ooh.'"). The old mentor shot just after telling the hero to go home.
- 10: planning a heist with the tea things ("the teapot is the boat").
- 14: the loyal man takes the fall for the boss ("I've just not told her you."), and the pawned watch for the funeral turns up at the key moment.
- 10: "Put me down as family. There's nobody else coming." This is sentimental and expected.
- 19: Fewson turning on Danny, signalled since 05 by the seat he ran back. Danny's own line turned back on him.
- 01 and 14: the backwards drive at speed down a narrow lane.
- 02 and 14: "Aye. Well. … still wants carrying / running." The echo is designed, but you can hear it coming.
- 17: an eccentric mourner who scores funerals out of ten.

## 6. Rule breaks and confusion

I found no alcohol or gambling (the flask holds tea), no children, and nobody asks about Frank's past. The dates check out: 1 December 1990 was a Saturday. Points that would confuse a reader who has not seen the plan:
- Two Franks at the gate (08).
- The Widow is only named as Maureen Jensen late.
- "Sheila's line" and "She put it to Mickey" (12) refer to unseen scenes.
- What is on the R.K. carbon.
- "Third line, no arrow" and the top line.
- The brass key Danny "never gave back", plus "Nine year I've carried that" and the ghost name under CAR 2. These suggest Danny drove for Mickey, but nothing says so.
- "the old hand lifted off the landing with paper in his coat" (12).
- Dialect such as tenfoot, brow and the pen is never glossed.

## 7. Recognition

- *Get Carter*: a man comes back to a northern port for a dead relative's funeral and walks into the local firms. The premise is quite close.
- *On the Waterfront*: the pen and the brass tallies handed out at the shape-up (08). The image is close.
- *The Long Good Friday*: the British gangland tone.
- Mafia (2002): a cab driver drawn into a crime firm. The resemblance is loose.

The ledger line that returns to bring Frank down mirrors RDR2's Downes debt in structure, not in content.

## 8. Distance from the bar

At the level of the individual scene this is good, shipped-game work, and in places better. Lesson five's handbrake, Ron's cap across the flap and the pen turning its back would sit well beside the reference scenes. It is still about a mark and a half under RDR2's best, and the ending is the weakest point. What stands most in the way is that the story's weight rests on paper. The day book's lines, the initials in a column, the order on a chalk board and a carbon in an envelope are what put Ron in a cell and what the climax is fought over, and a player has to be told what they mean before he can feel them. RDR2's best scenes rest on a body: a cough, a ride, a death. Here the bodies (the old man's arm, Frank's arm, Ron in a cell) are the strongest material, and they serve a ledger.

## 9. The people, by their own deeds

1. **Frank.** He has the most deeds the town retells: the tenfoot backwards, the car on the edge, carrying the old man, corking the gate with his arm, the car behind the hearse. But most of them answer someone else's move.
2. **Danny.** He makes the moves that start scenes: the hatchback on the rank, the lads on the landing, the roll in the street, the book on the edge.
3. **Ron.** He has fewer deeds, but the heaviest: opening the gate, bringing the car to the brow alone, walking into the station unasked.
4. **The old man.** He grips the gunman's sleeve ("Not on here"), and his torch and name drive two scenes.
5. **Fewson.** He runs the seat back, carries the roll, says "No.", and drives off with the crew.
6. **Kevin.** He calls the settle in the yard and leaves his key in the gate.
7. **Pauline.** She is the clock and the voice of the Hook, but she makes noise rather than deeds.
8. **Sheila.** She writes things down ("cash introduced, £23.50"). That matters, but it is passive.
9. **The Widow.** She takes off a coat and says "That's one." Everything else she does happens offstage.
