**01.** Strongest: the fare against Pauline's open-channel clock, ending in "Nobody said which boat. (...) I'd have walked down." Weakest: Frank is over-scripted, as in "I'll not be late twice on one street. Start your clock when my door shuts." The hands carry meaning: choosing the car over the grave is the character.

**02.** Strongest: Darren's undercut, "By Christmas. He never said which." Weakest: the want comes as a speech ("By Christmas, Ron. Mickey's runs the Hook. And when they say Mickey's, they'll mean me."), the stock rise-to-power announcement. The hands only pick a line or carry an envelope; mechanics only, bar the optional peek inside.

**05.** Strongest: the hatchback hanging over the water, then "It's on my rank, so it's on my board. Car three." Weakest: Ron walking a stranger's car to the edge for Frank in week one is staged rather than earned. The hands carry the meaning: how far the player drives is the whole statement.

**08.** Strongest: "Fourteen men are listening to a man be offered what they are standing there for," and Send Ron turning "fourteen backs" to the road. Weakest: Frank is a bystander in his own gate scene; Stathers and Ron own it. The hands carry meaning in Send Ron. The other four options are mechanics.

**10.** Strongest: the shot taken for a rocket: "Somebody says 'Ooh.' Nothing breaks." Then the next of kin, "Mickey's. Quay Street." Weakest: overloaded with L-plates, string, rail, crowd and constable, and the fireworks cover is convenient. The hands carry meaning: "Stop" costs two cases and makes the drive.

**12.** Strongest: "Handbrake's yours, boss." Only the player's release moves the car, so a lie is driven away on. Weakest: the stake is the wording of a day-book entry, abstract and clerical to anyone not tracking the ledger plot. The hands carry meaning, cleanly.

**14.** Strongest: the pen laid between two men, then "You've took the one thing I had to give" against "Hang that on my hook, boss." Weakest: overstuffed (a lights-out reverse run, the pawned watch, Wardell's bribe, the pen), and the watch reveal is sentimental. The hands carry meaning: who reaches the pen.

**15.** Strongest: the car corked in the gateway, and Darren's pound, "I'm the only one stood in this yard who does." Weakest: the arm is crushed by a contrived physics puzzle (a door pushed shut "a foot" per bounce) that nobody chooses to do to him. The held brake carries meaning, though the injury is mechanical.

**17.** Strongest: the Man in Black's "Seven. The ham was good. Nobody knew the second hymn," and the one-armed "Would you." to the Widow. Weakest: the torch auction is the systems talking ("Third line, no arrow"), and a reader is lost in it. The hands carry meaning in the hearse and the torch.

**19.** Strongest: Fewson's "No. I want to see what you do", his own humiliation returned. Weakest: a finale that is a fist fight over a bookkeeping threat, tied with neat callbacks ("Leave my mirror alone" again). Danny's motive is a chalk line. The fight is mechanics; the boat, board or detective line carries the meaning.

## 2. The fifteen principles

- **P1. Want and need: 1.** The want is loud and live in hour one: "Mickey's runs the Hook. By Christmas." The need is set up in scene 01 ("Frank is trying to be met") and forced in scene 14: "I never wanted it to be this." These scenes never show the want broken, though; after the night between he still runs the same race. Half.
- **P2. Lead loudest: 2.** Frank acts and is himself at risk in all of the heaviest scenes: 10 (the fight on the brow, then the edge drive), 14 (the pen, the bollards), 15 (the arm), 19 (the fists). The week-one deed is car three, which Danny answers ("He's moved my motor"). Note, though: the heaviest feelings are Ron's, not Frank's.
- **P3. The bond: 2.** Frank and Ron have a beat in every scene, mostly in cars (the lessons, the brow, the hearse). Strain comes at the gate (week 3) and the lie (week 5). In the breach Frank acts: he can take the pen. The last word is implied, not shown: in 19 Ron only stands at the bonnet. The pass rests on scene 20 delivering it.
- **P4. Few people, own deeds: 1.** Ron (walks into the station), Danny (the hatchback, the book) and the old man (takes the sleeve) each have a filmable deed. The Widow has none here: her deed is undoing a coat, and she works through Stathers. Sheila has a want only in bookkeeping. Half.
- **P5. Close opponent: 1.** Danny is in week one, in person, and faced on the quay where Frank can win or lose. His gains are not seen every week. His turn depends on the player ("from a cause the player made"). He wants "Top" of a board rather than the Hook itself. Three of four.
- **P6. Climbing stakes: 2.** The rent (£18), the rank, a fist fight at the gate, a man shot, liberty (the cells), the body (the arm), the firm (the book to Ellis). Built things can be lost ("Cammack has the water"). The last rung is paperwork, though.
- **P7. Early deed returns: 2.** The envelope of night one, with R.K. in the column, becomes the search, Ron's statement and Danny's leverage. "Eleven nights with R.K. at the end of the line." This is the best-built thing in the set.
- **P8. Climbing middle: 1.** The places and kinds of job vary, and the Landing changes play at the midpoint ("From tonight, the other side may bring one"). But the set pieces are small and repeat their moves: the quay edge three times (05, 10, 19), reversing at speed twice (01, 14), and held inputs twice (10, 15). There is no set piece of real scale.
- **P9. Clarity and pull: 1.** The want is clear. The ledger mechanism is not: "the line that stands in the day book", "Frank signed first", "third line, no arrow", Garbutt's three heads. Each needs explaining.
- **P10. Last act: 1.** These scenes cannot show the whole last act. Scene 19 pays off what was planted (the quay edge, the key, Fewson, £23.50), and its choices were taught by the torch and the pen. But it is not one of the three heaviest scenes (10, 14 and 15 are).
- **P11. One question: 1.** The question is "whose are you?" ("I ask whose." / "Nobody's. That were the job."), and the book and the town's memory keep its score. It is close to being said aloud ("Somebody has to be family"), and the ending's deed is not shown.
- **P12. Ending world: 1.** It is shown well: "three cranes with nothing hanging from them", "the timber ship has gone", "the late boat is no longer on the timetable", the pen and the tally. Period rules bite (L-plates, Customs, the licence). The loss is not shown complete; the ending scene is unseen.
- **P13. Prepared loss: 2.** Ron's loss to the cells has every lesson behind it, and the player's lie or truth and the pen touch it. The old man's death is off-screen ("Then it went to his chest"), which is weaker.
- **P14. Comedy: 2.** There is a joke in every scene: the packers' "Mickey had upholstery", "Not the stapler", "Seven. The ham was good." Running jokes pay off: the spelling of Nowak (Ron spells it right in 14), "two minutes / Real ones" (15), "Leave my mirror alone" (19).
- **P15. Choices come back: 2.** The tally, Billany's cap, Fewson's treatment, the pen and the lie all return. The ending is read from the record (£23.50 read out by Customs). No wrong deed is forced, and the jobs have several routes. Side stories cannot be shown here. Julie's fare and Darren's debt are implied as small returning threads.

**Total: 22 / 30.** Of the heavy seven, P1, P5 and P8 sit at half; none fails.

## 3. The three weakest scenes

1. **19 The quay at four.** Make the book's threat land on Ron, not on a chalk line. Danny forces Frank to choose between his board and Ron's freedom, so the climax is the bond's last word, and Ron does something other than stand at the bonnet. Cut the repeated mirror line.
2. **02 The ask on the rank.** Replace the speech with a deed. Frank opens the envelope in front of Ron, or chalks something, and keeps one line of want ("By Christmas, Ron."). Darren's line then lands on an act, not on oratory.
3. **15 The yard.** Make the arm someone's deed. Kevin, or one named driver, has to decide whether to give the last bounce, and does, or stops. The injury then has an author and a cost on a person.

## 4. Voices

Mostly yes. Ron is unmistakable: "Aye", "Nowt", naming the buildings, "Coming up on the ticket window." So are Pauline (counting, dry: "He can spell. Ten seconds."), Danny ("nephew", delighted), Sheila ("love", sums) and Agar ("One would expect"). Frank drifts into the writer's voice in his set speeches.

Alike: **Kevin and Terry** ("Six year" / "Mine's Fridays. It's always been Fridays."). Close behind are Stathers and the Widow, who share the clipped authority of "I don't ask, Mr Nowak" and "There's one Frank on this gate."

## 6. Rule breaks and confusion

No content rule is broken. There is no alcohol or gambling (Stathers brings "a flask of tea"; the pawnshop is not gambling), and no children: the "youngest" packer reads as adult.

These confuse a reader without the plan:
- Board terms are never explained: "Top line", "third line, no arrow", "my name chalked third, or nowhere".
- Who Garbutt is, and why three heads obey his order.
- "The Widow" is Maureen Jensen, inferred only.
- Two characters called Frank (Stathers and Nowak) are a deliberate joke but blur in summary.
- "Haddock's dear", "Frank signed first" and "a firm with no night money" are unexplained.
- "The Man in Black" reads as supernatural until he scores the funeral.
- In scene 14, Frank is "released" at six yet is at the counter for the nine o'clock pen. It works, but the timeline is hard to follow.
- Scene 12's question, "That first night. What did you have her put in the book?", needs the reader to recall Pauline's line on the carbon from scene 02.
- Scene 19 cites Pauline's words "in week two", which no scene shown contains.

## 7. Recognition

- **Get Carter**: a man comes north by ferry or train for a relative's funeral, into a port's criminal economy. The premise is close, the tone different.
- **On the Waterfront**: the shape-up pen, the brass hiring tally offered as a favour, men turning their backs. Scene 08 is close.
- **Mafia: The City of Lost Heaven**: a cab driver drawn into an outfit. Loose.
- **The Long Good Friday**: the British gangland ambition speech. Loose.

Nothing is copied.

## 8. Distance from the bar

The two best scenes (10 and 14) stand perhaps a step below Red Dead Redemption 2's best. The fireworks shot and the pen between two men are real, original moments, and the Frank and Ron bond is built in the hands as the bar demands. The rest sits at good-shipped-game level, held down by small set pieces that repeat their moves (the quay edge, reversing, a held button) and by a finale that is a fist fight. The single thing most in the way is that the story's engine is a line in a day book. The breach, the arrest, Danny's leverage and the climax all turn on who wrote what in a column. The heaviest consequences therefore feel clerical, and the lead's big moments are mostly restraint and careful driving, not deeds of passion with a man's life in the balance.

## 9. The people, by their own deeds

1. **Frank.** Every retold deed is his: backwards down the tenfoot, Danny's car on the edge, nine bollards in the dark, corking the gate with his arm, the hearse.
2. **Ron.** Walks into a police station "to sign for a line that was Frank's", opens the gate when sent, and drives to the brow alone.
3. **Danny.** Blocks the rank, takes the book to the edge, fights on the coping. He is the only rival who acts in person.
4. **The old man from the landing.** One deed, unforgettable: he takes the gunman's sleeve, "Not on here."
5. **Fewson.** Sets the seat to fit himself, then refuses to sit, and drives off with the crew.
6. **Sheila.** Writes "cash introduced, £23.50", which Customs reads at the end. She records rather than acts.
7. **The Widow.** Works offstage through Stathers. Her one deed on screen is undoing a coat.
