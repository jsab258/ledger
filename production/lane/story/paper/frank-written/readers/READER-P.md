**01.** Strongest: Pauline's count on an open channel and the blind reverse down the tenfoot; "Nobody came down for mine." / "I'd have walked down." Weakest: a crowded opening, nine named people in ten minutes, and Pauline playing to the gallery ("He can spell. Ten seconds."). The action carries the meaning: choosing the car over the grave is the scene.

**02.** Strongest: "Forty-one doors on this street, Ron. I counted. Not one opened." and Darren's "He never said which." Weakest: the want arrives as a speech ("By Christmas, Ron. Mickey's runs the Hook.") with the player holding nothing; the choice is a dialogue menu. Mechanics only, apart from opening the envelope.

**05.** Strongest: Ron's palms, then the fist, and the keys hung on the bare hook: "It's on my rank, so it's on my board. Car three." Weakest: Danny is a stock charming rival (gold chain, bacon sandwich, "Nephew"), and the branch list sprawls. The action carries the meaning: where the player stops the car is who Frank is.

**08.** Strongest: the tally held out across Frank, and "Send Ron": "By the time the leaf is on its stop, fourteen backs are to the road." Weakest: five routes laid out like a systems sampler. Frank is pushed to the edge of his own scene ("There's one Frank on this gate"). The meaning sits in Ron's deed. The player only picks it.

**10.** Strongest: the shot taken for a rocket, "Somebody says 'Ooh.' Nothing breaks.", and "Next of kin?" / "Mickey's. Quay Street." Weakest: the old man has had one word a night, so the loss leans on mood more than on hours. The sugar-lump planning is twee. The action carries the meaning: "Stop" costs two cases on the stones.

**12.** Strongest: "Handbrake's yours, boss." The car leans on the brake until the player's own hand lets it go on a truth or a lie. Weakest: a reader cannot tell which line stands in the book, so the lie has no edge; most of the scene is watching. The meaning is carried, if thinly, by one release.

**14.** Strongest: the watch pawned for Mickey's flowers ("He'd no flowers. There were nobody to send any."), "Hang that on my hook, boss", and "Coming up on." left unfinished. Weakest: overloaded, with the cage, Rita, Wardell and the pen in one night, and the backwards drive repeats scene 01. The action carries the meaning: the pen.

**15.** Strongest: corking the gate with car two, settling by name, and "That's the one I change gear with. Keys. On the board." Weakest: the held-input crush is contrived ("The men at the bumper cannot see that"), and Kevin's grievance arrives cold. The action carries the meaning: the arm is the price of not shouting.

**17.** Strongest: car two put in behind the hearse; "Thirty-nine. Forty. Forty-one." Every door is open, "not open for him"; "Cammack's?" / "Mickey's." Weakest: the three bids are mechanical bracket clauses, and Agar and the Widow stay sketches. The action carries the meaning in the drive and the coat. The torch is a menu.

**19.** Strongest: the mirror of car three; Fewson's "No. I want to see what you do." Weakest: a climax that is a fist fight with a nuisance over a ledger carried forty yards, with Ron standing mute at the bonnet. Mechanics mostly: the fight settles a price that the bond, not fists, should settle.

## 3. The three weakest scenes

1. **19 The quay at four.** Make Danny's price fall on Ron and not on the chalk. For example: the book goes to Ellis unless Frank names Ron as the carrier of all eleven nights. Then the last face-off puts the opponent and the bond in one frame, and Frank must choose with his own word, not his fists.
2. **02 The ask on the rank.** Cut the vow to two lines and play the carry in the same scene: the walk down in Kirby's coat and the first "Mickey's?" from the old man under the overhang. The want then lands in the hands on night one.
3. **12 Lesson five.** Let the camera read the day-book line over Frank's shoulder before Ron asks. The player then knows exactly what a lie would hide, and the handbrake release weighs what he chose.

## 4. Voices

Mostly distinct with names hidden. Ron is clipped and quay-trade ("Going astern", "Whoa", "Nowt coming"). Danny chatters and patronises ("nephew"). The old man has one word. Stathers is gate-formal.

**Sheila and Pauline sound alike:** both are dry, both say "love", and both do ledger wit ("I'm writing down that it was there" / "Board's for cars and the book's for fares"). **Garbutt and Agar** blur too: both are mild and formal. Frank, an incomer, speaks the Hook's idiom ("I'll not be late twice"), so he does not stand apart by ear.

## 5. Clichés and turns seen coming

- The tic announced, every scene: "Frank does up the middle button of his jacket. ... the player will learn what it means" (02, 05, 08, 15, 19).
- The vow speech, "By Christmas" (02).
- The charming rival with gold at the neck, eating, calling the lead "nephew" (05).
- The old man who says "Go home", then is shot (10). It was telegraphed by "Go home after. It's all I ever tell any of you."
- The gunshot mistaken for fireworks (10), a common film trope.
- "Put me down as family. There's nobody else coming." (10)
- The loyal man takes the rap for the boss (14).
- The bent detective selling a lost file: "Files lose things. Two hundred. You never met me." (14)
- The pawned watch for the funeral flowers (14). It works, but it is sentimental and expected.
- The callback left unfinished, "Coming up on." (14). It was seen coming the moment Ron "names nothing".
- The ending is the opening mirrored, car on the edge with "I want to see what you do" turned round (19). It was predictable once scene 05 dwelt on Fewson's seat and mirror.

## 6. Rule breaks and confusions

**Rule breaks:** none found. There is no drink and no gambling; the flask holds tea. No children appear, since "the lad" is always an adult. Nobody asks about Frank's past.

**Confusions:**
- "Maureen Jensen" (08) appears before the reader is told she is the Widow.
- Scene 10's header says the old man "has never called him anything but Mickey's", yet he says "Nowak".
- Which line stands in the book (envelope, fare, or "Sheila's line ... She put it to Mickey") is never made clear.
- Unexplained plan state: "night money", "Haddock's dear", "Frank signed first", "Garbutt's truce", "the search", Iveson, "a bought Ellis".
- Two Franks at the gate (deliberate, but it adds load).
- Regional terms ("tenfoot", "the pen") go unglossed.
- The brass key and the ghost name under CAR 2 imply that Danny drove for Mickey for nine years. This is only inferable.
- The Customs officer arrives "with his coat over his pyjama collar" at nine in the morning.
- "Thirty years" against "thirty-one years" for the book.

## 7. Recognition

Nothing copied. Loose echoes:
- *Mafia: The City of Lost Heaven*: a cab driver drawn into a crime firm. The premise is fairly close; the treatment is not.
- *The Long Good Friday* and *Get Carter*: northern and dockside British crime, and a man set on running the docks. Mood and ambition only.
- *The Italian Job*'s teetering vehicle, in the image of the car on the edge.
- *Boys from the Blackstuff* and *Brassed Off*: dockers' decline and pride.
- Fireworks covering a gunshot appears in many films.

The learner-driver docker who names the buildings feels original.

## 8. Distance from the bar

This reads as a strong 7 overall, with four scenes nearer 8. On page it is better written than most shipped crime games: exact, funny, and rich in consequence. It is still clearly short of RDR2's best. Those scenes put the lead's own body and fate at the centre of the loudest moment. Here the heaviest moments mostly belong to other men: the old man takes the bullet, Ron walks into the station, Ron takes the tally. Frank's deeds are clever, retellable stunts (the car on the edge, the cork in the gate, the backwards drive along the bollards), and the motifs repeat until they show (the button, the spelling, the held input, the reverse drive, "coming up on").

**The single thing most in the way is the climax.** The story's heart, Ron and the line with his initials, is settled in week five, and the last act ends in a fist fight with Danny over a ledger. The final scene does not bring the bond and the opponent into one reckoning.

## 9. The people, by their own deeds

1. **Ron Kirby.** He walks into the station unasked and signs; he opens the gate before the pen; he brings the L-plate car down the quay. Most scenes turn on him.
2. **Frank Nowak.** He puts Danny's car on the edge, corks the gate and holds the brake until the arm goes, and carries the old man. These are the deeds players retell, but they are often answered by others.
3. **Danny Cammack.** He parks across the rank and reads the book on the counter, then takes the book to the edge in car two. He is a steady pest more than a force.
4. **The old man from the landing.** One deed, grabbing the gunman's sleeve ("Not on here"), and it is the game's loudest.
5. **Craig Fewson.** He resets the seat, and later says "No. I want to see what you do". Quiet, but it pays off.
6. **Kevin Needham.** He calls the settle in the yard. It is a real deed, though it arrives cold.
7. **Sheila Dunn.** "Cash introduced, £23.50" is her deed. She writes things down; she does not act.
8. **Pauline Kitching.** She counts on the air. She is a voice, not a mover.
9. **The Widow.** Offstage. She undoes a coat and sends a man across the water. She barely drives anything shown.
