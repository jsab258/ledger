# Brief for the writer: a written Frank in the five key scenes (test)

Fixed on 24 November 2026 by the lane's lead before the writer ran.

You are the writer for LEDGER, a third-person crime game set in the Hook, the old port of the town of Meridian, in Britain, October to December 1990. The lead is Frank Nowak, 32, who inherits his dead uncle Mickey's minicab office and the half-dead outfit behind it. The story on paper is in production/lane/story/paper/ (in /home/claude/ledger).

## Where it stands

Earlier today seven deeds of Frank's own (frank-test/DEEDS.md, cards F1 to F7) were written into five key scenes (frank-test/scenes/): 01 the ferry and the office, 10 the Landing (a gun fired on every path), 14 the night between, 15 the yard, 17 the room at Grace's. Six blind readers (frank-test/readers/READER-G.md to READER-L.md) marked those five at 7.10 of 10 on average (01 7.0, 10 7.8, 14 7.7, 15 6.8, 17 6.2). No reader now calls Frank the quietest, but four of six still give the heaviest deeds and feelings to Ron, and call Frank's deeds feats of driving, often replies to someone else's move. Reader L: his inner life is a button, a spelling and a sum; he picks from menus of four or five outcomes; give him one moment where he wants something for himself that costs him. Reader K: more retellable deeds than anyone, but feats more than choices with a cost.

## The owner's decision (binding)

Test a fully written Frank in these five key scenes only. In each of the five, give Frank fully written lines of his own: his own voice, his wants and his feelings spoken and shown, as a written lead in a story game is written. Keep the deeds. Keep everything the readers praised. Aim the emotional weight of each scene at Frank: Ron keeps his weight (his walk into the station, "Hang that on my hook, boss", spelling the name to the sergeant, the buildings he names), but no longer carries it alone. Outside these five scenes, his ruling of 1 October stays as it is (Frank's lines "mixed": written for hellos, goodbyes and decisions, else live talk); this test is only about whether a written Frank in the key scenes lifts them. In these five, Frank's lines at each peak are one written line, not a list to pick from.

What that means in practice (the lead's reading of the readers; use your judgment):
- One want of Frank's own in each scene, said or shown, that costs him something he cares about, not only paint or liberty. The want runs across the five (for example: to be somebody's family on a street where nobody opened a door to him; to be owed nothing; to be the one who pays). Choose one and make it his.
- His feelings shown in what he does and the few things he says when it matters: about Mickey, whom he barely knew; about Ron; about being a stranger. He is not a stock hard man and does not make speeches. His voice is his own: sums and distances when he means it, but also a line or two in each scene that only he would say and that tells us what it costs him.
- The heaviest moment of each scene happens to Frank or is done by Frank, with Ron close. In 14, Ron's walk into the station stays the act it is; Frank's night must now be the other half of it, and what passes between them is the scene's weight.
- The player's choice moves into the hands where it can (as at lesson five's handbrake, the scene readers mark highest). Cut the route-and-outcome menus at each peak to two or three real choices, each with a cost Frank feels.
- The old man's death (10 to 17) was called thinly prepared: plant him better within 10 and 17 (a line or a habit of his, and Frank's tie to him) without adding scenes.
- Three readers said the reversing stunts repeat: keep the deeds, but do not add a reverse where one is not already the deed.
