**01 (7).** Strongest: Sheila counting his money and refusing it, "I'm writing down that it was there. Somebody always asks what a man started with." Weakest: the player only walks and listens; the bare third hook is planted a little loudly. Action carries mechanics only, though leaving the money on the counter is a small true choice.

**02 (6).** Strongest: Darren's "By Christmas. He never said which." Weakest: Frank's want arrives as a set speech, "And when they say Mickey's, they'll mean me," the only authored lines he has and a stock kingdom line. The yes/no to the envelope carries meaning; the speech is not the player's.

**03 (7).** Strongest: the torch stopping on the boarding-house line and the van leaving unlit; menace without a threat. "Somebody's started writing you down." Weakest: the player's part is to say one word; the three-times refusal is a mechanic. Meaning is in the coat-or-suit choice, lightly.

**04 (7).** Strongest: a throwaway answer to a bookkeeper becomes the game's spine, and nothing says so: "I can't carry half a line forward. What was it?" Weakest: on the day it is flat; £2.80 is the only felt stake. The player's own words carry the meaning, which is rare and good.

**05 (8).** Strongest: Ron's palms beckoning at the edge, then the fist; Danny's real delight, "He's moved my motor." Weakest: the twenty-pound-note sums and receipt business slow the payoff. The drive is the meaning: how far to the edge is who Frank is.

**06 (7).** Strongest: the meter and "Six minutes, the whole Hook"; the ghost of D. CAMMACK under CAR 2, "He used his cuff." Weakest: it is a priced tour, an exposition ride with patter. The chalk choice carries meaning; the drive itself is a passenger seat.

**07 (8).** Strongest: the rule that what Ron names he has seen, and his silence at the pen: "I'd seen it. I just didn't say it." Weakest: the lorry and bollard beats repeat the same stop/handbrake test three times. Action carries meaning: one word, "Stop", decides whether Ron is shamed.

**08 (7).** Strongest: the tally offered across Frank, "That's a day, Ron," and the eldest packer: "I danced at your wedding." Weakest: six routes (pay, bluff, push, send, round, walk) spread the scene thin, and the bluff rule reads as a system. "Send Ron" carries real meaning; the others are mechanics.

**09 (7).** Strongest: the red book left open on the counter while Sheila answers the phone: "R. Kirby. R. Kirby. R. Kirby." Weakest: three branches of book-keeping talk before the reveal; laundering is explained, not felt. Looking at the book is the meaningful act; the rest is clerical.

**10 (8).** Strongest: the learner car coming through the fireworks crowd at eleven miles an hour, "Coming up on the ticket window. That's the ticket window." Weakest: the player's set piece is to hold a button; the gun in the belt is stock. Meaning is in Ron's driving, not the player's hands.

**11 (8).** Strongest: the call by surname, and "Mister Kirby" in the rain: "Nobody in that pen has been called Mister in his life." Weakest: the fight branch is generic and the radio opening is a long run-in. The player's chosen word carries the whole meaning.

**12 (9).** Strongest: Ron pulls the handbrake himself at the crossing, asks his one question, then: "Handbrake's yours, boss." Weakest: the leak-keyed line is visibly a slot ("That's Grace's. That's the window."). The release of the brake carries the meaning; the best scene here and near the bar.

**13 (8).** Strongest: "I'm asking you to remember it the same way twice," and the player writing from memory. Weakest: Ellis's lines are slightly too shaped, and "R.K. Kirby, R. Door. It's all right. I can read" is a writer's flourish. The writing act carries meaning fully.

**14 (8).** Strongest: Ron through the wall, "Say nowt," and the sergeant's "Knowak" paid off by Ron's "Nowak. N, O, W, A, K." Weakest: the three priced doors (£200, £800) read as a shop menu, and Frank spends the heaviest scene sitting. Signing first carries the meaning; the bribes are mechanics.

**15 (6).** Strongest: the bulb set down whole on the step, and Terry's "Now then. Where to?" Weakest: a roll-call of grudges, one line each ("You should've give me my cap"), and a wheel brace. A brawl any crime game has. The arm choice carries some meaning; most is mechanics.

**16 (8).** Strongest: the haddock already cut for one hand, and the Widow tying his laces: "That's a dinner you owe me, Mr Nowak, and a pair of laces." Weakest: the tally speech is overexplained. Putting his foot up is a small act with all the meaning in it.

**17 (6).** Strongest: the one-armed man unable to get his coat off while three rivals watch, "Leave him. He can ask." Weakest: a mob sit-down where each head states terms in turn; Agar appears for the first time at the summit, with a paper the reader never saw fetched. The coat carries meaning; the terms are a menu.

**18 (7).** Strongest: "A man should say when he's not coming," and "Mind the bollard." Weakest: the free half is one idea (he no longer names things) at length; "Walked into a door" is stock. Ringing the test centre and naming the buildings oneself carry the meaning.

**19 (6).** Strongest: Fewson's "No. I want to see what you do," Danny's own line come back. Weakest: the villain dangling the evidence over the water, "You had the book, nephew. I am the book," is a stock beat with a written-for-trailer line. Mostly a fistfight; the meaning sits in what Danny is worth afterwards.

**20 (7).** Strongest: the ruler drawing a fourth column the book never had; "Thirty-one years I've written what I was told. You write this one." Weakest: procedural, three readings, Hebblewhite's verdict lines; it plays as a final three-way button. The writing carries meaning, strongly in "Write it true".

**21 (6).** Strongest: Frank reading the carbon and sending his own man to the van: "Chandler's. Landing. Ten past six." Weakest: a walk past a chorus of callbacks, each person saying his line; the Man in Black and "Frankie, top of the ladder" are opaque. One real act; the rest is a lap of honour.

**23 (6).** Strongest: Lily's "One," and the order put on the shelf for nobody. Weakest: a stranger off the boat with a suitcase and a typed envelope; the circle is seen coming from the first page. The player mostly watches from a doorway; mechanics only.

## 3. The five weakest, and the one change each

1. **15, the yard.** Cut the roll-call of grudges. Let one man Frank has dealt with on screen (Fewson or Kevin) lead it for a want of his own, so the fight is a conversation with fists rather than a wave.
2. **17, the room at Grace's.** Introduce Agar in play by week two and have the heads' terms pull against each other across the cloth, so the table is a fight between three people, not three menus read in turn.
3. **19, the quay at four.** Replace the book held over the water with leverage only Frank cares about (Ron's name, the red book): Danny walking to Ellis with it himself. Then the fight is to stop a betrayal, not to save an object.
4. **21, the Kingdom close.** Drop the chorus of callbacks. Keep the carbon and the car sent unlit, and give the remaining time to one face-to-face price: Ron at the hatch, with something said that cannot be unsaid.
5. **23, Burn Both.** The stranger with the suitcase is foreseeable. Have Frank asked "Mickey's?" or handed the new firm's envelope to carry, so he is the errand now, not a man watching his replacement arrive.

## 4. Voices

With names hidden, most lines can be placed. Ron is unmistakable: clipped, "Aye", "nowt", the naming. Danny is patter and "nephew". The Widow is formal and dry, "I shall". Agar talks in the third person to the chair. Ellis is procedure. Stathers is the rulebook.

Alike: **Brenda Laverack and Grace Rinaldi.** Both are seaside quip-merchants with one line each ("I've had faster trestles" could be Brenda's, "You can eat that one-handed" Grace's). **Sheila and Pauline** also share "love" and the dry put-down; "I only write what I'm told, love" and "I write what I'm told" are the same voice in two mouths. Frank, being mostly player-written, has no voice to compare.

## 6. World rules and reader confusion

Rules: no alcohol, gambling or children seen. One doubt: scene 14 variant, "Stathers is on the bench beside Ron at midnight with a flask"; say tea, or it will be read as drink. Rita's pawn ticket is not gambling. "I danced at your wedding" and "since I was courting" are fine.

Confusing to a reader without the plan:
- "Frank holds the second rung" (07), "the third rung" (10), "the top of the ladder" (21): the name ladder is never shown.
- "Garbutt has said the week's aim" (10, 11, 15). Garbutt's role and his truce appear only at 17.
- "The Core holds the line" (12, 13): a production term in the scene.
- Agar's paper (17), "Haddock's dear" (08), Tharratt's five mornings (08), "the honest firm", "the first man", "the man Frank hired a fortnight ago" (10), "number nine" at Lily's, Alison or Philip Danby (21, 22), Julie (15, 16), the Man in Black (21, 22), M. Suddaby (09; presumably Mickey).
- 11: "as on the ferry rank in week two", Ron at Frank's back in a fight. No such fight is in 07.
- The Widow's "fire" (16) is a hook with nothing behind it in these scenes.
- Ellis "bought" before the search (13, 14) assumes a scene not here.

## 7. Recognition

No scene copies a published work. Nearest:
- **Mafia (2002):** the cab driver drawn into an outfit. Loose; only the premise texture.
- **Get Carter (1971):** a man arriving in a northern port town for a dead relative's affairs. Moderate in atmosphere; nothing in plot.
- **The Long Good Friday:** a British boss wanting the docks, and a sit-down of heads. Loose.
- **The Godfather:** the dangled-evidence and summit beats are genre stock rather than borrowings.

The driving lessons, the naming of buildings and the book with a column for who brought it in feel original.

## 8. How far from the bar

At its best (lesson five, the pen, car three, the Widow's laces, Ron through the wall), this is close to the bar. The motifs are planted with patience and paid off in the player's hands as RDR2 does at its best. A careless answer to a bookkeeper becomes an arrest and an ending, a driving lesson becomes the grammar of trust, and its loss is felt as silence on the dock road. The last third weakens into stock forms (the yard brawl, the summit, the book over the water, a chorus of callbacks), and the scenes lean on a plan the reader never sees. The single thing most in the way is the lead. Frank is a space where the player types. After his one speech in scene 02 he has almost no written voice, temper or habit. The heaviest scene happens to Ron while Frank sits on a bench, and everyone round him, from Ron and Sheila to Danny and the Widow, is more vivid than he is. RDR2's best scenes stand on Arthur's voice. These stand on Ron's.
