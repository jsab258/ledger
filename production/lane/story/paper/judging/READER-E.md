Mean 7.3. One scene at 9, none at 10.

**01 (7).** Strongest: Sheila writing "New management, cash introduced, £23.50" and handing the money back: "I'm writing down that it was there." Weakest: it is a walk and a guided tour; nothing is at risk but £18. The hands carry only mechanics (walk, listen); the meaning (a name nobody takes) is all in other people's mouths.

**02 (7).** Strongest: Ron's "Left alone" and Darren's puncture, "By Christmas. He never said which." Weakest: Frank's speech ("By Christmas, Ron. Mickey's runs the Hook") is a stated want delivered as a set speech, the stockest moment in the script. The choice (carry, refuse, wait) carries meaning, because refusing is final and Ron says so twice.

**03 (8).** Strongest: the torch stopping on "9.40, boarding house, cold-store road", the van leaving unlit, and "Somebody's started writing you down." The player learns, by handing it over, that he has just sold a fare to men who hurt people. Weakest: the man's three-times "Mickey's?" refusal loop is a game-ish gate. The hand-over carries the meaning.

**04 (7).** Strongest: the choice of words becomes the line the whole game turns on; Pauline's "First in thirty-six year. Does he get the half?" Weakest: as played, it is a menu answer to a bookkeeper and paying £2.80; a player will not know it matters. Meaning is carried, but only in retrospect.

**05 (8).** Strongest: Ron walking ahead to the coping and beckoning, then the fist: "Whoa." The deed only Frank would do, answered by Danny's real laugh. Weakest: the image is close to a famous film ending (section 7), and the twenty-pound account sub-branch is fiddly. The player's driving carries the meaning: how far he goes is who he is.

**06 (7).** Strongest: "Did he say owt about me? In the letter," and the lie option ("He said you'd kept a key") that Danny half believes for five weeks. Weakest: the three-doors tour is exposition with prices ("A fiver a car, a week"). The chalk on the board carries meaning; the ride itself is mechanics.

**07 (8).** Strongest: the naming rule taught as comedy ("We've been overtaken by a milk float." "He knows the road.") and then broken in silence at the pen. Weakest: Brenda's postcard line is the only smut and is telegraphed. The player's "Stop" carries real meaning: stopping at the pen shames Ron in front of his own.

**08 (7).** Strongest: "Send Ron", and fourteen backs turning to the road. Weakest: five options laid side by side read as a menu; Stathers exists to state rules. The best route (Send Ron) is a choice whose cost lands in silence, so the action carries meaning; the others are tactics.

**09 (7).** Strongest: the red book open on the counter while Sheila takes a call, "R. Kirby. R. Kirby. R. Kirby," then the brown pages of M.S. Weakest: a lot of laundering arithmetic ("ten pounds and four fifths") and the theme said aloud: "There's no column for who it was brought to." Looking is the action, and it carries the meaning.

**10 (8).** Strongest: the L-plate car coming through the crowd at walking pace, arm out, "Coming up on the ticket window." Weakest: the job itself is holding a button on a cage; the plan in tea things is a stock bit; the gun is shown and put away. The hold carries meaning only because Ron comes; as action it is thin for a midpoint.

**11 (8).** Strongest: "Kirby" against "Mr Kirby" against "Ron" ("That's not a call. I've three Rons."), and the stand-with-him route. Weakest: Stathers again explains the rules aloud to the pen. The player's choice of a single word carries the whole meaning; this is the script's best use of a dialogue choice.

**12 (9).** Strongest: Ron puts the handbrake on himself, asks "That first night. What did you have her put in the book?", then "Handbrake's yours, boss." The player must release it on whatever he said. Weakest: the variant line naming the watched leak is clumsy. The hands carry the meaning completely; this stands near the bar.

**13 (7).** Strongest: "I'm not asking you to remember everything, Mr Nowak. I'm asking you to remember it the same way twice." Weakest: making the player type the line from memory turns a moral moment into a recall test, and an arrest for false accounting on one line is thin. The writing carries meaning; the memory test muddies it.

**14 (8).** Strongest: Ron spelling "Nowak. N, O, W, A, K" to the sergeant, and "Hang that on my hook, boss." Weakest: the four lit windows are a shop of escapes, priced, and the heaviest deed is Ron's, not Frank's. The steps carry meaning (going back up costs Ron "the one thing I had to give").

**15 (6).** Strongest: "Fridays", said by Terry and Ron, and Darren's pound on the fish box. Weakest: a queue of minor names (Lee, Julie, Screeton, Kevin) many players will barely know, and two set-ups to break the arm, each "refused by somebody" first, which reads as contrivance. Partly meaning (who stands by him), partly mechanics.

**16 (7).** Strongest: the haddock cut to fork size and "I never ask anybody for anything, Mr Nowak. I find out what they're short of." Then the laces. Weakest: the kind matriarch feeding the beaten hero is a familiar turn, and the player only eats and lifts a foot. The deed is hers; his action is small but does carry meaning (letting her).

**17 (6).** Strongest: the coat ("Leave him. He can ask.") and the four pounds from the dead man's FUNERAL tin. Weakest: handing three items round a table is a transaction screen, and it rests on jobs these scenes never show; Agar is a cipher. Mostly mechanics.

**18 (7).** Strongest: "A man should say when he's not coming," and "Mind the bollard." Weakest: the free half is a reprise of the lessons with the naming removed; affecting once, but it repeats a device the script has already used to the full. The test-centre call carries meaning; the drive is mood.

**19 (6).** Strongest: Fewson's "No. I want to see what you do," Danny's own line turned on him. Weakest: Danny's turn has no visible cause in play since week two, the fight is fists again, and Ellis waiting forty yards off in a plain car while the whole street watches is staged. The fight is mechanics; the choice after it carries meaning.

**20 (8).** Strongest: "Thirty-one years I've written what I was told. You write this one," and the ruler: Frank rules the fourth column himself. Weakest: the climax is a man writing in a book, and Hebblewhite's "a great deal more honest, and a great deal more expensive" comes close to saying the theme. The hand on the ruler carries the meaning.

**21 (7).** Strongest: Frank reading the carbon and saying "Chandler's. Landing. Ten past six", becoming the man under the overhang, and "Say Frank. Once." / "Boss." Weakest: a curtain call of townsfolk with one line each (Darren, Lily, the Man in Black, the Widow) like a checklist. The carbon reading carries meaning, if the player sees it.

**22 (8).** Strongest: Ron naming the gate as it is chained, and "Where to, Frankie?" Weakest: the echoes are stacked so tight (envelope, "never bought one in his life", Lily, L-plate, "Where to") that it tips into sentiment. The last answer is the player's, and the drop finishes the line: action and meaning are one.

**23 (7).** Strongest: Ron at the gate in the Widow's jacket: "Mr Nowak." Weakest: the symmetry is seen coming from the first page (the boat, Gordon, "You'll want telling the way"; the man saying "Cammack's?"). Envelope or suitcase is a real choice and carries meaning.

## 3. The five weakest, and the one change each

1. **17, The room at Grace's.** Cut the three-item hand-out. Make the tea one act, with the coat as the whole test, and have one head ask Frank, in front of the others, for one thing he cannot give without betraying Ron or Sheila.
2. **19, The quay at four.** Show Danny's rise in play in weeks four to six (the ferry rank taken in front of Frank, a driver poached at the rank, the unit three doors up lit), so the quay is a reckoning and not a mugging with a book.
3. **15, The yard.** Fill the queue only with people the player has driven with or paid by hand, and give the arm one cause that Frank chooses. Drop the two set-ups each "refused by somebody" first.
4. **13, Write it again.** Drop the typed-from-memory test. Show the player his own line and let him choose: copy it, change it, or refuse the pen. The scene should test his nerve, not his memory.
5. **21, Close: the Kingdom.** Cut the curtain call to the carbon and the hatch. The ending is Frank reading "Chandler's. Landing. Ten past six" in the old man's way, and Ron's "Boss"; the Man in Black, Darren and Lily dilute it.

## 4. Voices

With the names hidden I could tell Ron ("Aye", "nowt", "were", "I know about doors"), Pauline (runs on, "two real minutes", "pet"), Danny ("nephew", quick, pleased with himself) and Sheila ("love", sums, "I don't carry nothing forward") at once. The Widow is close behind.

Alike: **Hebblewhite and Garbutt** (and Agar with them): the same measured, wry official cadence. "I don't open a book early. It isn't fair to the book" could be Garbutt; "I'd not want it said anybody here gave me anything" could be Hebblewhite. **Ellis and the Widow** share the same chiselled aphorism ("I write everything down; that's what you're paying for" / "I find out what they're short of"). More widely, nearly everybody speaks in the writer's own short, turned epigram ("It's brass. It'll keep." "It read different through a wall."), which flattens the secondary cast. Frank is the hardest to place: he has few lines, and most of them are the player's to pick.

## 5. Clichés, stock lines and turns seen coming

- 02: the vow speech, "By Christmas... Mickey's runs the Hook."
- 01 and every later scene: the button tic is announced ("the camera always shows it"), then told again ("the player will learn what it means").
- 10: the plan laid out with the tea things; the heavy opens his coat to show a gun, "so you can't say you weren't shown".
- 14: the old retainer takes the fall for the young boss.
- 14, 18, 20: the dead man's watch that runs five minutes fast, and the pawn ticket redeemed.
- 16: the crime matriarch feeding the beaten hero.
- 17: "Somebody has to be family."
- 18: "Walked into a door."
- 19: the fight on the same quay edge where car three hung; seen coming from 05's own note.
- 21 and 22: the man who only says "boss" is asked to "Say Frank. Once"; the payoff "Frankie" is foreseeable.
- 23: leaving on the boat he came on, with Gordon's matching line ("You'll want telling the way").
- The Man in Black's counted verdicts ("Six. The ham was good.") are a chorus device used twice.
- Generally: almost every scene ends on an echo of an earlier line. The pattern becomes the expectation, and the last act is too neat.

## 6. Rule breaks and confusions

No breaks of the content rules found: no drink (Stathers brings a flask of tea), no gambling, no children (the "youngest" packer is a working adult), no questions about Frank's past. The dates hold for 1990 (Monday 15 October, Friday 16 November, Saturday 1 December), and the caution is the pre-1994 wording.

Points that confuse a reader without the plan:
- Unseen systems named as if known: "the second rung", "the third rung", "the day of looking", "ways in", "Garbutt has said the week's aim", "the three goes at Frank's men", "Haddock's dear", "Friday means Friday", "number nine" and Lily's counting.
- The leaks in 12 and 13 (Danny in the phone box, Iveson at Ellis's car, a crewman at the caff window) come from scenes not among the 23.
- Mickey's watch at Rita's: how it got there is never shown.
- The old man from the landing is "lifted" (12) and then buried (16, 17); how he died is never said.
- Who the "old firm" is, and how it relates to the Widow or Agar, is unclear. What the van did at the boarding house, and why Danny's lodger was hurt, is only implied.
- The Widow's cases ("Sixty a case") are contraband of an unnamed kind. In a world without drink the reader wonders what they are.
- Plausibility: a learner driving fare-paying packers for a cab firm (08); a cab owner walked onto a remand wing at lock-up (21); an arrest for false accounting resting on one day-book line (13).
- 21: Frank reading the carbon and sending a man out marks him as the new old firm. It is subtle enough that many players will miss it.

## 7. Recognition

- **On the Waterfront**: the dock shape-up with brass checks and a foreman calling names (08, 11) is close in mechanism, though the "call him by surname" turn is new.
- **The Italian Job**: a car hanging over the edge with its wheels turning (05, 06, 19) is a near echo of that film's last shot.
- **Mafia (the first game)**: an ordinary man drawn from a cab firm into a crime family. A loose likeness in premise only.
- **Breaking Bad**: laundering through a working business with a bookkeeper who reasons it out ("The book will hide ten pounds and four fifths"). A moderate likeness for Sheila.
- **The Godfather**: the heads of the firms at one table under a broker (17). A loose likeness.
- **Get Carter** and **The Long Good Friday**: northern-port and docks gangland in decline. Tone only.
- The lessons and the naming of buildings I do not recognise from anywhere. They are the script's own.

## 8. How far from the bar

This is closer to the bar in craft than most shipped crime games. The early deed coming back (03 and 04 into 13 and 20), the bond built in a car through a rule the player learns with his own hands, and the handbrake in lesson five are as well made as anything on the reference shelf. Lesson five would stand beside the best of it. What holds the whole about a point and a half below Red Dead Redemption 2's best is the lead. Ron, Sheila, Danny and the Widow each have a deed a player will retell; Frank's are correcting his name, doing sums, and making choices off a menu of other people's offers. The heaviest night in the game is Ron's sacrifice, watched and priced by Frank. Beside that, the set pieces are small (fists, a held cage, a man writing in a book), so the story's loudest moments are quiet ones. The single thing in the way is a lead with no fire of his own: give Frank a temper, an appetite or a deed in the middle weeks that only he would do and that costs him in his body, and the rest of this is already near the bar.
