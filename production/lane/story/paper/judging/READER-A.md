## 3. The five weakest scenes, and the one change that would gain most

1. **04, the line in the book.** Put Ron at the counter while Frank answers, so the first lie or truth is said in front of the man whose initials are on it. That makes the seed hurt now, not only in week five.
2. **17, the room at Grace's.** Cut the three terms readings down to one live threat at the table: the Widow lays tally 39 on the cloth and asks, in front of Danny, whose Ron is. Let the coat and that question be the scene.
3. **15, the yard.** Drop the roll call of grievances ("Each of them says one thing, and each thing is a deed of Frank's"). Let the attackers be silent and recognisable by what they carry (the cap, the wheel brace), so the player names the debt himself.
4. **23, Burn Both.** Remove the arriving stranger who repeats the opening. End on Ron hanging the tally on the Widow's hook with his back to Frank, the loss seen in a person, not a device.
5. **10, the Landing.** Give the midpoint a danger in Frank's own body that changes play: the shown gun is drawn and fired, and from that night the Hook carries guns. Holding a button while Ron drives is too little for the loudest scene of the middle.

## 4. Voices

Ron, Danny, the Widow and Brenda can be told with the names hidden: Ron's clipped Yorkshire ("Nowt coming", "I know about doors"), Danny's patter ("I'm not greedy, I'm just first"), the Widow's grand dryness ("I never ask anybody for anything, Mr Nowak. I find out what they're short of").

Alike:
- **Sheila and Pauline.** Both say "love", both deal in dry office put-downs: Sheila's "It's my stapler. Use the milk." could be Pauline's; Pauline's "I only write what I'm told, love" is Sheila's creed ("I write what I'm told", 13).
- **DS Ellis and Hebblewhite.** Both are measured officials who speak in neat epigrams: "I'm asking you to remember it the same way twice" against "I don't open a book early. It isn't fair to the book." Stathers and Garbutt share the same register.
- Frank has no voice to compare. His scripted lines would fit on a postcard.

More broadly, the whole town speaks in finished, quotable one-liners. A street where everybody is a wit stops sounding like a street.

## 6. Rule breaks and confusions

Rules. No alcohol, gambling or children seen. One doubt: in 14, Stathers sits "on the bench beside Ron at midnight with a flask". A reader assumes spirits; make it tea. Nobody asks Frank's past. The dates check (Monday 15 October, Friday 16 November and Saturday 1 December 1990 fall on the right days), and so does the pre-1994 caution.

Confusions for a reader who has not seen the plan:
- Undefined system words: "the second rung", "the third rung", "the Ledger takes its first aim", "the Core", "the honest firm", "the first man", "the better turn", "the day of looking".
- Garbutt sets "the week's aim" from week four but is first seen in 17. What he is, and why the heads obey his table, is never shown.
- People and events never staged: Billany's cap "in week two"; "as on the ferry rank in week two" (11); Tharratt's five mornings; Haddock's dear; Friday means Friday; Lee Drewery; Alison and Philip Danby; the Widow's fire; Mickey's revolver.
- How Mickey's watch got into Rita's window is never shown before 14.
- "Number nine" at Lily's, "one" and "two", needs a scene that is not here.
- The Man in Black counting ("Five. No flowers, and done in the dark"; "Seven. Half a firm") is opaque.
- What the old firm moves (cases at £60) and why it reads fare sheets is only half clear. The van and the strapped hand suggest violence the scenes never own.
- Why Customs and Excise inspects a cab office's books is never said.
- 14: the interview room hears through a cell wall, a staging convenience a reader notices.

## 8. Distance from the bar

This is a good game's story, with four or five scenes (11, 12, 14, 16, 22) a fine shipped crime game would be proud of. It is still a clear step below Red Dead Redemption 2's best. Its craft is unusual and real: one half line, said in the player's own words on the second morning, carries the arrest, a friend's liberty and the last choice, and a driving lesson's rule ("what Ron names, he has seen") becomes the sound of trust and of its loss. But the bar scenes are loud. They put the lead's body, his loyalty and his death in the player's hands. This story's climaxes are a man writing in a ledger before a VAT inspector and a fist fight on a quay, and its middle circles one street, one counter and one dock road. The single thing that stands most in the way is Frank. The scenes hand him to the player, "From here Frank's words are the player's", and give him no temper, no history, no joke and no wound of his own. So every heavy scene is carried by Ron, Sheila, Danny or the Widow, and the lead the player is meant to want to be is an empty seat at the centre of a town full of people more alive than he is.
