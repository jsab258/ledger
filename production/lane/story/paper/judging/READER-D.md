Mean 6.5. No scene reaches 9. Two reach 8.

**01 (6).** Strongest: Sheila writing the £23.50 down and handing it back, "I'm writing down that it was there. Somebody always asks what a man started with." Weakest: nothing is at risk, and the name joke is laid on with a trowel ("Both names are mine"). The player only walks and listens, so the hands carry nothing yet.

**02 (5).** Strongest: Ron's "Left alone." and the envelope reused since Easter. Weakest: the speech. "By Christmas, Ron. Mickey's runs the Hook. And when they say Mickey's, they'll mean me." is the stock gangster vow, and Darren's comeback only half rescues it. The choice to carry, refuse or wait is real, but it is a menu.

**03 (7).** Strongest: the torch stops on "Boarding house. Cold-store road. Twenty to ten." and the van pulls away. The player sees he has delivered somebody, and he never sees who. Weakest: the man's three-times "Mickey's?" spells out the name theme. The hand-over carries the meaning: the player's own delivery does the harm.

**04 (5).** Strongest: "I don't mind who the passenger was, love. I mind that he paid." Weakest: this is a dialogue menu about wording in a ledger, at a counter, with Pauline's chatter as padding. It sets up the whole game, but as play it is a form to fill in. Only the mechanics carry it.

**05 (7).** Strongest: Ron's palms beckoning the car to the coping, then "Whoa", with the player braking on the fist. Weakest: the fare arithmetic reprises scene 04's joke ("Twenty-one minutes stood on our rank, first five free"). The action carries the meaning: an ownership claim made with a car's front wheels.

**06 (7).** Strongest: "Did he say owt about me? In the letter.", and the lie option that lets Danny half believe he was meant to keep the key. Weakest: the three-door tour is an exposition ride ("Her gate. A fiver a car, a week."). The letter choice carries meaning; the ride carries mechanics.

**07 (8).** Strongest: the mechanic teaches a meaning. "What Ron names, he has seen", and then he does not name the pen. "I'd seen it. I just didn't say it." Weakest: Brenda's smut line is the only stock note ("it's not the size of the car"). The hands carry the meaning completely: the stop word becomes how the player reads Ron.

**08 (6).** Strongest: tally 39 held out across Frank, and "Send Ron", where fourteen backs turn to the road. Weakest: the "one Frank on this gate" naming joke, and the packers' chorus is music-hall. Of six options, only Send Ron carries meaning; the others are tactics.

**09 (6).** Strongest: the red book left open while she answers the phone, "R. Kirby. R. Kirby. R. Kirby", then the brown pages of M.S. Weakest: it is the third counter scene in the same camera place, and "There's no column for who it was brought to" is the theme said aloud. Looking at the book is a real act, but the rest is talk.

**10 (7).** Strongest: the arm straight out of the window in the crowd, and "Coming up on the ticket window. That's the ticket window." Weakest: the gun shown under the coat, "That's for next time", is stock. The set piece itself is a held button at 11 mph. The hold does carry the meaning (alone, Frank cannot do it), but the danger is thin.

**11 (7).** Strongest: the call, "Kirby." / "Mr Kirby.", and "Go on, then. Mister Kirby." Weakest: five branches flatten the scene, and the fight branch is generic. The call by name carries the meaning precisely. It is the best choice-as-character in the set.

**12 (8).** Strongest: Ron draws the handbrake himself, asks "That first night. What did you have her put in the book?", then says "Handbrake's yours, boss." Weakest: "That's the box. That's where he stood" is cute recall. The player's hand releasing the brake is the meaning: whatever he said is now what Ron drives away with.

**13 (7).** Strongest: "I'm asking you to remember it the same way twice. Write it again." The player types the line from memory. Weakest: the arrest itself is procedural boilerplate, and the empty wall with Ron's coat on its hook is a familiar shot. The writing carries the meaning. It is the best idea in the set, though it asks a lot of a player's memory.

**14 (7).** Strongest: Ron's statement, "New. Didn't know what he had hold of.", then "Hang that on my hook, boss." Weakest: it is overloaded. There are four lit windows, bribe prices and a menu of exits, and Ron spelling "N, O, W, A, K" is the pay-off the reader saw coming in scene 01. Carrying a thing up the steps to Ron does carry the meaning.

**15 (5).** Strongest: Darren's pound coin, "I'm the only one stood in this yard who does." Weakest: a crowd of people the reader has barely met (Screeton, Julie, Iveson, Lee, Terry), and a sentimental "Fridays" chorus. Most of the choices are mechanics: pay order, the tin, the arm.

**16 (7).** Strongest: the haddock cut to fork size, and the Widow tying his laces in a double bow. Weakest: "I never ask anybody for anything. I find out what they're short of." and "You've not got a week" are matriarch-gangster stock. Putting his foot up is a small act that carries the meaning.

**17 (6).** Strongest: the coat, which Frank cannot take off one-armed with three heads watching ("Leave him. He can ask."). Weakest: the deals are traded as abstract objects ("the paper, the man, the load") whose stakes the scene must explain, and the Man in Black's verdict is a writer's device. The coat carries the meaning; the cloth trades are mechanics.

**18 (7).** Strongest, inside: "Mind the bollard." Strongest, free: the silent drive, which the player must now watch for himself. Weakest: "Walked into a door. I know about doors." is the stock prison line, half turned. In the free half, the absence of naming carries the meaning in the hands. That is excellent.

**19 (6).** Strongest: Fewson's "No. I want to see what you do." Weakest: the climax opponent's threat is to walk a ledger forty yards, with the detective conveniently parked at the top. The fistfight is physical but small, and the reversal was visible from scene 05. The fight is mechanics; the choice of what Danny is worth carries some meaning.

**20 (7).** Strongest: ruling a fourth column with Sheila's ruler, "It wants a fourth column, Mrs Dunn." Weakest: Hebblewhite's tidy epigrams ("It is a great deal more honest, and a great deal more expensive"), and four people each given one wise line in the five minutes. The pen and ruler carry the meaning fully.

**21 (5).** Strongest: Frank now reads the carbon and stops the finger two lines from the bottom. He has become the man with the torch. Weakest: the rest is a parade of tributes ("Frankie", "You're early", "I don't mark those"), and the cost is never felt in play. Mostly watched.

**22 (7).** Strongest: Ron lets the handbrake off "as he was taught", names the gate, then asks "Where to, Frankie?" Weakest: it is warm to the edge of sentiment, and the envelope with "5.40, Office" is one rhyme too many. The drive carries the meaning, though the player mostly sits.

**23 (6).** Strongest: Ron at the gate in the Widow's jacket, "Mr Nowak." Weakest: the mirror of scene 01 is total and expected (Gordon: "You'll want telling the way, the other side." / "I'll find it."). Choosing envelope or suitcase carries the meaning, but the rest is a walk past tableaux.

## 3. The five weakest scenes, and the one change for each

1. **21, the Kingdom.** Make the cost happen in play. After Frank reads "Chandler's. Landing. Ten past six", let him see what his line sets off, a van moving off unlit as in scene 03, instead of a procession of people paying tribute.
2. **15, the yard.** Cut the cast to Kevin, Screeton and the people the player has driven with. Build Kevin's forty-two miles into a betrayal the player has watched grow, so the settle is between two men rather than a payroll.
3. **02, the ask on the rank.** Cut the speech to one act and one sentence. Let the want come out of something only Frank would do on the first night, not a vow to "run the Hook by Christmas".
4. **04, the line in the book.** Give the hands something to do. Fold it into scene 05 or 09, or put Sheila's question while Frank is counting the rent short, so that choosing the words costs him in the same moment.
5. **19, the quay at four.** Raise what Danny holds over him beyond a walk to a car window. Let the fight turn on the letter Danny asked about in scene 06 ("Did he say owt about me?"), so the opponent's want is personal and the climax is not about carrying a ledger forty yards.

## 4. Voices

Most are distinct with names hidden. Ron is clipped Yorkshire docker ("Aye", "nowt", the buildings). Danny is quick and calls Frank "nephew". Sheila is arithmetic and "love". Pauline is radio patter. The Widow is formal and cold-kind.

Two who sound alike: **DS Ellis and Derek Hebblewhite.** Both speak in neat, balanced epigrams about records: Ellis, "I'm not asking you to remember everything... I'm asking you to remember it the same way twice"; Hebblewhite, "I don't open a book early. It isn't fair to the book." Garbutt and the Man in Black belong to the same family of tidy aphorists.

Frank is the least identifiable voice in his own game. His lines could be any dry Englishman's: "I'll try not to go mad", "A pound for a cup of tea. I'm in the wrong trade", "It's a start".

## 5. Clichés, stock lines and turns seen coming

- 02: the vow to run the town by a deadline, "By Christmas... Mickey's runs the Hook."
- 01 onward: the announced tic, "He does that before anything he means, and the camera always shows it." The spelled name, "N, O, W, A, K", appears in 01, 03, 08, 14 (twice) and 20.
- 07: "it's not the size of the car."
- 10: a gun shown, not drawn, with "That's for next time... so you can't say you weren't shown."
- 13: the empty wall and the coat on its hook as the hero is led out.
- 14: the old loyal man takes the fall for the young boss. Ron spelling NOWAK correctly was visible from scene 01.
- 16: the kindly matriarch boss, "I never ask anybody for anything. I find out what they're short of." / "You've not got a week."
- 18: "Walked into a door."
- 19: Fewson turning Danny's "I want to see what he does" back on him, seen coming from scene 05. Likewise "Leave my mirror alone" and "Mickey would've said sorry", each reprised.
- 21: the Widow's "Frankie", expected once nobody else had called him that.
- 22: Ron's silence broken by naming the gate, seen coming from scene 14.
- 23: the circular ferry ending, "You'll want telling the way, the other side." / "I'll find it."

The mirroring is so complete that the machinery shows. Almost every line from week one comes back word for word.

## 6. Rule breaks and confusions

- No alcohol, gambling or questions about Frank's past found. Tobacco only.
- **Possible child.** Scene 15, Grace: "Peter, he's woke. Fetch the car." Peter is never introduced. If he is Grace's son and young, he breaks the rule. He needs to be fixed as an adult.
- **Mickey's watch** arrives in scene 14 ("Rita's, first, if the watch is in it") with nothing in these scenes to plant it.
- **Lily Tang and "number nine"** first appear in scene 14 and become an ending refrain without setup.
- **M. Suddaby.** Scene 09's "a/c M. Suddaby" reads as a debtor until the reader works out it may be Mickey's surname. Nothing says so.
- **Unexplained machinery:** "the second rung", "the third rung", "Garbutt has said the week's aim", "the day of looking", "Haddock's dear", "Friday means Friday", "a pact with the Widow", the Widow's "fire", and "Ellis offered it on Thursday" (scene 20 refers to a scene not among these).
- **Key continuity.** Scene 01 has car two's key staying in the car ("Stops in her"); scene 06 has "The car's own key is where it has been all day: on its hook."
- **Ron's thirty years.** Ron's "Mickey's arrangement were mine to carry. Thirty year" is a cover story, but it sits against "Thirty-six year" in the pen and the red book's M.S. pages. A reader may take it as a continuity slip rather than a lie.
- The dates check: Monday 15 October, Friday 16 November and Saturday 1 December 1990 all fall on the right days, and Remembrance Sunday falls on 11 November.

## 7. Recognition

- **Get Carter (1971):** the nearest film. A man arrives in a northern port after a relative's death and takes on the local firms. The setting and premise are close; the plot is not.
- **Mafia: The City of Lost Heaven:** a cab firm as the way into organised crime. Loose.
- **Red Dead Redemption 2:** the red book as a moral record, like Arthur's journal and honour, and an early routine job returning as ruin, like the Downes debt. Deliberate in structure, not copied.
- **The Long Good Friday:** a firm's reach over a dying dockland. Distant.

No scene is lifted.

## 8. How far from the bar

This is a careful, unusually unified piece of writing. Its best scenes, lesson one, the pen, lesson five and the counter at five, tie a mechanic to a meaning more tightly than most shipped crime games manage. Ron's naming of buildings, and then his silence, is a device worthy of the bar. But the set sits a clear step below Red Dead Redemption 2's best. Its climaxes are bookkeeping, and the loudest choice in the game is which words go in a ledger. Its set pieces run deliberately at eleven miles an hour. Every rhyme comes back so exactly that a player will feel the plan rather than the town. The single thing that stands most in the way is Frank. Everyone round him has a want, a deed and a voice: Ron walks into the station, Danny parks on the edge, the Widow ties the laces, Sheila keeps the true book. Frank has a button, a spelling and some quips. Until he does one thing in the middle weeks that only he would do, and that costs him in his own body or name before the arrest forces it, the story is Ron's, told from the passenger seat.
