## Setting
The cab office at five in the evening on the last day, with the street dark and the strip light on. A tax inspector is about to open the books. Two clocks disagree by five minutes. The mood is still and waiting.

## People
Abel inspects the books, and has passed four of five mornings. Ada keeps them, and has written what she was told for thirty-one years. Bess is a detective by the door with her case. Cora works the radio. Bram is the doorman. The lead must write one line under a name he can live with.

## What happens
Cora told every car the books open at five. Abel would not open early, so there were five minutes, and the lead could speak to anyone in the room. At five Abel read the first week's cash entry, and Ada said it was all that was there. He found two entries for one night, one in two hands and one a month later in the lead's initials, and asked which was the firm's. Ada turned the book to face the lead, laid her pen in the gutter and told him to write this one. The lead wrote the true account in a third line. Ada brought out a second, hidden book. The lead ruled a new column down the page, for who each job was brought to, and put his own name in it. Abel said it was more honest and more expensive. Bess said a man cannot sign another man's column. Cora took off her headphones. Abel left.

## The lines that matter
Abel asks which entry is the firm's. Ada says she has written what she was told for thirty-one years, and now the lead writes. Bess says there is no column for who the jobs were brought to. Abel says that not everybody produces it, and the lead did.

## What the player does
The lead may leave the entries as they stand, write the account true, or put a rival's name to it, which costs the rival a charge. Anything else is read as leaving it. Each choice decides who is charged and whether the firm survives.
