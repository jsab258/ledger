# LEDGER story bible v2: script editor's note

Read in the order the bible asks: sections 1 to 15, then section 0. Judged with the section 0 changes in place and inside the owner's frame.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7

1. Tom: 7. The want (his name said with warmth), the flaw (asks nobody for anything) and the fear (the suitcase) are clean and well set up, and the Ledger is a clever voice for an unvoiced lead. But the change is mostly authored text in his notebook, and the test of the flaw, the five calls, comes to every player whatever he has done, so the need is shown rather than earned.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Pauline, Reese and Agar are people, each with a habit, a want and a line you would quote. A few are names on a bench (Christine Pickering, Brenda's Derek, Ray Coultas) and June is underused for the daughter of the dead man.
3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow, who prices and never threatens, and Agar, who apologises before every blow and then walks down Quay Street in the rain, are first-rate and come at Tom in genuinely different ways. Cammack is the familiar one: the loud local lad who burns a car on Bonfire Night, saved only by the turn of his father's page.
4. The central mystery: 7. Mickey making Ada believe the fire was her idea, and writing the truth in the plainest book, is a real truth that reframes the second tea, "It wasn't his to say" and the letter. The proof chain holds by one absent man (the importer) and one improbable survival (his rent card), and Agar knows things he could not know.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The priest's forty-one empty envelopes, the badge test, the carry and the failed carry that writes "Nowak, T. £82" in the debt book are the best pages in the bible. It leans on "as written" for material a fresh reader cannot see.
6. Structure and pace: 6. The act shape is sound and the calendar pulls a slow player forward, but Act II's second half piles its set pieces into three weeks while the first half is spread thin.
7. The middle: 6. Each scene costs somebody something, which is right, but read end to end it is a ladder of rivals' rungs with the side stories doing the turning. The straight road in particular watches the club run around Tom.
8. Surprise against cliché: 6. The old woman at the window who turns out to run it is a well-worn crime-drama turn, and the bible signposts it hard (the first envelope, "there is no cat", Cyril on night seven). The second turn, that Mickey played her, is fresh and is what saves this score.
9. The endings: 8. Each close has a last scene, a last image and a line in two books, and "Nowak!" from the constable on the first night's quay is earned by the stand-or-run press from minute nine. "Stayed" is lovely. Ron's driving in every ending is the one weak joint (see section 3).
10. The side stories: 8. The watch, Norman Dent, Karen's wardrobe and Hal's tape are whole stories with turns and ends the town keeps. The master and the building is the thinnest.
11. Tone and humour: 8. Pauline as the bar, Reese's ride-along with the crabs, the ham with its own seat and Sheila's dry lines are actually funny and local, and the darkest scenes are rightly kept quiet.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 7. Most beats are things the hands do, and the hearing built from the town's memory is excellent. But several decisive beats are talk with one choice (the two widows, Agar's request), against the bible's own rule that talk decides nothing alone, and the climax's days are a man reading books aloud while Tom watches.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The proof of the truth hangs on an absent man and an improbable paper. Where: section 0 item 5, section 8 Wednesday, section 10 layer five. What is wrong: the importer, who was burned out while paying every week, never speaks. A man ruined that way would have gone straight to Cyril or to the row and said "I paid", and the street's version (insurance job, gone south) means an insurer and the brigade investigated him, which Ellis would know after a year. His rent card, initialled by Mickey, then survives in a box of office papers out of the building that burned. And on Wednesday night Agar asks for "one line in a fare book" to be lost, when nothing tells him the line exists and the line does not name him; what condemns Agar is the debt and the plot purchase. Why it matters: this is the climax's evidence, and a player who stops to think will feel the story arranging itself. Fix: give the importer one scene, findable across the water, where he says he paid to the last week and was called a liar by Cyril's people, and left because nobody would hear him. Move the rent card off the fire: his own receipts, or the collector's copy in Mickey's office that Sheila has had all along. Have Agar want the real book's pages on the £4,000 and the plot rather than the fare line, and set up his knowing they exist with one earlier line.

2. The second half of Act II is a pile-up and the first half thin. Where: section 7 scenes 10 to 20. What is wrong: Remembrance on the 11th, the anniversary on the 18th, the search on the 19th, the hearing on the 20th, the bolt cutters, Danny's bout on the 1st, June, Les and the sit-down all land in three weeks, on top of three rival ladders and Ellis, Garbutt and Les as further pressures. Before the Fifth, scenes 1 to 9 spread thinly across two weeks. Why it matters: players punish a slow middle and a hurried last act; this has both, in the wrong halves. Fix: move the licence hearing to Tuesday 30 October so Agar's ladder starts before the Fifth; move Les Coultas to late October, a first-half mystery the truth later explains; keep Remembrance and the anniversary as the late fixed points.

3. The straight road is passive. Where: section 7 scenes 3 (S), 8 (S), 12 (S), 18. What is wrong: on the straight road the club runs on without Tom, Darren carries it, Tom tails him without touching anything, the real book stays locked, and the cost scenes are mostly money short on a Friday. The S version of scene 12 has June's society "tried" while scene 18 says that is "where she says it first", a contradiction a state-driven game will hit. Why it matters: the bar says the road of the player who refuses the crime must not be thin. As written it is the road where the hands have least to do. Fix: give the S road its own hands-on ladder: Tom walking the round himself with an open book from scene 3, and breaking into his own locked room at the Fifth so the S player meets Mickey's words by deed. Make June's line in scene 18 the first time she says it, and move the school-hall meeting after it.

4. Tom's change is told by the notebook and tested by a scene every player gets. Where: section 3 "What he needs", section 8 Friday, section 14 "You never ask for owt". What is wrong: the flaw is that he asks nobody, but the player spends the game asking (for jobs, for silence, for help on the gate) and the systems have nothing that counts asking. Then the phone box gives every player five calls, so the man who never learned to ask still asks. The Ledger entries changing from money to people are written to state, not caused by a choice. Why it matters: Henry and Hans and Arthur Morgan are remembered because the player felt the change in play. Fix: make asking a tracked act from week one (taking Ron's chips, letting Grace not charge, asking Sheila rather than reading behind her back), let the flaw cost something visible before the Fifth, and let the phone box hold only as many coins as people he has let give him something, from none to five. A player with no one to call stands in the box in silence.

5. The midpoint twist is guessable and Cammack is stock. Where: sections 4, 6 (Friday round, night seven), 7 the Fifth; section 5 Cammack. What is wrong: Ada first in the book, Ada's "There is no cat", Cyril's "It wasn't his to say" and the tallyman in her front room under the firelight together tell a genre-literate player by the end of week one that the nice old lady is the boss, and the burned car on Bonfire Night under her window is the move anyone expects of the loud local lad. Why it matters: the bar's first punishment is the plot the player has seen before; the Fifth must turn, not confirm. Fix: hide Ada behind a false head that the player is invited to suspect: let Cyril's authority be real and visible in Act I (he gives an order, he refuses Ron), and drop the line about the cat, which is a wink. Give Cammack a first-half act that is not violence: he buys Akers Cars before the Widow can, or hires Kevin, so the car fire is the second move of a man already beating Tom on business.

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Private hire licences in 1990 were the district council's, heard by a licensing sub-committee at the town hall, not a panel at the Harbour Board. Move it to the council chamber; keep Agar's room at the Harbour Board.
2. Ron cannot drive in October and is driving car one, days or the gate's van by mid-December. Test waits ran to months, and a minicab needs a full licence and a badge. Put the endings' driving on the docks (the gate's van inside the dock estate) or date the last images to spring 1991 with a line saying he passed second time.
3. Ada "borrowing against the tenancy": a tenancy cannot be borrowed against. Make it a sale-and-rent-back she has spent, with rent arrears to Agar's agents.
4. A detective sergeant would not spend a year on a no-casualty warehouse fire. Make it her own unfinished file, kept open after it was closed, because she grew up on the row.
5. The VAT officer riding a cab route to check mileage, and Customs' seal on the door, are not how VAT visits worked. Keep the ride-along as comedy, but make it Reese's own habit ("I always ride a route"), and change the seal to a padlock from Agar's agents for unpaid rent.
6. "The only camcorder in town": by 1990 camcorders were common in British homes. Make it the only one on Quay Street.
7. An 80p minicab fare in 1990 is about half the going minimum. Make it £2; keep the fifty-pound note.
8. A man who delivered Agar the importer's plot in 1989 still owes him the £4,000 in 1990. Say the plot was meant to clear it and Agar never wrote it off.
9. On the straight road the Fifth burns car two but the bible never says how Terry is hurt, yet Terry is lost "whatever he does". Put Terry in the car on both roads.
10. Ellis asking for building plans before a search is odd. Have Pauline report her at the magistrates' getting a warrant.
11. A licensing panel would ask about previous convictions. Have the clerk read "no record disclosed" and move on, so nobody asks.

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review

No. Not yet, though it is closer than most. The town is true to a British port after the docks went, the opening is excellent, Ron is a friendship people would talk about, Agar in the rain and the two widows in the minicab are scenes a drama editor would fight to keep, and the fare book as the only honest book is a premise a reviewer would quote. What stops me: the climax's evidence rests on an absent man and a paper that should have burned, the midpoint turn is seen coming, the middle is crowded late and thin early, and the straight road gives the hands too little. Fix those and this becomes a story a reviewer names as a reason to play.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. The fare book as alibi and noose: Mickey's free Saturdays and the 18 November run, found by a mild, kind Customs officer whose father drove a cab, with "No charge" coming back as the Suitcase's last line.
2. Ron Kirby: the passenger seat, the map as a list of things that shut, the three lessons that stop being funny, and the empty seat that Pauline names on air.
3. The opening week: the priest's forty-one empty envelopes, the badge "Mickey wouldn't have asked", the carry and its honest failure that writes Tom's own name into the debt book, and Sheila's withheld "Mr Nowak".
