# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 7: a critic's review

I came to this expecting a crime story in a cab office. What I found is better than that, in places much better, and in two places weaker than it thinks it is. Here is the hard version.

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7 out of 10. It has three or four ideas I have not seen in a game before. A good story with a leaking mystery and a crammed last act is still a 7.

1. Tom: 8. The flaw is something the player does, not something he is told: a gift taken or paid for, one press at a time, and a page headed "Asked" that the game never points to. That is clever, and it gives an unvoiced man with no past a real arc. It stops short of 9 because his change comes mostly at one moment, the kitchen, rather than through the middle.

2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Ada, Pauline, Terry and Reese are people I would remember. Ron and Tom in one car for three weeks has a real chance of being this game's Henry and Hans. Kevin, Darren and Julie are thinner, and Julie only gets used as someone for things to happen to.

3. The antagonists: 7. The Widow is excellent: lawful, she keeps every deal, never lies when asked straight, and comes at Tom four different ways. Danny has a good late reveal in his father. Agar is a clock with a hat on, and his best scene hands the player a file.

4. The central mystery: 7. The truth is good and fair, and it changes how earlier scenes read: the fare book, the name she holds back, the seat, a year of good mornings. But the evidence points to the driver far earlier than the bible says it does (weak point 1). Making Sheila the driver also needs the owner's ruling, because the frame says she stays as written.

5. The opening and its retold scene: 8. The ferry, £23.50, the walk-round and the carry with three honest routes is a tight first hour. The sixth name in the debt book, "Nowak, T. £80", is the line players will quote.

6. Structure and pace: 6. Acts I and II are well measured. Act III tries to fit ten scenes into two and a half hours, and the ending conditions are written in jargon that nobody has turned into anything a player can feel.

7. The middle: 7. The week table is the best defence against a repeating loop that I have seen in a design document: a returning fare, a dusk job and something found in the count, with the round shrinking from twenty doors. The Empty Seat movement leans too heavily on watching and listening.

8. Surprise against cliché: 7. "The car went, and came back", found on a mileometer, is real surprise. "The quiet trusted one did it" is a familiar move in itself, and its clues are too loud.

9. The endings: 7. The better Kingdom close and the Straight Life close are earned and bittersweet, and they finish on strong last images: Sheila as a fare paying £2, and Ron at the wheel saying "You never asked." The plain turns are sketches, and eight states for a sixteen-hour game is more than the writing can carry.

10. The side stories: 7. Ivy and the sergeant, each protecting the other from a thing they both know, is the best thing outside the main line. The watch and the umbrella are good. "One way" is a premise without a story.

11. Tone and humour: 7. The humour is dry and belongs to the characters: Pauline on the radio, Terry's fifth word, the crabs, the gate lifted off its hinges with the padlock still locked. It is funny the way a good sitcom is, not the way KCD2's set pieces are. It needs one great comic sequence, not five nice ones.

12. Story and play feeding each other: 9. Driving the eleven miles yourself, the handbrake that has meant "somebody else goes, Tom stops them" all game and is best left alone at the end, the offers, Ron's lie that only works if you taught him to drive: this is what designers should be doing. It is the reason to make this game.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The mystery gives itself away at scene 10, not scene 15. What is wrong: when the seat is freed, the tally shows that car two's seat has been jammed fully forward since the fire. The player has watched Ron folded behind that same wheel every Sunday, and has heard Kevin complain about it all game, so both are ruled out. Mickey is on a ward and Terry is in car one. That leaves one short person who can drive and whose small upright hand wrote both lines. Section 7 says the player still thinks "the doorman drove" at the end of the Empty Seat. The player has handled evidence that says otherwise. Where: Act II, scene 10, and the summary that closes the second movement. Why a player feels it: a sharp player names Sheila three hours early and spends the third movement waiting for the story to catch up, which is exactly the "guessed in the first hour" complaint. Fix: move the seat find to the third movement. Scene 10 rests on the books and the two fare-book lines alone, which points away from Mickey and leaves Ron's burnt sleeves standing. The fitter frees the seat in the week of scene 15, so the tally and "Still where she left it" land together. Use Pauline as a fair second suspect: Mickey taught her too, and "nine years without a Saturday off" sits uneasily next to her going off at ten that Saturday.

2. Act III is rushed. What is wrong: Agar's fare and file, the padlocked yard, five days of Reese, the kitchen, the phone box, the gate, the fight in Ropery Lane, the Friday drive, Ellis's charges and Saturday's ending all fit into two and a half hours. Where: section 8. Why a player feels it: the best scenes in the game, the kitchen and the eleven miles, arrive breathless after two set pieces that are mostly paperwork. It is the GTA V last act all over again. Fix: cut the yard padlock, or fold it into the last week of Act II, where the gate off its hinges already lives. Take an hour from the Seat movement and give Act III three and a half hours.

3. The road of the player who refuses the crime is a table. What is wrong: section 11 is six one-line weeks and a paragraph. The bar names this road by name. Where: section 11. Why a player feels it: the player who winds the firm down on the first Sunday takes the moral path and gets the game's thinnest middle. Fix: write the six weeks as scenes at the density of Act II, and make Edith Stockill and Albert Suggitt returning people with their own turns. Treat the corridor of stacked chairs on Bonfire Night as its retold set piece and build it out properly.

4. Too much of the truth is handed over in speeches to a silent driver. What is wrong: Ada's tea, Shiner's monologue in scene 13, the two widows in scene 14 and Reese's Wednesday all deliver key facts in talk while Tom watches the mirror. Where: Act I's tea, Act II scenes 13 and 14, Act III Wednesday. Why a player feels it: the frame promises a game of hands where talk is where consequences come back, and here talk is where the plot is explained. Scene 14 asks nothing of the player at all. Fix: let the player find that Ron asked for the coat for himself, from the hire list on the Widow's clipboard dated that Friday, or from the wage packet he never collected. Then the widows' exchange can confirm it in four lines. Cut Shiner's speech in half and let the player overhear the brag in the Tangs' queue on an earlier Friday if he is there.

5. The branch for the player who asks Ron first is underwritten, and that player is punished. What is wrong: the player who lives the theme and asks his friend what he wants gets a softer midpoint. The scene the bible calls "the hardest in the game" is four lines long, and it is unclear who split whose knuckle or how Tom's line holds. Where: section 7, "If the player asked first" and "If Ron never left". Why a player feels it: doing the right thing should cost as much as missing it, and here it costs less and is written less. Fix: write the night gate scene in full. Make the Widow's revenge land on a named person in front of Tom, Kevin on the rank, as hard as the empty seat would have landed.

## 3. Would I single this story out in a review

No, not yet. I would give the systems a paragraph of praise: the mileometer, the handbrake, the offers and the lessons are the most original story-play ideas I have read this year. But a reviewer who works out the driver at scene 10 and then sits through a crammed last act writes "clever, but you can see it coming". Fix the first two weak points and the answer could become yes.

## 4. What must not be lost

1. The eleven miles. Driving the route twice on a real dial, finding four miles missing and a bollard in the firm's paint, and then at the end sitting beside the driver while she brakes a yard short of it herself. Nothing in KCD2 or RDR2 turns a clue into something the player drives quite like this.

2. Ron and the passenger seat. The building-by-building list of what has shut, the stories that stop one sentence later each time, a fifty-eight-year-old docker learning to drive at eleven miles an hour, and the bench of dockers at the hearing: "I can't write you one, boss. I can fetch them as'll sit." Which seat he ends in is the verdict, and that is a last image worthy of Arthur Morgan.

3. Reese and the fare book. A mild man with a flask and a bad knee, from the department that was robbed, reading two lines aloud. "A fare at no charge is either a kindness or a hole." The book as alibi and noose, paid off by a VAT inspection, is the frame's best promise, and this version keeps it.
