## What it is about

Britain, October to December 1990, a port town whose docks have died. A stranger inherits his dead uncle's minicab office, two cars and a bookkeeper, and finds the firm has quietly kept an old dockers' sick club alive: forty-one families paid four pounds a week, once from cargo lifted at the dock gate and lately from the uncle's own pocket. The story is about what people owe each other where the official books have written them off, and how much goes unpaid because nobody will ask. Its spine is a friendship between the lead and an old docker who cannot drive, and a mystery: what one of the firm's cars did on the night a bonded warehouse burned a year earlier. The player drives fares, carries envelopes, keeps the books, teaches a man to drive and finally decides whose name goes on a Customs paper. The story never says which answer is right; every answer costs a named person something visible.

## The lead

The lead is 32, son of a Polish settler and the uncle's sister. He arrives by ferry with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter: there is a book, people are owed, keep the doorman if he will stay, every fare goes in the book, and do not let them call you the nephew for ever. That postscript is his want: a place of his own and his name said by people who mean it. His past is never told and nobody asks. He is not voiced; he speaks only the words the player types or picks, and his character lives in a used notebook, its first thirty pages cut out, where short dry entries record what the player did.

His flaw is played. He takes nothing and asks nothing, out of fear that a man who owes cannot pick up his suitcase and leave. All game people offer him things, some gifts and some with hooks, unmarked, and each is one press: take it or pay. Taking means owing; taking nothing means nobody will speak for him or open a door when it matters. From the second week he can also ask anyone, in his own words, what they want, and a question asked is an answer owed. He lays hands on no woman, nobody over sixty and not the priest.

## The people who matter

The doorman, 58, an old docker, calls the lead "boss", cannot drive, and rides in his passenger seat naming every building by what it used to be. He, too, never asks for anything. The bookkeeper, 53, has kept the uncle's books since she was 22, has never been heard to say anything untrue, and keeps the lead at arm's length as "new management". The main opponent is the widow who runs the dock gate, the port's lawful labour contractor, who picks twenty men from sixty each morning, never threatens, prices everything, puts each offer once and keeps every deal; she wants the firm as the gate's cab firm and the club's book shut. Two smaller pressures: the rival cab owner, young, loud on purpose, cutting fares, the one opponent who hurts someone; and the landlord, courteous, apologising before each blow, who needs the yard behind the office before his loan falls due on 31 December.

Around them: the doorman's old gang, the quiet docker and the talkative docker (now on the widow's gate) and the burned docker, an invalid; the radio operator; the night driver; the day driver who wants a plate of his own; a young hustler; the old widow at her window, on the club's list; the uncle's daughter; the priest; the sergeant, who has an hour on the row with nobody in it; the port reporter; the fixer who carries the widow's offers; a laid-off cold-store worker who can drive; and, at the end, the inspector from Customs and Excise.

## The opening and the scene players retell

The first week is the most scripted stretch, about two hours of roughly sixteen. The bookkeeper walks the lead round: every fare goes in the book, and car two was the uncle's own, its seat stuck forward because he was a small man. His first fare is the priest, who says forty-one empty envelopes were laid on the funeral plate.

The scene players retell is the carry. The doorman hands him an envelope for the tallyman at the ferry landing, and the player chooses a route: the lit quay, the dark yard and alley, or the cab with the firm's name on its roof, with a constable who can turn round. If the constable takes it, the sergeant has it by morning, twenty families are short, and the bookkeeper writes the lead into the debt book for £80. Next morning the old widow asks what he was doing in her alley.

Through the week the old widow explains the club her husband founded in 1953 and mentions the doorman coming home one night last November with no sleeves to his coat. The council says the lead has been driving on a dead man's badge; a hearing is set. On Friday the doorman walks the club's round of twenty doors, one of them his alone. On Sunday comes the first driving lesson: the doorman at the wheel with biscuit-tin L-plates, the player holding the handbrake, "I go, you stop", and a bollard hit or missed by a yard. Then the bookkeeper asks which it will be: wind the club down, take it over, or refuse to say.

## The middle

The middle runs from late October to early December, about nine and a half hours, in three movements and seventeen scenes. Each week has the same shape: fares by day, a job at dusk set off by whichever opponent is moving, the night count, and Friday wages, rent and a round that shrinks as members die or sign with the gate.

In the first movement the player wins back a ferry freephone from the rival, rides the widow to the gate (she takes the doorman's seat, offers fifty pence a fare and is the first to ask the lead what he wants), and catches the day driver carrying parcels for her; he may be sacked, used, or asked what he wants. At the licensing hearing only people the lead has let do him a turn write for him; then twenty old dockers fill the public bench, fetched unasked by the doorman. Licence, conditions or refusal: none ends the game. If the fifty pence went unpaid, the widow blocks the cold-store road with her men and herself; the lead may wait, pay, turn round, or send the doorman, who opens the line with six words while she watches. The second lesson goes well until the doorman refuses the warehouse row and then, for the first time, asks for a night off.

The midpoint is Bonfire Night. The radio sends the lead to the gate, where the doorman stands beside the widow in a foreman's coat with the gate's name on it. On the dockers' bonfire burns an effigy wearing the firm's roof sign; he watches it burn. The rival's lads break the office window. The old widow calls the lead by his first name for the first time and says the doorman would not cross the road for money. Next morning his key is on the counter. If the player asked him first what he wants, he says only that he has a reason not his to tell, and stays; the widow takes the day driver and the cold-store run instead, and what follows is harder.

In the second movement the passenger seat is empty or filled by others. At the memorial service the burned docker sits in gloves. A mechanic finds a docker's tally and a burnt glove jammed in car two's seat runner. Setting the old books side by side, the player finds two fare-book lines for the fire night: the uncle taken to the infirmary at ten to eleven, and car two out and back, eleven miles, no sheet. The burned docker says the doorman carried him out and the car came back. Driving the route on car two's dial gives seven miles; the missing four reach an old painted bollard. A staged visit by two "strangers" frightens the rival and reveals that the talkative docker boasted he could name the driver, and that the doorman took the coat to control where he works. The gate's widow tells the old widow, in the lead's cab, that the doorman asked her for it. The player now believes, wrongly, that the doorman drove. Four side stories run alongside.

On the other road, where the club is wound down, the same story happens but each week's job is building a lawful friendly society; Bonfire Night costs more, and a pensioner auditing the books finds the eleven miles.

## The turn

On the anniversary the third lesson reaches the row's mouth. The doorman brakes himself: he has never been down it in a car; he came up on foot with a man on his back, the car gone, and then it came back. He tries the seat: "Still where she left it." One word too many. The only woman who could drive was the bookkeeper. He has been learning so the firm never again depends on her alone. He asks what the lead thinks; the player can call him back, let him stay at the gate, or ask what he wants.

The widow makes her last offer from the passenger seat and reveals the landlord's watchman saw a cab on the row. The sergeant inspects car two; the rival steals it, and refusing his price puts the cold-store worker's arm in plaster. The act ends on a letter from the inspector.

## The last act

Two weeks, about two and a half hours. The landlord, driven to the gate, admits he has told the Crown, offers a solicitor as a gift, and padlocks the yard; the player signs an option, forces the lock, or lets the old gang lift the gate off its hinges. The inspector rides in the doorman's seat and reads the two lines: the store was bonded. By Friday he wants the driver's name in the driver's hand and the eleven miles shown on the road, or nearly four thousand pounds assessed and investigators sent.

In her kitchen the bookkeeper, her licence from 1961 on the table, says she took the men because forty-one people depended on it, and wrote the trip down because every fare goes in the book. The lead may leave both lines, strike the uncle's alibi, burn the book, or ask what she wants: to have it read and sign her own name. Outside the doorman says she is not to sign; he can drive now. On Thursday the lead can ring only those he once let give him something, clears the rival's cars from a lane by asking what he wants (a day's work for his father), and asks the widow before sixty men for her dockers' signatures. Dealt with straight, she gives them and hires the father, losing a contract; dealt with badly, she asks for the firm.

On Friday whoever signs drives the eleven miles with the inspector aboard: the bookkeeper, the doorman on L-plates lying for her, the lead under his uncle's name, or the lead declining to name anyone. At the bollard the last input is the handbrake, and the right thing is to leave it. The sergeant charges whoever signed.

## The endings

The Kingdom, when the business stands and the books hold. Plain turn: a false name is on the paper. With the uncle's, the bookkeeper leaves her keys on the fare book; with the doorman's, he faces court in January for what he did not do and the seat is empty. A new carrier walks the envelope down the quay and the tallyman asks for the lead by name. Better turn: the bookkeeper signs because he asked; the inspector recommends no proceedings; she is bailed, gives the book to the radio operator, and rides home at dawn as a paying fare while the doorman, in front by choice, names buildings and promises to fetch the dockers for her hearing.

The Straight Life, when no business of his stands and one friendship holds. Better turn: he signs only as owner; the firm passes to the cold-store worker, the club becomes a lawful society, and the doorman drives him round on L-plates saying he never asked them. Plain turn: he sells to the widow, the roof reads THE GATE, and the doorman lifts one hand from her van.

Burn Both, when nothing else fits. The Reckoning: his books fail, the bookkeeper hands over her keys, the doorman confesses falsely, and a constable calls the lead's name on the quay; stand or run, cut on the press. The Suitcase: he never asked anyone; he leaves by ferry, the doorman not looking across.
