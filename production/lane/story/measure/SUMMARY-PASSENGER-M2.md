## What it is about

Britain, a port town whose docks have died, October 1990. A stranger of thirty-two arrives on the ferry with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter from an uncle he never met, and takes over the uncle's minicab office: two cars, a doorman who cannot drive, and a bookkeeper who will not use his name and walks home every night in any weather. An old dockers' sick club pays four pounds a week to forty-one families, and the firm keeps it going by hand, outside the official books. A year ago a bonded warehouse on the row burned, an hour of that night is missing, and one of the firm's cars was out. Three pressures close on the firm: the widow who runs the dock gate, a rival cab owner, and the landlord. The story asks what people owe each other in a place the official books have written off, and how much goes unpaid because nobody will ask. Its lead asks nobody for anything and takes nothing.

## The lead

The lead is the son of a Polish post-war settler and the uncle's sister. His past is never told: "I'll not ask where you were before. Nobody on this street will." He is not voiced. He speaks only the words the player types or picks, and who he is lives in his notebook, in short dry entries written from what the player did: "Envelope. Landing. Nobody said thank you. Nobody was meant to."

He wants a place that is his and his own name said by people who mean it. His flaw is played with the hands. People offer him things all game, a tea, a flask, a lift, a fifty-pound note, and if the player does nothing his hand goes to his pocket and he pays. To take, the player must hold a button, slowly. Some offers are gifts and some carry a hook, and nothing marks which. To take is to owe, and only those he has let do him a turn will later stand for him. From the second week a notebook page headed "Asked" lets the player put the question "what do you want?" to anyone, once each. He lays hands on no woman, nobody over sixty, and not the priest.

## The people who matter

The doorman, fifty-eight, an old docker, calls the lead "boss", rides in the passenger seat and names every building by what it was. The bookkeeper, fifty-three, has never been heard to say a thing that was not so, and keeps the rule "Every fare goes in the book." The widow who runs the dock gate picks twenty men from sixty every morning, lawfully; she puts each offer once, never lies when asked straight, and has never spoken to the police about anyone. The rival runs two yellow cars at £1.50 a ride and takes whatever the street leaves undefended. The landlord wants the yard behind the office, his only route to the old basin, before his loan falls due on 31 December. The sergeant worked the fire and has not let it go. The inspector, from Customs and Excise, comes at the end.

Around them: the talker, of the doorman's old gang; a man in a chair at a window across the quarter, in grey cotton gloves; the radio operator, heard in every cab; the day driver, six foot, folded into a car whose seat sticks; the old woman at her window; the uncle's daughter, a night sister; the night driver; and a hawker who sells what he hears.

## The opening and the scene players retell

The first week runs about two hours. On the forecourt the bookkeeper says the second car was the uncle's own and its seat sticks: "He was a small man."

The scene players retell comes at minute nine. The doorman hands the lead an envelope for the tallyman at the landing, "That's all I'll say on a pavement, boss," and the player carries it by one of three routes: the lit quay, the dark yard and lane, or the cab with the firm's name on its roof, past a constable who can turn round. If the constable takes it, the sergeant has it by morning and the bookkeeper writes the lead into the debt book for eighty pounds.

Each night the player counts coin while the bookkeeper writes. The old woman explains the club over tea and mentions the doorman came home one November night "with no sleeves to his coat, and I never asked." On Friday the doorman walks the club round and takes one packet alone to the man in the chair, who says the lead has the uncle's ears. On Sunday comes the first driving lesson: the doorman at the wheel, the player on the handbrake, "I go, you stop," and a bollard hit or missed by a yard. Then the bookkeeper asks: wind the club down, take it over, or refuse to say. Winding it down plays the same story with three differences, chiefly that the lead tries to make the club a lawful society.

## The middle

The middle runs four weeks, about eight of roughly fifteen hours, split by Bonfire Night. Before it, days are fares with the doorman beside him, dusk is a job one opponent sets off, night is the count, Friday is wages, rent and the round. The player wins back the ferry's freephone, decides whether to pay the widow fifty pence a fare, catches the day driver carrying parcels for the gate and sacks him or asks what he wants, and sits a licensing hearing where, unasked, twenty old dockers in funeral suits fill the bench; licensed or refused, play goes on. The bookkeeper makes him write one fare line a night and rules through anything untrue. Offered a lift home, she says, "I've legs."

The second lesson is played for laughs: the doorman drives the dock road at eleven miles an hour with a ship's pilot's commentary, refuses to sound the horn at a gull, and waves at a night-watchman with his whole arm while the car follows the arm. Then he changes down by ear, untaught, and will not drive down the warehouse row. The hawker reports he sits in the second car at night in the dark, hands moving. In the chip queue the talker says he could tell who had the cab on the row; the doorman asks for nights off to "go down the gate". The player can take the request or not; nothing keeps him off the gate.

On Bonfire Night, fixed for every player, the doorman stands beside the widow in a foreman's coat with the gate's name on it while a guy wearing the firm's roof sign burns. If the player took his request, he nods across the fire; if not, he does not. The old woman uses the lead's first name, and the bookkeeper accepts one lift, then writes herself into the book for it.

After this the lead drives alone at night. The mechanic finds a docker's brass tally and a burnt glove wedged in the second car's seat runner, and the bookkeeper sets last year's books before him. For the fire's night they hold a line for the uncle's trip to the infirmary, and another for the second car: "Out and back. Eleven miles. No sheet. No charge." The man in the chair names the tally as his: the doorman carried him out. Driving the route, the player finds it is seven miles; the other four reach an old bollard and back. Then, alone at the counter, he may write two initials after last year's line: the doorman's, hers, or none. Most leave it blank.

The other comic piece is a decoy: the talker and the hawker cross on the ferry in borrowed coats and come back as two hard strangers to stare at the rival's lock-up. The talker, forbidden to speak, lasts until the chip queue, where he corrects "two" to "Three"; the hawker sells a radio. On the way back the talker lets slip that the doorman took the coat to keep a name from being said. When the radio operator takes a Saturday, the lead runs the radio, and that evening, a car short with keys on the hook, the bookkeeper says, "I don't drive."

Side stories are optional. In one, the uncle's watch sits in a pawnshop; the doorman pawned it for funeral flowers rather than ask, and the player redeems it or leaves it. In another, the sergeant's elderly mother takes the club's money and tells her daughter it comes from chapel, while the daughter chooses to believe her; the lead keeps the confidence, tells the sergeant, or lets the mother know he knows, which shapes the sergeant's note on the last Friday. A third, light one and thirty-two short ones sit around them, about two hours in all.

## The turn

On Sunday 18 November, a year to the day, the doorman drives the route at the third lesson and stops at the row: "I went down it on my feet and I come up it with a man on my back, and there was no car. It had gone. Then it come back." That night the radio breaks: the man in the chair cannot breathe. The second car is gone from the yard. The player catches it on the dock road and follows it a mile and a half: the bookkeeper is driving, a small head below the top of the seat, the seat untouched. This is fixed for every player. They carry the man in his chair down the stairs in step, and the lead drives him to the infirmary, knees round the wheel, while she counts breaths in the back. The night sister says, "Dad's car. I never knew who'd had this one." In the corridor, one of the gravest scenes, the doorman tells the bookkeeper he has been learning to drive at night "so it'd not be you"; she answers that the next time was tonight and he was at the gate. At two she writes the trip in the book, signs it with her initials, the same hand as last year's line, and calls the lead by his name.

## The last act

Two weeks, opened by a letter from the inspector, whose coming is fixed. The landlord, in the lead's cab, admits his watchman saw a small driver and that he has now told the Crown. The man in the chair dies on the Thursday, and nothing the player does prevents it; the lead carries a corner of his coffin. The rival steals the second car and runs the day driver into a wall; the lead and the doorman break into the lock-up to take it back.

The inspector rides along and a woman's crabs escape onto the dashboard. Then he reads last year's two lines aloud, and any initials the lead added. By Friday at two he wants the driver's name in the driver's own hand and the eleven miles shown on the road; otherwise the firm is ruined, and his report goes to the police either way. The doorman says he will drive it and confess at four. In her kitchen the bookkeeper, asked what she wants, says, "To write my own name to it." Three courses remain: her name, his, or the book on the fire. On Thursday, ringing only people he let give him something, the lead seeks the dying man's statement from the night sister and the talker's from the widow, who may price it at the firm. On Friday men from the dock wall lift a locked yard gate if enough owe him; otherwise he signs the yard away. Whoever is named drives the inspector. If she is, the lead races up the hill to reach the doorman before he lies to the sergeant. In time, the doorman reads her name and puts his cap on; late, he must unsay his confession on the steps, another of the gravest scenes.

## The endings

Three, decided by what the lead wrote in last year's book, whose name went on the paper, and whether the book survived. How much he could prove shapes the detail within each.

With her name on the paper, the sergeant charges the bookkeeper, the doorman and the talker, to answer at Easter, with her note that the driver went back. The bookkeeper will not keep a book she is in. Last scene: the dock road at six, the doorman back in the passenger seat by day, naming buildings, having handed back the coat; the widow picks twenty, the rival's father among them; the rival greets the lead by his first name. The bookkeeper, a paying fare, gets out at her door, pays, and tells him to mind how he goes, by his first name.

With his name, the doorman answers alone at Easter and stays at the gate in the coat. The bookkeeper leaves her keys on the fare book. Last scene: the lead watching the gate from an empty car; the doorman does not come over, and lifts one hand, not high. The notebook: "He drove it for her. Nobody asked her."

If the book burns, investigators question everyone, the doorman confesses and nobody is in time, and the landlord's lock goes on the door. Last scene: the lead with his suitcase at the last ferry as a constable calls his name; stand or run, and the old woman at her window. The notebook's last page is cut out.
