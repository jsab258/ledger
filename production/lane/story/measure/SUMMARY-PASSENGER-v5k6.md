## What it is about

Britain, a port town whose docks have died, October to December 1990. A stranger of thirty-two comes over on the ferry with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter from an uncle he never met, and inherits the uncle's minicab office: its cars, its bookkeeper, its doorman, and the rule the letter ends on, that every fare goes in the book. The office carries more than fares. For thirty years it has fed an old dockers' sick club that pays forty-one families four pounds a week. Three people want what the firm has: the widow who runs the dock gate and hires the port's labour, a young rival with cheaper cabs, and a landlord whose loan falls due at the year's end. A bonded warehouse burned on the waterfront a year ago, and the police sergeant who had the file still has an hour on that row with nobody in it. In the passenger seat rides an old docker who cannot drive. The story asks what people in a written-off place owe one another, and how much goes unpaid because nobody will ask: the lead takes nothing and asks nothing, and neither does his friend.

## The lead

The lead is the son of a Polish post-war settler and the uncle's sister. His life before the town is never told. He speaks only the words the player types or picks; who he is lives in his notebook, a used one with its first thirty pages cut out, where short, dry entries are written from what the player did. He wants a place that is his and his own name said by people who mean it. His flaw is that he lets nobody give him anything and asks nobody for anything, which is fear: a man who owes nothing can pick up his suitcase, and it stays packed under the bed until the player unpacks it. The flaw is played two ways. People offer him things, each one press, take it or pay: a café's first tea, the night driver's flask, but also the rival's fifty-pound note and the gate widow's lift, and nothing marks gift from hook. And from the second week he can ask anyone, in his own words, what they want; an answer asked is an answer owed.

## The people who matter

The doorman is fifty-eight, thirty years a docker, calls the lead "boss" and cannot drive. The bookkeeper is fifty-three, at the firm since she was twenty-two, has never been heard to say a thing that was not so, and keeps the lead at arm's length by his surname. The gate widow, fifty, is the port's registered labour contractor: sixty men stand at the gate at six and she picks twenty. She never threatens, puts each offer once, keeps every deal and tells no lie when asked straight. The doorman's old gang are the quiet docker and the talker, both on her gate now, and their third man, who is on nobody's gate. The firm has a radio operator with no Saturday off in nine years, a night driver of four words a shift, and a day driver who wants a plate of his own. The old widow, seventy-four, is on the club's list and misses nothing. The rival runs two yellow cabs and is quicker than he lets on. The landlord apologises before every blow and wants the yard behind the office for its right of way to an old basin. The reporter wants to know where an hour went. Last comes the inspector, a mild officer of Customs and Excise with a flask and a bad knee. There are also a young hanger-on who sells what he hears, the priest, and a woman laid off from the cold store who can drive.

## The opening and the scene players retell

Act I is the first week, about two hours, and it is the most scripted act. Nobody meets the lead off the ferry. The bookkeeper walks him round: "Every fare goes in the book." Car two, she adds, was the uncle's own and its seat sticks forward, because he was a small man. The first fare is the priest, who says forty-one envelopes went on the funeral plate, every one empty. On the first night the doorman asks him to carry an envelope to a tallyman. This carry, at minute nine, is the scene players retell: three routes with their costs (the lit quay, the dark yard and lane, or the cab with the firm's name on its roof) and a constable who can turn round. If the constable takes the envelope, the sergeant has it by morning, and the bookkeeper writes the lead's name in the uncle's debt book for £80. Over tea the old widow tells him what he carried, and that the doorman came home one night last November with no sleeves to his coat. On the first Thursday the doorman rides with him: "You never ask for owt, do you." On Sunday comes the first driving lesson: the doorman has a provisional licence and L-plates cut from a biscuit tin, and sets the terms, "I go, you stop"; the player has the handbrake, and stops him a yard short of a bollard, or does not. Then the bookkeeper puts the club's real book on the counter: wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say.

## The middle

Act II runs about nine and a half hours of some fourteen, in three movements. Each week has one shape: fares by day, a job at dusk set off by whichever opponent is moving, the count at night, and on Friday wages, rent and the round, which falls from twenty doors to eighteen as people die, sign with the gate or send the packet back. What changes is a returning fare, the dusk job, and what the bookkeeper finds in the count. In the first movement the doorman rides in front naming every building by what it was. The gate widow books a car, takes the front seat so the doorman must sit behind, offers fifty pence on every fare, and asks the lead what he wants; she prices her last offer on his answer. The day driver is carrying parcels for the gate; the lead can sack him, use him, or ask him what he wants. At the licensing hearing only people he let do him a turn write for him; then twenty old dockers in funeral suits, fetched by the doorman, fill the public bench, a gift he is never offered the press for. The second lesson is the one meant to be funny: the doorman, knees at his ears in the stuck seat, drives the whole dock at eleven miles an hour and asks to go round again; then he stops short of the warehouse row ("Surface is bad") and asks, for the first time, for a night off.

If the lead chose to wind the club down, the weekly jobs become founding a lawful friendly society, while the friendship, the gate and the fire play as on the other road.

Beside thirty-two short side stories there are four whole ones. In one, the lead drives the sergeant home in the rain; her mother then books him every Sunday for chapel, and turns out to know exactly what her four pounds is, while telling her daughter it comes from the chapel. He keeps the confidence, tells the sergeant, or lets the mother know he knows, and it decides how the sergeant reads a folder on the last Friday. In another, the reporter leaves her umbrella in his cab, later laughs at his notebook on the quay wall, and in the anniversary week asks him whether to take the news desk her editor offers if she drops the fire. She takes it, keeps the story and loses it, or he tells her what he knows and she holds it until he says.

## The turn

Bonfire Night, the first night the lead drives alone, is the midpoint. A fare on the widow's account takes him to the dockers' bonfire at the gate, and beside her stands the doorman in a foreman's jacket with the gate's name across the shoulders. He does not come over. On the fire sits an effigy in a car coat with the firm's roof sign round its neck, and the doorman watches it burn. The operator, to every cab: "Car one. You on your own?" The old widow calls the lead in by his first name, the first time anyone has, and says the doorman would not cross the road for money, and that the uncle got the bookkeeper through her driving test in 1961 and she has not driven since. In the morning the doorman's key is on the counter. If the player asked him first what he wanted, he says "To be let alone", stays, and the widow takes the day driver instead.

In the second movement others take the empty seat. At Remembrance, where the player chooses only where to look, a man in a wheelchair with grey cotton gloves sits by the dockers' wreath. The mechanic finds a docker's brass tally and a burnt glove jammed in car two's seat runner. The count, done by hand, finds the night of the fire: the uncle to the infirmary in car one, and "Car two. Out and back. Eleven miles. No sheet." The gloved man, the third of the gang, says the doorman carried him down the row "before the car come back". On the mileometer the route is seven miles; the other four are a run to a bollard with the firm's old paint, and back. To answer the broken window the lead stages a job, the other scene meant to be funny: two old dockers return on the last ferry as strangers to stare at the rival's lock-up, and the talker, ordered not to speak, lasts as far as the chip shop queue, then blurts that he bragged he could name the driver, and the doorman took the coat to keep him on the night gate. In the third lesson, a year to the day, the doorman brakes alone at the mouth of the row, says the car went and came back, tries the seat and says, "Still where she left it." The driver was the bookkeeper, and he is learning so that it never falls to her again. He asks whether to come back.

## The last act

Act III, about two and a half hours over two weeks, belongs to the inspector. The rival steals car two and, refused, runs the new driver into a wall. The landlord tells the Crown about a cab on the row and padlocks the yard; the old gang can lift the gate off its hinges. The inspector rides in front, and a woman's box of live crabs will not stay shut. He reads both lines aloud and wants by Friday the driver's name in the driver's hand and the eleven miles shown on the road, or he assesses near four thousand pounds. The gravest scene is the bookkeeper's kitchen: the old book, a licence kept since 1961, a pen and a fire. With her back to him she says she took the men because the uncle was on the floor. He can leave both lines, strike the uncle's alibi, burn the book, or ask her what she wants: "To have it read." On Thursday he gathers signatures, the widow pricing hers at the firm or, if dealt with straight, giving them at a cost to herself. On Friday the named driver drives: the bookkeeper, the doorman on L-plates lying for her, the lead with the alibi struck, or the lead naming nobody. The last input is the handbrake at the bollard. Whoever signs, the sergeant charges.

## The endings

The Kingdom comes when the firm stands and the books hold. In its plain turn a false name is signed: if the uncle's, the bookkeeper leaves her keys on the fare book; if the doorman's, he answers in January for what he did not do. The last image is the hanger-on carrying the envelope down the lit quay and a cab coming in with the passenger seat empty, or the doorman in it staring ahead. In its better turn the bookkeeper signed because she was asked; the inspector recommends no proceedings, the sergeant bails her, and she gives the book up to the operator. The last scene is car one at six in the morning, the doorman in front by choice and the bookkeeper in the back as a fare, paying at her door and using the lead's first name. The Straight Life comes when no business of his stands and one friendship holds. In its better turn he signs as owner, is charged, and the firm passes to the new driver on a loan the priest signed; the doorman, on L-plates, drives him round at eleven miles an hour and tells him he never asked. In its plain turn he sells to the gate widow, and her van passes with the doorman lifting one hand. Burn Both comes when nothing else fits. In the Reckoning the books fail, the doorman falsely confesses and is driven away, and a constable calls the lead's name on the quay; the close cuts on stand or run. In the Suitcase, for the man who never asked, he leaves by the ferry, the doorman not looking across, the old widow watching.
