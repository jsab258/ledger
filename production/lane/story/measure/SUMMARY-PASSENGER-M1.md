## What it is about

In October 1990 a stranger of thirty-two comes off the ferry into a British port town whose docks have closed, with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter from an uncle he never met. The uncle has died and left him a two-car minicab office, a bookkeeper, an old docker on the door, and a book: a sick club that forty-one dockers' families live on. The letter says people are owed and every fare goes in the book. The lead must decide whether to keep the firm and the club going while three people press on them: the widow who runs the dock gate, a young rival cab owner, and a landlord who wants the yard behind the office. A year earlier a bonded warehouse on the row burned, and nobody will say what happened that night. The story asks what people owe one another where the official books have written them off, what one of the firm's cars was doing on that row, and what a man who asks nobody for anything will do once he knows.

## The lead

He is the son of a Polish post-war settler and the uncle's sister; his life before the town is never told. He is not voiced: he speaks only in words the player types or picks, and he lives in his notebook, a used one with its first thirty pages cut out, where short, dry entries follow what the player did: "Envelope. Landing. Nobody said thank you. Nobody was meant to." He wants a place that is his and his own name said by people who mean it. His flaw is that he takes nothing and asks nothing, and the player handles it three ways. People offer him things, a tea, a flask, a fifty-pound note, a lift, and each is one press, take it or pay, with nothing to show which has a hook in it; to take is to owe, and only those he let do him a turn will stand up for him later. From the morning the widow at the gate asks what he wants, his notebook has a page headed "Asked", and the player may put her question to anyone, once each. And on the last Friday he must cross a road and ask sixty men for their hands. He lays hands on no woman, nobody over sixty and not the priest. His suitcase stays packed under the bed until the player unpacks it, and the bookkeeper notices.

## The people who matter

The old docker, fifty-eight, is on the door and the rank after thirty years on the quay. He calls the lead "boss", cannot drive, and rides in front naming each building by what it was; five times he starts a story about his old gang and stops. The bookkeeper, fifty-three, has kept the books since she was twenty-two, has never been heard to say a thing that was not so, calls the lead "New management", and has walked home every night for thirty-one years; nobody has seen her in a car. Each night she reads the drivers' sheets aloud while he counts coin, their one hour alone. The widow at the gate, fifty, the port's lawful labour contractor, picks twenty of sixty men at six each morning; she prices, puts each offer once, keeps every deal, tells no lie when asked straight and never talks to the police. She wants all the gate's work through her hands, the firm as her cab firm, and the old club shut. The rival, twenty-six, runs cheap yellow cars and takes whatever the street leaves undefended. The landlord, sixty-one, apologises before every blow; his loan on the old basin falls due on 31 December, and the yard is its only right of way. Around them are the quiet docker and the talker, of the old gang, now on the gate; the invalid docker, on nobody's; the radio operator; the night driver; the day driver; a hanger-on who sells what he hears; the old neighbour, widow of the club's founder; the uncle's daughter, an infirmary night sister; the priest; a detective sergeant who has not let the fire go; a reporter; and, late, a mild officer of Customs and Excise, the inspector.

## The opening and the scene players retell

The first week is the most scripted. On the walk-round the bookkeeper says every fare goes in the book, and that car two was the uncle's own, its seat stuck where a small man set it; the tall day driver drives it all game with his knees round the wheel. The first fare, the priest, says forty-one empty envelopes went on the plate at the funeral. That night the old docker hands the lead an envelope for the tallyman over the water, and the retold scene follows, nine minutes in: the carry. The player takes the lit quay, the dark yard and lane, or a cab with the firm's name on its roof, past a constable who can turn round. If the constable takes it, the sergeant has it by morning, twenty people over the water go short, and the bookkeeper writes the lead into the uncle's debt book for £80. Next day the neighbour asks what he was doing in her alley. Over tea she explains the club and mentions that the old docker came home one November night with no sleeves to his coat. On Friday the old docker walks twenty doors with a holdall, and takes one packet alone to a door of the uncle's own. The second retold scene is the first lesson that Sunday: the old docker, L-plates cut from a biscuit tin, says "I go, you stop," and the player at the handbrake stops him short of a bollard or does not. Then the bookkeeper puts the club's real book on the counter: wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say.

## The middle

The middle is four weeks, over half the game. Before Bonfire Night a day is fares with the old docker in front, dusk a job set off by an opponent, night the count, Friday wages, rent and the round. The bookkeeper turns the fare book round and makes the lead write one line a night, ruling through anything untrue. The widow takes the front seat, offers fifty pence on every gate fare, and asks what he wants. The day driver is caught carrying for the gate: sack him or ask him. At the licensing hearing only those he took from have written for him, and twenty old dockers in funeral suits, fetched unasked by the old docker, fill the bench; he is licensed with conditions or refused. Refuse the fifty pence and her men block the cold-store road; he waits, pays, turns back, or sends the old docker, who opens it in six words. In the chip-shop queue the talker says he could tell who had the cab on the row, and the old docker asks for time to go to the gate. The second lesson is a comic set piece: eleven miles an hour, a pilot's commentary, a gull he will not hoot at. Then he changes gear by ear, untaught, and will not drive down the row.

On Bonfire Night, the midpoint, the old docker stands beside the widow in a foreman's coat, and an effigy on the dockers' fire wears the firm's roof sign while he watches it burn. If the player granted his request, he nods across at the cab; if not, a friend has been bought. The office window is smashed; the bookkeeper takes a lift once, in the back of car one, never car two, and writes herself into the book.

Then the lead drives nights alone and goes looking. A mechanic finds a docker's tally jamming car two's seat at its most forward notch, with a burnt glove; the bookkeeper sets the 1989 books before him and walks home. On the fire night car two went "Out and back. Eleven miles. No sheet." The invalid docker says the old docker carried him out. On the mileometer the route is seven miles; the other four reach a bollard and back. In the second set piece the talker and the quiet docker come off the last ferry in borrowed coats as hard men from away, the talker forbidden to speak, until he corrects a stranger's "two hard cases" with "Three." Then he lets slip that the old docker took the coat to keep a name unsaid. A third: the lead on the radio, the bookkeeper correcting every call; that night, a car short, she says "I don't drive." The player believes the old docker drove.

Two side stories. The uncle's watch is in a pawnshop; the old docker pawned it for the funeral flowers rather than ask the bookkeeper, and the lead redeems it or leaves it. A fare for the sergeant leads to her mother, who takes the club's money and tells her daughter it is from the chapel, the daughter choosing to believe her; the lead keeps the secret, tells, or lets the mother know he knows, which shapes the sergeant's note on the last Friday. There are two more full side stories and thirty-two short ones. Winding the club down plays the same events, the club becoming a lawful society.

## The turn

On 18 November, a year on, the lead gives the old docker car two. At the mouth of the row he brakes: he went down it on foot, came up with a man on his back, and the car had gone, then came back. That night the radio says the invalid docker cannot breathe. Car two is gone. The lead follows its tail lights for a mile and a half, a small head below the seat top: the bookkeeper. They carry the invalid docker down in a chair, in step, and the lead drives car two with his knees round the wheel while she counts breaths in the back. At the infirmary the uncle's daughter says she never knew who had the other car. In the corridor the old docker sits at the far end of the bookkeeper's bench; nobody speaks. At two she writes the night and initials it, the hand of 1989, and calls the lead by his first name. A year before, the uncle planned the bond theft to save the club, his heart failed, and she drove in his place; a heater went over, nothing was taken, she fled to the bollard and turned back. Then the inspector's letter comes. The old docker's going, her drive and the inspector are fixed for every player.

## The last act

The old docker returns for nights. The landlord says his watchman saw the cab and its small driver and he has told the Crown, then offers a solicitor. The widow's last offer is the firm. The rival steals car two; refused, a yellow car puts a new driver into a wall, and the two men break it out. The inspector rides along in the last comic scene, a crab loose on the parcel shelf whom he calls "sir"; then he wants the driver's name in the driver's hand and the eleven miles driven, by Friday at two, his report going to the police either way. The old docker will tell the sergeant at four that it was him, "for it never to have been her." In her kitchen, the gravest scene, the bookkeeper, asked, wants to write her own name. Two names, and a fire. On Thursday the uncle's daughter gives the invalid docker's statement if shown the 1989 book; the widow, by how he has dealt with her, prices the firm, or lets her men sign and hires the rival's father, losing a contract. On Friday the landlord padlocks the yard with car two inside: sign his option, or ask the men on the dock wall, and those he let help him lift the gate off; if nobody, he signs. If her name is given, she drives the eleven miles, the lead at the handbrake; pulled, she says "Thank you, I can manage." The inspector writes that the driver went back. If the old docker's, he drives and the inspector notes doubt. Then the old docker walks uphill, and the lead races him to the police station.

## The endings

Three, decided by which seat the old docker ends in. In the first, her name stands and the lead is in time: the bookkeeper, the old docker and his two gang mates are charged for the bond and bailed; she gives the book to the radio operator, the widow takes back her coat and makes the quiet docker foreman, and the landlord loses his basin to the bank. The last scene is the dock road at six, the old docker beside the lead naming buildings, the bookkeeper in the back paying her fare home and counting it twice. In the second, his name stands or he confessed first: he stays in the widow's coat to face trial, perhaps for a false statement too, the bookkeeper leaves her keys on the fare book, and the lead waits alone opposite the gate while the old docker lifts one hand, not high. In the third, the book burned, the books failed or the yard was signed away: investigators question everybody, the old docker is driven off in a police car having confessed, and on Saturday night the lead stands at the last ferry with his suitcase as a constable calls his name; the game cuts on stand or run, the fare book closed and the notebook's last page cut out.
