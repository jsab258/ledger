# LEDGER story bible, phase 2 version 6: a script editor's note

## 1. Scores

Overall: 7 out of 10. The seat, the dial and the coat beat most game mysteries. But the reveal leaks early, the middle leans on one template, and the last act swaps menace for a kind man with a clipboard.

1. Tom: 7. The flaw is played rather than stated, through the take-or-pay offers and the page headed "Asked", and the endings judge it. He is still mostly a shape the player fills, and his line is enforced by the game, not chosen, except at the cold-store road.
2. The cast: 8. Ron, Sheila, Pauline, Terry and Ada each have a voice in one line, and nobody is decoration.
3. The antagonists: 6. The Widow is a fine creation, but she ends as a near ally. Danny is the familiar loud rival with a sad father. Agar is mostly letters. Nobody presses on Tom with real menace after the Fifth.
4. The central mystery: 7. It is fair, it is found with a steering wheel, and it changes what minute two meant. It is also given away in scene 10, five scenes before the bible says it turns.
5. The opening and its retold scene: 7. Act I is dense and specific, and the first fare's empty envelopes is a lovely line. The retold carry is only referred to here ("as written"), so I cannot score it higher on this page.
6. Structure and pace: 6. The three movements are shaped well. But the "asked first" branch is sketched, not built. Act III crowds ten beats of talk into two and a half hours, and the hands-on verbs thin out as the story deepens.
7. The middle: 6. The week table is varied on paper, but every week runs fare, dusk job and count. Danny sets off four of the six dusk jobs. The Empty Seat is mostly finding things and being told things.
8. Surprise against cliché: 7. "Ron asked for the coat to keep a name out of a queue" is a genuine turn. The frame around it is familiar: the dying uncle's letter, the inherited business, the decent inspector who bends.
9. The endings: 8. They have last scenes and last images I would remember, Sheila as a paying fare above all. Their conditions are written in system language, and too much hangs on one signature.
10. The side stories: 6. The sergeant's ride is excellent and the watch is good. One Way is a sketch, and the umbrella is a love interest kept so small it hardly breathes.
11. Tone and humour: 7. The radio as the bar is a smart answer to the frame. The crabs, Terry's fifth word and Agar's hat are funny. There are set pieces, but few big laughs.
12. Story and play feeding each other: 8. The mileometer, the handbrake and the seat are the best ideas in the document. Free-typed answers that the Widow prices, and the near absence of fights, are the weak links.

## 2. The five weakest points

1. The reveal leaks at scene 10. What is wrong: in one scene the player sees the seat repair struck off twice "with the initials S.D.". June says Sheila was taught and has not driven in thirty years, and Kevin says the driver "couldn't find his way home". Ada has already listed Sheila among Mickey's pupils. Where: Act II, scene 10, against section 7's closing claim that the player still believes Ron drove until scene 15. Why it matters: the bible's own surprise depends on the misdirection, and any player who has seen a crime drama will put S.D. and a short seat together at once. Scene 15 then confirms a guess instead of turning the story. Fix: strike the repair through with no initials in scene 10, and put the initials in Sheila's kitchen. Move June's "never sat behind a wheel" to after scene 15. Let scene 10 point hard at Ron, for example through Kevin remembering Ron in the yard that night with his coat in his hands.

2. The middle is one loop in six coats, and the hands go quiet. What is wrong: every week is fare, dusk job, count. The dusk jobs are mostly Danny's, and the player's verbs in the Empty Seat are driving, asking and reading books. There is barely a fight in Act II. Where: section 7, the week table and scenes 8 to 12. Why it matters: BAR names the repeated loop and the slow middle as complaints, and the frame says he mostly acts with his hands. Fix: give the Widow and Agar two of Danny's dusk jobs, each a different verb (a night move of the club's stock before Agar's agents change a lock, a race to fill the cold-store shift). Make the lock-up and the car-two fetch real fights with Danny's lads, whom Tom's line allows.

3. The antagonists soften and the last act loses its menace. What is wrong: by Act III the Widow is setting work for Frank Cammack and paying the Bishop's Fridays. Agar apologises and gives a solicitor. Danny's last act is cars parked across a lane. Where: section 5, scenes 16 and 17, and Act III's Thursday. Why it matters: BAR singles out antagonists thinner than the leads and a last act that hurries or deflates. Fix: let one opponent stay hard to the end. The Widow should take something the player sees go, on screen: if Tom dealt straight, she takes Tapper and Shiner off the paper unless Tom gives her the club's book. Danny's lane blockade should be his lads in the road at night, a confrontation the player has to settle.

4. The branches and "found early" claims are asserted, not designed. What is wrong: section 16 says every turn can be found early, and the "asked first" branch keeps Ron in the car. But scenes 13, 14 and 15 rely on Ron being foreman (lending Tapper and Shiner, opening Ada's door, the keys held out at the gate). The Bishop's scene needs the tally, which needs the seat, which the text says becomes possible "at last" in scene 10. Where: "If Ron never left", scene 11, section 16. Why it matters: without rails, each early find needs a designed, smaller answer. Fix: write a one-line yield for each early find (the Bishop without the tally says only "Mickey's own"; Shaun will not touch the seat until the wing is in). Write a seat-full scene 15: Ron brakes at the row, same line.

5. Reese's demand is outside his job. What is wrong: a VAT officer on a control visit needs to know whether takings were suppressed, not who drove. Yet the whole climax hangs on him wanting "who drove, in the driver's own hand" and the miles shown on the road. Where: Act III, Wednesday and Friday. Why it matters: the climax must be forced by the world, not the plot. Fix: give him a stated reason within his department's duty. The night touches a bond his department lost, so he must report it to the Investigation Division unless he can certify it himself as a single non-trading night with nothing taken. A named driver and a driven route are the only things that let him certify it. He says so in one line.

## 3. Logic holes and period faults

1. Pauline has had no Saturday off in nine years, so she was on the radio that Saturday night and must know car two went out. Fix: she knows and has kept quiet; write her beat.
2. Terry was in the office when Mickey fell, so "two people left who had ever held a licence" is wrong, and Sheila could have waited for him to come back. Fix: Terry was out on a fare to the far bank, and Pauline rang the ambulance.
3. Sheila's licence "in a drawer since 1961" would have run out, since licences before 1976 lasted only a few years. Fix: she renews it by post the week of the inspection, the entitlement intact.
4. Ron carried a burning man with his sleeves gone and has no burns. Fix: scarred forearms, sleeves always down, seen once.
5. If Sheila knew the tally was in the runner she would have removed it; if she did not, why strike the repair? Fix: she never looked; she struck it because she could not bear the seat moved from where she left it, and she says so in the kitchen.
6. The letter says two cars; car three appears on Bonfire Night. Fix: a laid-up third car in the yard, one press to put it back on the road.
7. The Widow "holds nothing over anybody", yet she has refused Frank Cammack work for years for talking to Customs. Fix: the line will not stand beside him and she honours the line, not a grudge.
8. Frank's ruined hand would explain his never being picked, and it blurs the Bishop's gloved hands. Fix: Frank's hands are sound.
9. Agar telling Tom he phoned the Crown exposes his own insurance claim (his watchman off his rounds, his heater). Fix: he says it knowing the bank owns him on the thirty-first anyway,.
10. Whose warehouse was it? The importer's in the street's version, Agar's heater and insurance in his. Fix: say once that Agar is the freeholder and the importer the tenant who went south.
11. Ellis's mother, Ivy, is on the club's list, paid from the gang's thefts, and Ellis charges the gang. A sergeant with that interest would be taken off the file. Fix: the side story's end decides whether she declares it.
12. In the Kingdom's better turn, "Mind how you go, Tom" follows a sentence about the Widow and reads as hers. Fix: name Sheila as the speaker.
13. Ada's memory of Mickey teaching June to drive puts June at about seventeen, which brushes the no-children rule. Fix: "June, when she was twenty".
14. Free-typed answers that the Widow prices are a system the bible does not budget. Fix: four short answers and silence.

## 4. Would I single this story out in a review?

No, not yet. I would praise it in a paragraph: the friendship told through a passenger seat, a mystery solved with a mileometer, and a climax that is a fifty-three-year-old woman braking a yard short of a bollard. But a reviewer singles a story out when it surprises and keeps its grip, and this one shows its hand at scene 10, sags through a looped middle, and ends with every opponent turned decent. Fix the leak, harden one antagonist, and break the weekly template, and it is close to a yes.

## 5. What must not be lost

1. The eleven miles. Driving the route, counting it on the dial, and the handbrake as the last input, where the right thing to do is nothing.
2. Ron's coat. A man who never asks anything asks the Widow for a foreman's coat to keep the name of the woman who drove away from him out of a queue.
3. Sheila's last ride. Sheila in the back of car one as a paying fare, going home from a counter that is not hers, with "Dunn, S. Office to home. £2. Paid." in Pauline's round hand.
