# What every version keeps, and what every version must fix

20 November 2026, phase 2 of the story loop. Written before the three competing versions, from the eight rounds of phase 1 (twenty-four fresh readers), canon.md, RULINGS.md, BAR.md and the design. Canon outranks this file. It binds CONTEST-A, CONTEST-B, CONTEST-C and every bible that follows.

## 1. The frame (the owner's rules; breaking one is never an improvement)

- Britain, the port town of Meridian, the Hook and its docks, October to December 1990. Third person. Tom Nowak, 32, arrives by ferry with one suitcase, £23.50 and a letter; his uncle Mickey Suddaby has died of his heart at his desk and left him a minicab office on Quay Street, a book of debts and what is left of an old outfit.
- No alcohol and no gambling, shown or spoken of. No children anywhere, in any line. Tobacco is allowed. Full period swearing, no slurs. Religion is part of life and never mocked. Police are corruptible as individuals, never as a thesis. Drugs are an off-screen trade other people run.
- Play first. The player acts with his hands: fares, carries, collections, break-ins, tails, fights, driving, working the street. Talk is where consequences come back. Few short scenes, no long cutscenes.
- No story on rails: scenes fire on states; only the town's own calendar and dates that arrive in letters happen whether he is ready or not. Every job has three keys. No game over: a failure becomes a debt, a story or a harder game. Tom cannot die. A car hurts and never kills. Guns are rare and late. Killing is rare and permanent. No redemption path is added, and reloading is never stopped.
- Tom's life before the Hook is unsaid: nobody asks and he never tells. He is not voiced. He rises in position, never in numbers.
- One main story; three endings shown as states (the Kingdom, the Straight Life, Burn Both), never picked from a menu, each seen coming from the player's own play.
- Fifteen to twenty hours. Three acts: the Inheritance (week one, about two hours), the Rise and the Squeeze, the Ledger Comes Due (a VAT inspection by Customs and Excise).
- The three rivals come at three different parts of him: Maureen Jensen, the Widow, at his people; Geoffrey Agar at his clean money; Danny Cammack at his cover. A version may make one of them the main opponent and keep the others small. It may not delete one.
- Reference games: Kingdom Come: Deliverance II, Grand Theft Auto V, Red Dead Redemption 2 only, known from the outside.

## 2. What readers praised in every round, kept in every version

1. Ron and Tom. Ron Kirby, 58, a docker for thirty years, cannot drive and rides in the passenger seat. He names every building by what it was, forecasts rain by four, and has a name for every man in his old gang and a story for none of them that he will finish. Three driving lessons on the empty dock road (the first ends on a bollard; in the second he drives the whole dock at eleven miles an hour, talking; the third is not funny). Two of number nine from the Tangs' on the quay wall on Fridays, and Ron pays. In every ending Ron is in a vehicle, and which seat he is in, and whether he looks across, is the verdict on the friendship.
2. Sheila Dunn, as written. "New management." The walk-round. "Every fare goes in the book. That was Mickey's rule, and it's mine now." The locked door. The Sunday question ("So which is it going to be?"). She withholds his name until she trusts him: "Mr Nowak" once, and "Tom" later, for something she finds out for herself. A line in the fare book in her small upright hand is the story's heart: "Nobody reads a fare book."
3. The fare book as alibi and noose, paid off by the VAT inspection. Mr T. A. Reese, a mild man with a flask, a bad knee and a propelling pencil, whose father drove a cab. "A fare at no charge is either a kindness or a hole, Mr Nowak. I'm paid to ask which." "Somebody drove a long way for nothing, Mr Nowak." His ride-along with the box of crabs.
4. The opening week. The letter ("People are owed. Every fare goes in the book. P.S. Don't let them call you Mickey's nephew for ever"). The first fare, Father Walsh: "Forty-one envelopes on the plate, and every one of them empty. They wanted him to know they'd come." The carry on the first night with three honest routes and a constable who can turn round; a failed carry puts "Nowak, T. £80" into the debt book in Sheila's hand. Ada at her window, her one invitation to tea, and next morning "What were you doing in my alley?" The five debts. Friday's rent and wages.
5. The offers. Tom lets nobody give him anything. All game people offer him things for nothing, one press each: take it, or pay for it. Some are gifts and some are hooks and nothing marks which. It pays off twice: at the licence hearing on Tuesday 30 October, where only people he has let do him a turn stand up for him, and on the last Friday at the phone box on the corner, with five coins, where only they will answer.
6. Bonfire Night, Monday 5 November 1990, as the night the story turns: the quay full after dark, the fireworks off the old coal jetty, something burning that should not be, and "You'd better come in, Tom."
7. Pauline Kitching on the radio as the game's chorus, standing in for the bar the game does not have. Terry Hodgson's four words a shift and the fifth: "Tell Pauline it don't count." Tom's Ledger as the straight man ("The ham had a seat").
8. The endings as states, each closed by two lines: one Sheila writes in the fare book and one Tom writes in his Ledger. "Nowak!" from the constable. "Ron takes his test in March. He says rain by four." "Ferry landing. One. No charge."
9. Side stories that are whole stories, above all Ivy Leadley and her daughter, each protecting the other from a thing both know; Ron and Mickey's pawned watch.
10. The calendar as a spine: the hearing, the Fifth, Remembrance Sunday, the anniversary of the fire (Sunday 18 November), the inspector's letter.

Praised often and kept where the shape allows, never forced in: Agar in the rain without a hat; two widows in the back of the minicab with the meter running; Danny Cammack taking credit for a fire he did not light; Frank Cammack's "Go on, Tommy"; June filling envelopes under the ward lamp; the name ladder (the new owner, Nowak, Tom, Tommy).

## 3. The faults every version must fix

Named in two or more rounds, most of them in all eight.

1. Too full for its hours. Phase 1 carried some thirty-five named people, four books, two full roads, three rivals at equal weight, six layers of truth and twenty-one middle scenes in a hands-first game. Every version here: at most about twenty named people who matter; one question at the centre; the hours of each act stated and kept.
2. A guessable midpoint and guessable hidden hands. Readers saw each of these coming: the sweet old widow who secretly runs it all; the kindly dead uncle who did it; the developer who paid for an arson; the framed loud rival who is innocent; the eager lad who lit the fire. No version may rest its surprise on a hidden hand of those kinds. A turn must be fair (set up in play, in plain sight) and still land.
3. The truth is told, not found. Confession after confession, then the climax repeating what was already known. Each piece of the truth must come by a deed: something driven, counted, followed, opened or read by the player himself, with at most one person telling one thing.
4. The last act is a crowded checklist and its climax is paperwork. The last act holds few beats; the climax is done with the hands; the opponent in it is the one the story has been about, never whichever rival is left over.
5. The middle loops, and its rungs have no people. Each stretch of the middle is a named person's story with a turn; the Friday round is a set piece a few times, never a chore seven times.
6. A small world tied in one knot. Coincidence must not do the plotting: not everybody is related to, lodging with or on the list of everybody else.
7. The rivals are stock or lose their teeth after the midpoint. The main opponent stays dangerous to the end and is right about something.
8. Tom reacts. His change is stated in the last two lines instead of forced by a choice only he could make. His flaw (he takes nothing, so he owes nobody and nobody knows him) must be put under a load it cannot carry.
9. The road of the player who winds the firm down is thinner than the keeper's. It gets its own stakes for the hands, or the two roads share one line of scenes that reads differently.
10. Alison Sedman is a function. She is a person with a want and a turn of her own, or she is small.
11. The sums and the period must hold: £4 a week to forty-one people is £164 a week; a fare book records fares; private hire is licensed by the council; pound coins, not notes; nothing the inspector could not lawfully do.

## 4. How the three versions must differ

Each takes a different spine for the middle act, a different midpoint and a different central turn, and each is leaner than story bible v8. They are written one at a time, and before each the choices of the earlier ones are listed in CONTEST-NOTES.md so that the next does not repeat them.
