# 1990 interface style sheet: draft (18 November 2026)

Summary: a first draft of the rules every written word and screen in LEDGER follows so it reads as Britain in 1990. It turns proposal 1 of production/research/lane/PERIOD-FEEL-LANGUAGE-INTERFACE-2026-11-18.md into rules, and sits beside the interface look Jafar already picked on 1 October (the evening paper, production/design/ui/STYLE-GUIDE.md), which it does not change. Draft only: nothing here is ruled.

Written by a research helper, about 30 minutes, 18 November 2026. Tags: (S) source read in full that day, with link; (SN) search snippet only, unverified; (P) the project's own earlier research or rulings; (C) a choice, not a source; (E) estimate. Every web source below was read on 18 November 2026.

## Questions asked first

1. Which coins and notes were in pockets in 1990, and when did the 5p change?
2. What did stamps cost in 1990, and on what date did the price change (the earlier note's open recheck)?
3. When did VAT go from 15% to 17.5%?
4. How were dates and times written and spoken in Britain in 1990?
5. Which faces fit typed letters, signs, rail and road lettering, and which free revivals are on the allowlist?
6. How were evening-paper contents bills written?
7. Which words are anachronisms in 1990 (text, mobile, email), and is "cheers" safe?

## 1. Type faces (only the SIL Open Font Licence 1.1 is allowed: allowlist item 7)

1.1 Use only faces already checked at source as OFL 1.1 in production/design/ui/FONTS.md (checked 1 October 2026) (P). No new face enters without the same three checks (metadata, the OFL.txt, the licence string in the file). Cutive Mono, Lekton, Patrick Hand, Caveat and Anton, named below, are OFL 1.1 in PERIOD-PRINT-AND-FONTS.md but have no row in FONTS.md yet: each needs its row and the three checks before use.
1.2 Typed letters, forms, the fare book's typed entries: Courier Prime (OFL 1.1) (P). A 1990 office letter is a crisp daisy-wheel impression, not a worn manual (P, PERIOD-PRINT-AND-FONTS, 1 Oct). Cutive Mono or Lekton for a second machine in another office (P).
1.3 Handwriting: Kalam for ballpoint words; Patrick Hand for neat print figures in the account book; Caveat for Sheila's quick notes (all OFL 1.1, P). Handwriting is for a few words, never a paragraph (C).
1.4 Interface reading text, choices, subtitles: Libre Franklin as the style guide rules (P). Bands and contents bills: League Gothic or Anton, capitals (P).
1.5 Street name plates: Marcellus SC (Jafar's ruling of 30 September) (P).
1.6 Road-sign look: Work Sans is the nearest allowed face to Transport (P). Rail look: none on the list; draw rail signs in Work Sans or leave them out (C).
1.7 Off the list, with the licence each would need:
   - Transport Heavy as a free font (smugpie/uk-ire-road-sign-fonts): its README says the working drawings are Crown Copyright under the Open Government Licence and the fonts are derived from them; no separate font licence is stated (S, github.com/smugpie/uk-ire-road-sign-fonts). OGL is not OFL: it would need Jafar to add the Open Government Licence to the allowlist, and a check that the font files themselves carry it.
   - Transport files from roads.org.uk: private non-commercial use only (P, snippet). Not usable at any ruling short of buying.
   - Rail Alphabet: British Rail used it from 1965 on nearly all lettering, still near-universal on station signs in the late 1980s; the only digital versions found are New Rail Alphabet (2009, A2-Type) and Rail Alphabet 2 (2020, Network Rail), both commercial (S, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_Alphabet). Would need a bought commercial licence, which is money: Jafar's call.
   - Bedstead (the Teletext face): public domain, not OFL (P). Would need public domain added to the allowlist.
   - Special Elite (worn typewriter): Apache 2.0 (P). Would need Apache 2.0 added.
1.8 Never the British Rail double arrow, the BT mark, the A to Z cover or any real masthead (canon, Brands and law) (P).

## 2. Colours

2.1 The interface keeps the style guide's newsprint, ink and one spot red (#E8E3D6, #1E1E1D, #B4191F); regional papers printed no full colour in editorial pages until the mid 1990s (P, STYLE-GUIDE.md).
2.2 Paper props in the world may add, each from its own trade (C, judgement from P):
   - account book: pale blue feint lines, red money rules (P, judgement; feint ruling confirmed on a current Collins double cash book, colour not stated: S, staples.co.uk Collins Ideal double cash book);
   - carbon copies: blue-violet blur (P, judgement, unsourced);
   - rubber stamps: violet or red ink (P, judgement, unsourced);
   - Teletext, if ever: only its eight colours, black, red, green, yellow, blue, magenta, cyan, white (P, Wikipedia via the earlier note).
2.3 No gradients, glows or soft drop shadows on anything meant to be printed (C).

## 3. Money

3.1 Pounds and pence only, never dollars. Under a pound: "45p", "99p". A pound and over: "£1.20", "£12", "£12.50". Never "£0.45", never "1.20p" (C, standard usage).
3.2 Coins in 1990: 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1 coin (P, predecimal.com). No £2 coin, no halfpenny, no £1 note in England after 11 March 1988 (P, Bank of England).
3.3 The 5p changed in 1990: the small 5p came on 27 June 1990 (S, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_pence_(British_coin)); the old large 5p ceased to be legal tender after 31 December 1990 (S, Hansard written answer, 19 November 1990, api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1990/nov/19/coinage). So from late June to the end of 1990 both sizes are in change. The earlier note's "June 1990" stands; its date is now exact.
3.4 Notes: two sizes of fiver from 7 June 1990 (new Series E) until 29 November 1991; £10, £20 and £50 the old Series D all 1990 (P, Bank of England). Draw generic notes in the right colours and sizes, never a copy (P, decision 5 there).
3.5 VAT 15% on any 1990 receipt. It rose to 17.5% from 1 April 1991 (S, Hansard written answer, 18 April 1991, api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1991/apr/18/value-added-tax). This closes the earlier note's open date.
3.6 Stamps: 20p first class, 15p second from 2 October 1989 (P, edinphoto). The 1990 rise took effect on 17 September 1990: the London Gazette prints the Post Office Inland Letter Post Amendment (No. 1) Scheme 1990, dated 3 September, effective 17 September, setting ceilings of 23p first and 18p second for a letter up to 60 g (S, thegazette.co.uk issue 52266 page 14366). The prices actually charged, 22p and 17p, come from edinphoto, which dates them 17 November 1990 (S, edinphoto.org.uk). Registration fees also changed on 17 September 1990 (S, gbps.org.uk inland registration). Rule: 22p and 17p from 17 September 1990; the November date on edinphoto is taken as its error (C, primary source over secondary). The 22p and 17p figures themselves still rest on one secondary source.
3.7 Money in talk: "a fiver", "a tenner", "two quid", "fifty pence" or "fifty p", "a pound coin" (C, common usage; no source read).

## 4. Dates and times

4.1 Dates day, month, year. In print: "14 August 1990". In handwriting and the account book: "14/8/90" or "Tue 14 Aug". Spoken: "the fourteenth of August" (S, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_and_time_notation_in_the_United_Kingdom). Ordinals ("14th August") only in handwriting and talk, not in typed letters (C, the same page says formal style discourages them).
4.2 Talk, shop signs, handwriting, letters: the 12-hour clock. Spoken "half nine", "quarter to six", "eight o'clock tonight"; 21:30 is said "half past nine", never "twenty-one thirty" (S, same page; S, notoneoffbritishisms.com/2018/03/14/24-hour-clock/).
4.3 Written 12-hour times take a full stop: "6.30pm", "9.15am" (S, same Wikipedia page: the full stop common, recommended by Oxford, the Guardian and the Times; "1.45pm" without a space mainly in news writing). The colon is for digital clocks only (C).
4.4 The 24-hour clock on timetables, police records, legal documents and the fare book's booking times: "23:10" (S, same page: transport timetables use it exclusively, as do most legal documents). Railways and London Transport adopted it in 1964 (S, notoneoffbritishisms, second-hand history).
4.5 Nobody says "fourteen hundred hours" (S, notoneoffbritishisms: an American military and film habit).

## 5. Wording and spelling

5.1 British spelling throughout: colour, centre, licence (noun), practise (verb), cheque, tyre, kerb, programme, organise (C, standard).
5.2 Dialect by word choice, grammar and rhythm only, never phonetic spelling (P, eye-dialect sources in the earlier note). Regional word lists wait for their own source pass (P).
5.3 Tom named, never "I", in the Ledger's narration and other system text about him: "Tom has no change" (C; the project fact "third-person, never first-person" is about the camera, not the wording, so this is a choice to match it). Key hints may still speak to the player ("Tab: suggested lines").

| Use | Avoid (and why) |
|---|---|
| phone box, phonecard, the phone, ring, give him a ring | text, texting, "text me" (the first text message was sent on 3 December 1992: S, vodafone.co.uk newscentre, 30th anniversary feature) |
| car phone, a "brick" (rare, rich men only) | mobile in an ordinary pocket (canon: no mobiles in ordinary pockets); "cell phone" (American). Commercial cellular calls began 1 January 1985 (S, theregister.com, 30 years of mobile); first-generation phones were nicknamed bricks and aimed at business users (S, cntr.salford.ac.uk/comms/mobile.php) |
| pager, bleeper | email, online, website, download, Google, app (C: no internet, canon) |
| answering machine, message on the machine | voicemail (C, judgement) |
| cheers (thanks, or goodbye), ta | cheers as a toast (content rule: no drink spoken of). Cheers as thanks dates from the 1970s (S, open.edu, Philip Seargeant, 23 September 2014) |
| quid, fiver, tenner, p | bucks, dollars, cents (the game's own design notes still write "$": see the note at the end) |
| telly, the box, video, video recorder | streaming, DVD, flat screen (C) |
| the paper, the evening paper | "the news feed", "post" for a social message (C) |
| a cassette, a tape, a personal stereo | CD only for a rich character (C, judgement); MP3, iPod (C) |
| poll tax, community charge (England from 1 April 1990: unverified here) | anything later in the 1990s (C) |
| fortnight, a bit of bother, sorted (late 1980s: unverified) | awesome, "my bad", "reach out", "going forward", "no worries" (C: later or American) |

5.4 Headlines and bills pile nouns and use short words: probe, row, quiz, slam, shame, bid, fury (P, Language Log, 5 March 2009). Never "tot" or any word for a child (content rule).

## 6. The Ledger as an account book

6.1 The page is a bound account book: date column at the left, a narrative column, then £ and p columns (P, PERIOD-PRINT-AND-FONTS; a current Collins double cash book is feint ruled with double cash columns: S, staples.co.uk). Two cash columns, money in and money out (C).
6.2 Pocket-notebook rules: entries are never torn out; a mistake is struck through once and stays readable; each day ends with a ruled line (P, police notebook practice). This matches canon's moat, nothing is ever wiped (P).
6.3 Money in the columns as figures without the sign: "12 | 50" under the £ and p heads (C, account-book practice; judgement).
6.4 Dated entries "Tue 14 Aug"; times in the margin 12-hour, except copied booking times, which keep the fare book's 24-hour (C, follows 4.2 and 4.4).
6.5 Typed lines in Courier Prime, hand notes in Patrick Hand or Kalam; never more than one hand per entry (C).

## 7. Letters, forms and receipts

7.1 Typed business letters: address top right or as a printed heading, date under it written "14th August 1990" or "14 August 1990", "Dear Mr Nowak" with no full stop after Mr (C, British practice, unsourced today).
7.2 "Dear Sir" or "Dear Sir or Madam" closes "Yours faithfully"; a named greeting closes "Yours sincerely" (S, grammar-monster.com, faithfully or sincerely; a current guide, taken as long-standing practice).
7.3 Typed references at the top: "Our ref: GA/JS/114" (C, judgement).
7.4 Shop receipts: shop name, date as 14/08/90, items, total, "VAT No." and VAT at 15% where shown (C; rate S at 3.5).
7.5 Police forms: no caution and no section 9 declaration text appears until a 1990 original is read (P, earlier note). Interview records are handwritten in 1990 (P).
7.6 Forms printed by a council or firm carry its minted name only; the town's paper, phone operator and postal cypher stay unnamed until the brand bible mints them (canon) (P).

## 8. Loading-screen posters (evening-paper contents bills)

8.1 Four or five words at most, a cryptic hook that is still honest (S, InPublishing, Roger Winfield, "Bill writing: a dying art?", 1 November 2004).
8.2 Capitals in League Gothic or Anton, black on white or newsprint, the paper's name in a strip at the top once minted (C, from 1.4 and P).
8.3 Handwritten-in-felt-pen bills are allowed as a variant: they were once written by hand with felt-tipped pens, later pre-printed on A2 (S, same article; when the switch happened locally is not known).
8.4 Examples of the form (C, invented, checked against the content rule): "QUAY STREET FIRE PROBE", "DOCKS JOBS ROW", "MERIDIAN COUNCIL FURY", "CAB OFFICE MYSTERY". Nothing about drink, betting, children or real names.
8.5 One bill per load, picked from what the town actually knows (C, keeps to the style guide's "nothing he could not know").

## 9. What never appears

9.1 Real brands, logos, mastheads, the BR arrow, the BT mark, the A to Z cover, real banknote designs (canon; P).
9.2 Drink, pubs open for drink, betting shops, bookmakers, lottery (the National Lottery began 1994 anyway), drink adverts, toasts (content rule).
9.3 Children, schools in use, toys as props for a child (content rule).
9.4 Slurs of any kind (content rule).
9.5 The £2 coin, euro sign, 17.5% VAT before April 1991, text messages, email, the internet, mobiles in ordinary pockets (3.2, 3.5, section 5, canon).
9.6 First person in system text (5.3, a choice).
9.7 A progress bar that guesses (P, style guide's progress rule); a spinning save icon (C, judgement: the style guide allows "a short moving piece" when work cannot be counted).
