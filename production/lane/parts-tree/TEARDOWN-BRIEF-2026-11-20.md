## His question, answered

"Everytime we find new things. Is this now the best way to get a complete view of the game?"

Not complete on its own. It is the best next step for what happens on screen, which text cannot reach. It will still miss: sound cues that are heard but not seen (see "Sound" below), systems that sit off the story path, and anything the chosen hours do not show. A cheaper addition, if the week is tight, is short clips aimed only at the top gaps in the reference mechanics list.

The full view comes from three sources, each catching what the others miss:
1. Text: DESIGN, canon, his rulings and the reference mechanics list (REFERENCE-MECHANICS-2026-11-20.md, 481 mechanics from the five games' wikis and guides, 19 Nov). Its limits: some big wiki and guide sites were not reached, and the review named a few mechanics still missing; both are listed in that file.
2. Screen: this teardown, an hour of each game with everything on screen listed (from 23 Nov).
3. Play: the AI player types and the panel playing LEDGER's own build (4t and 4l, from the one-shot on).

Wikis list what a game has. They do not list what the player sees from second to second: the passer-by who turns when Tom shoves someone, the camera easing in before a fight, the prompt that stops showing after the third use, the cut from a mission back to free play. Those are what make KCD2 feel immersive and GTA feel busy, and they are where the new things keep coming from. After all three sources, new things will still turn up, but from playing our own game rather than from gaps in the list. That is the normal state of a production, and the tree takes them the same way.

## How it is watched

The builder steps through the video as the driving method does: paused, one screenshot about every half second of video (step 30 frames with the "." key) through play, and frame by frame (step 1 frame) around each event to time it. A 15-minute piece is about 1,800 screenshots at that rate. If that proves too slow or too costly after the first piece, the builder may step once a second through quiet driving or walking and say so in the notes.

Sound: the builder reads screenshots, which carry no sound. So sound cues are logged only where they show on screen: subtitles, on-screen sound indicators, a character visibly reacting to a noise, music shown in a radio display. Cues that are only heard (a siren far off, footsteps behind, a sting when the player is noticed) are not covered by this method and the notes say so. Those cues matter to LEDGER (DESIGN 7's warning before a blow, DESIGN 2's sound rule), so the gap goes into the tree as a research item: how to log sound cues from the outside, to be researched before anyone relies on it.

## What to list

Every event on screen, one row each. An event is anything the player does or anything the game does in answer:

- Action: every input that has a visible effect (walk, sprint, turn, punch, block, enter a car, open a door, pick up, talk, choose a line, open the map or phone).
- Reaction: what the world does back (a passer-by flinches, shouts, runs, calls the police; a shopkeeper raises hands; a driver gets out; an enemy staggers; a guard turns).
- Seen sound: a sound cue visible on screen, as above.
- Camera cue: any change of camera (zoom in for a fight, pull back in a car, shake on a hit, slow motion, a cut to show a witness, the camera turning to a talker).
- Prompt: any text, icon or button hint, how long it stays and whether it returns.
- Transition: any change of mode (free play to mission, mission to cut scene and back, fade, loading, a fail screen, a save).
- Interface: anything else on screen that changes (meters, wanted stars or eye icons, money, notices, map flashes).
- Empty time: any stretch over 20 seconds where the hands do nothing and nothing new happens (timed properly by the activity sheet; here just noted).

Each row: game; piece (early, middle, late, town); time in the video to the second; kind (one of the eight above); what happened in plain words; how long it took if timed by frame steps; and, where it matches, the reference mechanic id (M1.26 and so on from REFERENCE-MECHANICS-2026-11-20.md) and the parts tree part id. A row that matches no mechanic and no part is marked NEW.

Under each piece: events per minute (all kinds) and the longest gap between player actions.

Expected size: about 4 to 8 rows a minute in busy play, so roughly 250 to 450 rows a game. That is the point: the small things are the ones the text missed.

Saved as one CSV per game with the columns above, plus a short notes file, in the research folder on his PC that retention names for it. No pictures.

## How the findings go into the tree

1. Match: each row is matched to an M id and a part id where it fits (as it is listed, and finished in the second pass).
2. NEW rows: grouped (the same event in several games is one candidate). Each candidate becomes a part in PARTS-TREE.json with its standard written from what was seen (for example "a passer-by who sees a shove turns and stares within about half a second"), or is marked "not for LEDGER" with the reason, by the same rule the lane used for the reference mechanics (decided within canon, the pillars and his rulings; nothing goes to him unless it is canon or scope).
3. Standards: a row that times something already in the tree (a warning before a blow, a prompt fading, a camera zoom) is added to that part's standard as a lead from one viewing, not a measured value. It becomes a measured value only through the feel measurement's own rule (five samples, two measurers; its method file, FEEL-MEASUREMENT-METHOD, is to be written next by the lane).
4. Check: tools/lane/parts-tree/check.py runs after the merge, and one line goes to the overview: rows logged per game, how many matched, how many NEW, how many became parts.

Who does steps 2 to 4: the cloud lane while it runs (it ends 21 Dec), otherwise the main session.
