# People and police: reality-check research
Researched 24 Nov 2026 (web only, WebSearch then WebFetch). Setting: northern English port town, Britain 1988-92.
Marks: [READ] fetched and read today; [SNIPPET] search result only; [INF] inference, not a source.
Fetch tool summarises pages with a small model, so figures are second-hand to that extent. Treat exact quotes as unchecked.

## 1. Walking speed on British pavements
- TRL PPR700 "Review of pedestrian walking speeds", Crabtree/Lodge/Emmerson, 2014. https://www.trl.co.uk/uploads/trl/documents/PPR700 - Review of pedestrian walking speeds.pdf [READ]
  - UK crossing design value 1.2 m/s (DfT 2009; pelican clearance 1.0-1.1). Report says origin of 1.2 is unknown. (Not a 1990 figure; 1990-era DoT crossing design used a similar 1.2 m/s, but I did NOT reach a period source: unverified.)
  - TRL London 10 sites (Sterling 2009): all-age mean 1.46 m/s, 15th percentile 1.14-1.32.
  - TRL PCaTS (York 2011): site means 1.30-1.68.
  - Over-65s UK: mean about 0.9-1.0 (Musselwhite 2014, Asher 2012).
  - Gates (US, 2006): solo 1.44, groups of 2-4 1.32, 5+ 1.25; WALK-phase 1.37 vs hurrying (DON'T WALK) 1.53-1.56.
  - Knoblauch (US): under 65 means 1.46-1.56; over 65 1.19-1.31.
  - Report does not label "purposeful" vs "leisure".
- AL-Azzawi, Napier University thesis, Oct 2004, https://napier-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/257244 [READ, first 100k of 373k chars only]
  - Cites White (1994): city-centre free-flow mean about 80 m/min = 1.33 m/s (males 1.35, females 1.27). Cites Older (1968), O'Flaherty & Parkinson (1972), May & Hopkinson (1991), Leake (1991): shoppers slower, trip type matters; no values read. Commuters faster (Lautso & Murole 1974, Helsinki).
Best-supported (cross-check, [INF] for banding): unhurried/shopping/stroll about 1.0-1.2 m/s; typical free walk 1.3-1.5 m/s (central 1.4); purposeful/hurrying 1.5-1.7 m/s; older adult 0.9-1.1; baggage, groups, children slower. No pre-1992 UK primary source reached. Confidence: free speed medium-high (modern UK data; pedestrians are not known to have changed), bands medium-low.

## 2. Police foot patrol c.1990
- Police Foundation "The Value of Foot Patrol", Wakefield, 18 Jan 2006. https://www.police-foundation.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/foot_patrol.pdf [READ first 100k of 186k chars]. Unit beat policing introduced mid-1960s (area constable + area car); foot patrol was what got cut when resources were short. 1990 Operational Policing Review: only 20% of public said police did foot patrol a great deal/fair amount; 72% wanted balance or more on foot. No pace, beat size, pass-frequency numbers there.
- Hansard HC written answer 3 Nov 1988 (Hogg). https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1988/nov/03/police-constables [READ]: 31 Dec 1987, 43,347 constables on patrol = 60.5% of available strength (provincial forces); Met divisional constables about 30% of time on street duty. "Patrol" includes car, dog, support groups etc, so not a foot figure.
- Hansard 2 Mar 1989 (Hogg). https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written_answers/1989/mar/02/police-patrols [READ]: end 1988, 52,906 officers of all ranks on patrol = 55.8% of strength; Met about 1.6 million man-days street duty in 1988.
- Home Office PRSU Report 11/75 "Study of Urban Workloads", Comrie & Kings, Feb 1975 (1972-73 data, nine forces). https://ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Photocopy/29577NCJRS.pdf [READ first 100k of 173k]: foot patrol roughly 53% of unit-beat patrol time (tentative, garbled table). Panda cars normally single-manned. No beat length or pace.
- Hansard 12 Mar 1970 (Callaghan). https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1970/mar/12/police-patrol-methods [READ]: no data on beat percentage; "disproportionate effort."
- Edinburgh Review July 1842 via https://historyhome.co.uk/peel/laworder/patrols.htm [READ]: Metropolitan beats covered in 7 to 25 minutes (1842, not 1990; shows interval concept only).
- NOT FOUND: traditional beat pace (the "2.5 mph" or "3 mph" figure), measured pace, typical beat length, night pass frequency, single vs paired night patrol. Also not reached: Home Office/HMIC foot-patrol studies of 1985-92, Audit Commission "Streetwise" 1996 (cited only second-hand by Police Foundation), Newark Foot Patrol (US, 1981). Wikipedia "Beat (police)" and others appeared only as snippets [SNIPPET] and are not evidence.
- [INF] for the game: a beat constable's walk is about 1.1-1.3 m/s with stops (checking doors, points). Passing interval at a given spot on a night beat: unknown from sources; do not state a number as fact. Night foot patrol by single constables is plausible for the time but UNVERIFIED; the 1975 report only says foot patrol effort could be cut at certain times (para 119). Confidence: low.
- Ekblom & Heal, "The Police Response to Calls from the Public", Home Office RPU 1982 (NIJ reprint NCJ-92598). https://ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/92598NCJRS.pdf [READ]: one northern English subdivision, ~510 calls in 7 days; caller-reported response under 15 min in over half of cases; 17% over an hour; 13% arrived after the incident was over.

## 3. Custody under PACE 1984 (as in 1990)
- PACE as enacted, Part IV ss.40-44. https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1984/60/part/IV/crossheading/detentionconditions-and-duration/enacted [READ]:
  - s.41: 24 h limit from relevant time, then charge or release (bail or not).
  - s.42: superintendent may extend to 36 h, only if serious arrestable offence, evidence need, diligent investigation; not before second review, not after 24 h.
  - ss.43-44: magistrates (court) warrant, up to 36 h at a time, total max 96 h.
  - s.40: reviews: first no later than 6 h after detention authorised, second within 9 h of the first, later reviews at no more than 9 h intervals (as enacted). Inspector does review pre-charge. NB the 12 h interval in Kemp et al is a later amendment, not the 1990 rule.
  - I did not fetch the 1991-02-01 version of s.40; an Act amendment (Criminal Justice Act 1991? unverified) may have altered it. Treat 9 h as 1990 position with moderate confidence.
- Brown, "Detention at the Police Station under PACE 1984", Home Office Research Study 104, HMSO 1989. https://www.statewatch.org/statewatch-database/detention-at-the-police-station-under-the-police-and-criminal and https://www.ncjrs.gov/App/Publications/abstract.aspx?ID=136434 [READ, abstracts only]. Abstract gives no duration numbers.
- Bottomley, Coleman, Dixon, Gill, Wall, BJ Criminology 31(4), Autumn 1991, 347-364 (a medium northern English town; records 4 years before/after 1986, observations 1987, interviews 1988). https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/detention-suspects-police-custody-impact-police-and-criminal [READ, abstract]: mean detention before charge did not change significantly in 1986 but rose significantly in 1987. No numbers read.
- Brown, Ellis, Larcombe, "Changing the Code", HORS 129, 1992. https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/changing-code-police-detention-under-revised-pace-codes-practice [READ, abstract]: no durations.
- Kemp, Balmer, Pleasence, "Whose time is it anyway? Factors associated with duration in police custody", Crim LR (about 2012; references run to 2011), 44 stations, 24,922 first detentions. https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/711683 [READ]: modern data, NOT 1990: median 6 h 36 min, mean 9 h 18 min; 47% released within 6 h; 5.2% over 24 h; 2.5% 36 h+. Useful only as an upper-end comparator. It says earlier studies found more releases in the first review period.
- NOT FOUND: any numeric typical custody length for 1989-93 (Brown 1989/1992/1997 figures not reached). [INF, low confidence] the older studies are generally recalled as showing most detentions under about 6 hours with a median of a few hours; this is memory, not evidence.
- Refusing name and address, 1990: PACE s.25 (as at 1 Feb 1991). https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1984/60/section/25/1991-02-01?view=plain [READ]. Section lets a constable arrest for a non-arrestable offence when a summons is impracticable and a general arrest condition applies, including inability to learn a name, doubt it is genuine, or no satisfactory address for service. [INF, from the statute's structure] There was no general offence of refusing to give name and address; refusal was a ground to arrest for an offence the person was suspected of, not an offence in itself. Specific statutory duties existed (e.g. road traffic) but I did not verify those. Confidence medium-high on the general rule, not confirmed by a case or commentary today.

## 4. Face recognition distance and light
- Wagenaar & van der Schrier 1996, "Face recognition as a function of distance and illumination: a practical tool for use in the courtroom", Psychology, Crime & Law 2(4):321-332. https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/10683160410001715123 (page title fetched; page text appears to be the abstract of the 1996 paper or the De Jong et al 2005 follow-up: the tool's summary does not separate them, so check) [READ, abstract only]
  - Famous or familiar faces, 12 s per image, 7 distances from 3 to 40 m, 9 illuminances 0.3 to 3000 lux. Recognition improved with nearer and brighter. "Rule of Fifteen": the target diagnostic value of 15 is reached at no more than 15 m and no less than 15 lux, even in ideal conditions. Wikipedia-style "15 m / 15 lux" is correct.
- De Jong, Wagenaar, Wolters, Verstijnen 2005, "Familiar face recognition as a function of distance and illumination: a practical tool for use in the courtroom", Psychology, Crime & Law 11(1):87-97 (the 10.1080/10683160410001715123 link). Dating per the Frontiers paper's reference list [SNIPPET-level via Frontiers, READ].
- Lim, Lee & Or, Frontiers in Psychology, 28 Feb 2022. https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.834806/pdf [READ]: confirms W&vdS 1996 / De Jong 2005 used 9 illuminations 0.3-3000 lux; DiNardo & Rainey 1989/1991 and Nyman et al 2019 found accuracy rises with illumination (0.7, 10, 300 lux). Lindsay distance work not mentioned in it; not reached.
- NOT FOUND: unfamiliar-vs-familiar distance numbers; Lindsay distance study; street light levels. [INF] Lit generally says familiar faces are recognised much further and in poorer light than strangers; for strangers the 15 m / 15 lux limit is an optimistic ceiling. Unverified today.
- 15 lux context [INF, not sourced]: typical British residential streetlighting is of the order of a few lux, so the rule implies night strangers beyond a few metres are not reliably identifiable.

## 5. Response times around 1990
- Hansard HC written answer 28 Jan 1991 (Dorrell). https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1991/jan/28/ambulance-services [READ]: London Ambulance Service standards "since 1974": 50th percentile 7 min, 95th 14 min; under the new classification 50th is 8 min, 95th unchanged. (Describes London only, so for northern English towns the generic ORCON standard is not shown in this answer.)
- Hansard 21 Jan 1991 (Dorrell). https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1991/jan/21/ambulance-services [READ]: response times "always recommended rather than mandatory"; new management executive guide reclassified services as urban/rural, urban standards slightly relaxed, rural tightened. No numbers.
- Hansard 8 Feb 1994. https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1994/feb/08/ambulance-response-times [READ]: Patient's Charter 1992/93 standards: urban 14 min, rural 19 min, 95% of emergency calls; performance 94.1% urban (ex-London), 96.9% rural.
- Hansard 16 Mar 1994. https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1994/mar/16/ambulance-services [READ]: ORCON standards date from 1974; revised standards from a 1990 review are in the Patient's Charter.
- Hansard 22 Jul 1987 [READ]: ORCON standard performance not held centrally.
- The "8 minutes for 50%" version is from the 1990 review per the 1991 London answer; I did not read the original 1974 ORCON text (Wikipedia/other pages appeared only as snippets [SNIPPET]). Treat 1974 detail as unverified.
- Police: no 1988-92 emergency response target found. Hansard 7 Feb 1994 [READ] says target times for answering calls and rapid-reaction incidents are not held centrally; forces to publish indicators for 1993-94 during 1994-95. The only measured data: Ekblom & Heal 1982 (above). [INF] Local urgent targets existed, usually around 10-15 min urban, not verified.
