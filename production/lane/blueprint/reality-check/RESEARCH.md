## L. Street lamps (worst first)

L1. Spacing. BS 5489-3:1977, the code side streets lit between 1977 and 1989 were laid out under, had two groups: B8 (district roads, 8 m) and B5/6 (residential roads, 5 to 6 m mounting height). For group B5/6 the target spacing was 34 m plus or minus 10% (31 to 37 m); for B8 the spacing varied with road width (figure not reached). Source: S. Fotios and C. Robbins, "Research note: Describing average illuminance for P-class roads", Lighting Research and Technology 52(8), 1057 to 1062, 2020 (https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/158459/10/1477153520911193.pdf), fetched 24 Nov 2026 by the lamp helper and again by a separate fetch (both recorded in research/lamps.md). It is a modern paper describing the 1977 code, not the code itself (the code's text: UNREACHED, paywalled). Confidence: good for the code's figure; real streets may be older layouts or may differ.

L2. Brightness. BS 5489-3:1989, kept in the 1992 reprint, dropped spacing rules for three performance classes for subsidiary roads (average and minimum lux): 3/1 10.0 and 5.0; 3/2 6.0 and 2.5; 3/3 3.5 and 1.0. Same source, Table 1, from the second fetch (table layout flattened, values aligned by order; research/lamps.md). The S1 class (15 and 5) belongs to the 2003 code, not 1989. The blueprint's "real side streets lit 3.5 to 6 lux" is therefore the period's own classes 3/3 to 3/2.

L3. Not found: one side, staggered or opposite pairs; junction and bend practice; which SOX wattage went on 5 to 6 m columns (35, 55, 90 W are the period ratings: lamptech.co.uk, read). DTp TD 30/87 (Nov 1987, read) gives no spacing and defers to BS 5489.

L4. Owed from the PC: column counts against street length in dated 1988 to 1992 photographs of northern port terraces (Hull History Centre "Photographs of Hull in the 1980s" catalogue, SNIPPET; Geograph by date).

## P. Phone boxes and street furniture

P1. BT had about 100,000 public call boxes in 1992 and 78,000 in 1987, with about 5,000 Mercury boxes in 1992 (Hansard, 12 Feb 1992, read). Ofcom gives 92,000 for 1992 (modern, read): the figures disagree. No density, walking-distance or siting rule found. Inferred, low confidence (population from memory, density assumed): about one box per 570 people, so roughly 3 to 6 for a district this size. The map's three sit inside that rough estimate.

P2. Furniture per 10 m of street: no period count found. Only modern central London counts (2023: 6.5 to 15 items per 100 m) were read, an upper bound for a different place. The blueprint's "5 props per 10 m" came from a Mafia sample and our own scene: Mafia is never a reference, so the number has no standing. Owed from the PC: counts on dated photographs.

## W. Widths and frontages

W1. Model byelaws of 1877 (via Designing Buildings Wiki, 2021, read; primary text UNREACHED): new streets at least 36 ft (11 m) wide, carriageway at least 24 ft (7.3 m), each footway at least one sixth of the street (about 6 ft, 1.8 m), kerb 3 to 7 in (76 to 178 mm), back streets 13 to 16 ft. Hansard 1976 confirms 7.3 m as the usual carriageway (read). Older dockside streets laid out before the byelaws could be narrower. The map's 2.0 m pavements, 115 mm kerbs and 7 to 10 m carriageways sit inside these; Quay Street's 10.25 m front to front is just under the byelaw 11 m, possible for a pre-byelaw street.

W2. Terraced frontage, plot depth and door width: no source reached. Inferred, no source, low confidence: frontage about 13 to 16 ft (4 to 5 m), so about 40 to 50 front doors per 100 m of terraced street counting both sides; a warehoused dock road far fewer. Hessle Road, Hull (conservation appraisal, read): some three-storey terraces, 10 ft back passages. Byelaw terraces in general (Wikipedia, "Byelaw terraced house", read): 6 ft front gardens in some. Inter-war shops 18 to 20 ft wide, about 50 ft deep (read).

## C. People and police

C1. PACE 1984 as enacted (read): 24 h before charge, a superintendent may extend to 36 h, magistrates to 96 h in all; reviews at 6 h, then 9 h later, then every 9 h. Typical 1990 durations: not reached (Brown 1989, Bottomley 1991, Brown 1992: abstracts only). Modern comparator only: median about 6.6 h. Refusing a name and address to a constable was not in itself an offence; it was a ground for arrest under s.25 (read; the "not an offence" reading is inference from the statute).

C2. Face recognition: Wagenaar and van der Schrier 1996, the "rule of fifteen": familiar faces identified reliably only within about 15 m and above about 15 lux (abstract only; the page may be the 2005 follow-up by De Jong and others). 15 lux is brighter than a lit side street (L2). Stranger figures: not reached.

C3. Walking speed: TRL PPR700 (2014, read): London mean about 1.46 m/s; DfT crossing design value 1.2 m/s. White 1994 as cited (partly read): city-centre free flow about 1.33 m/s. No pre-1992 UK source reached. Beat constable's pace, beat length and night revisit: not found.

C4. Ambulance standard (London service): 1974 figures 7 min for 50% and 14 min for 95%; revised to 8 min in 1990 (Hansard 1991, read); Patient's Charter 1992 to 1993: 14 min urban, 19 min rural (read). No central police response target in the period (Hansard 1994, read).

## F. Fares

F1. London 1990 (SI 1990/1076, in force 8 June 1990; Hansard 18 May 1990; read): 1.00 pound minimum including a 40p hire charge for the first 1,021.5 yd, then 20p per 340.5 yd (about 1.04 pounds a mile). A 1 mile ride: about 1.60 pounds (inference).

F2. Outside London (ITS Leeds working paper 382, mean fares at Nov 1991 across England and Wales districts, read): 2 miles by day 2.12 to 2.49 pounds, 4 miles 3.69 to 4.22. That implies roughly 80p a mile and a flag of 55 to 75p (inference by the session; the helper note's own derivation of about 1.10 pounds a mile is wrong and is corrected here). These are means across England and Wales districts, not a northern figure, and 17 months after the London tariff (London fares rose about 9% in June 1991). A Sefton 1991 card: 1.20 pounds for 1 mile (blog image, low confidence). No Hull or Grimsby tariff found.

F3. So: the game's 80p plus 1.10 pounds a mile is above both the London rate and the average outside London; a ride inside the Hook (under a mile) would have cost about 1.00 to 1.40 pounds (estimate); his ruled 2.50 to 3.50 a fare is roughly a 2 to 4 mile ride at the late-1991 average.

## D. Docks
