Researched 2026-11-25 from the cloud sandbox. ## 1. How long real street fights last (CCTV)

- Philpot, Liebst, Levine, Bernasco, Lindegaard, "Would I be helped?", American Psychologist 2020 (online 3 June 2019). 219 CCTV clips (UK 95, Netherlands 63, South Africa 61), drawn from 1,225 raw clips. Mean conflict length 3.27 minutes (SD 4.20). Mean 16.3 bystanders. Clips ran until the incident dissipated or police separated the parties. Includes mild disagreements up to grave violence, so it is not a pure "fight" length. https://gwern.net/doc/crime/2020-philpot.pdf (page extract; abstract at https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/37604). Solid for duration of conflicts in general. No blow counts or knockdown rate reported.
- Levine, Taylor, Best, "Third parties, violence, and conflict resolution", Psychological Science 22(3), 2011, pp. 406-412. 42 CCTV incidents, 312 people, two northwest England town-centre drinking areas, data Jan 2006 to Feb 2007. Clips ran 1 to 8 minutes (that is the clip length coded, not fight length). Mean 5.4 bystanders per clip. Bystanders produced 1,152 de-escalating acts and 287 escalating acts, so calming outnumbered escalating by about 4 to 1; calming rose with group size. https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/shb09/levine1.pdf and https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/49921/ . Solid for third-party behaviour. No blow counts.
- A PhD thesis cited in the search (University of Amsterdam, Thesis.pdf) reports 33 percent of recorded conflicts had no physical aggression, and that about 7 in 10 assaults have people present besides the two parties (citing Planty 2002). I read only the first 100,000 characters. https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/69133238/Thesis.pdf . Fairly solid, secondary.
- Not found: number of blows per fight, knockdown rate, share of fights ending with a knockdown. No source in this pass gave these. Treat as an open gap (see list at end).

## 2. Stab wound lethality

- Christensen, Nielsen, Ridley, Lecky, Morris, "Outcomes and costs of penetrating trauma injury in England and Wales" (TARN data, patients Jan 2000 to Dec 2005; publication date not shown on the page I read). 1,365 patients reached hospital; overall in-hospital mortality 8.3 percent; stabbings 7.2 percent, shootings 10.6 percent. Mortality by worst injury severity: ISS 16-24 12.1 percent, ISS 25-34 22.4 percent, ISS 35-75 55.1 percent. Mean time to death 1.6 days. Region mortality for all penetrating injuries (stab and shot together; column alignment unclear, verify): head 25.4, neck 15.1, abdomen 10.8, thorax 9.4, lower limb 9.1, upper limb 4.4 percent. Of serious injuries, thorax 43.8 percent, abdomen 21.0, head 9.8, lower limb 8.1, upper limb 6.6, neck 3.9. https://core.ac.uk/download/337055.pdf . Fair: hospital-arrival cases only, so it misses people who died at the scene.
- "The rising burden of penetrating knife injuries", Injury Prevention 27(5), study period 2014 to 2018 (UK major trauma centre; publication year not shown). 1,373 penetrating knife injuries, fatality 0.9 percent (12), 39.4 percent thoracic, 25.8 percent abdominal, 14.1 percent needed surgery. https://injuryprevention.bmj.com/content/27/5/467.full.pdf . Fair. In-hospital only, so it shows what prompt treatment achieves: under 1 in 100.
- Johannesdottir et al., "Penetrating stab injuries in Iceland", Scand J Trauma Resusc Emerg Med 27:7, 23 Jan 2019. 88 stabbed 2000-2015; 15 died before reaching hospital (17 percent of all); of 73 admitted, 3 died within 30 days (4.1 percent). Injury counts: chest 32, abdomen 26, upper limbs 26, head/neck/face 21, lower limbs 10, back 6. https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s13049-018-0582-2 . Solid but small and not UK.
- Lund forensic abstract on fatal single stab injuries (pubmed 29713941, 2018): the single fatal stab was usually to the left chest. No percentages. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29713941/ . Qualitative only.
- Useful band from these: of those who reach hospital alive, roughly 1 to 8 percent die (the lower figure from recent knife-only data, the higher from older all-penetrating data); about 1 in 6 of stabbed people in the Iceland series died before hospital. Chest and abdomen hold most serious injuries; head and neck had the highest per-region mortality; limb mortality was lowest (4 to 9 percent in the mixed TARN table). Death usually took days after reaching hospital (mean 1.6 days), but death at the scene is common among fatal cases.
- Not found: a clean stab-only case fatality by region, and time-to-death-from-bleeding figures (minutes). Not reached.

## 3. Single-punch deaths

- Pilgrim, Gerostamoulos, Drummer, "King hit" fatalities in Australia 2000-2012 (Drug and Alcohol Dependence, Dec 2013, reported via MedicalXpress). 90 deaths in 12 years; toxicology available for 63, alcohol in 49 of those. The page gave no figure for head-hitting-ground. https://medicalxpress.com/news/2013-12-alcohol-key-lethal-incidents.amp . Solid on counts; page extract only, not the paper.
- Monash Lens, 29 May 2025: 172 Australians killed in one-punch attacks since 2000; describes a "two-stage injury profile", punch incapacitates, then a fall onto concrete causes the fatal head injury; cites Sarkar and Schumann, Medicine Science and the Law 2025, 287 convictions 1990-2020. https://lens.monash.edu/@medicine-health/2025/05/29/1387622/behind-the-blow-why-coward-punch-laws-are-failing-to-deliver-justice . Fair: a news-style piece, no percentage.
- Springer reference entry (Patton and McIntosh, 2017, Handbook of Human Motion): typical case is an intoxicated man punched in the face, falling backward, striking the back of the head on a hard surface. Injury from punch, fall or both; no figures on the page. https://link.springer.com/referenceworkentry/10.1007/978-3-319-14418-4_185 . Qualitative.
- Not found: percentage where the ground impact is the cause, and rate relative to all assaults. The count (about 7 a year in Australia) set against many thousands of assaults shows rarity, but I have no UK denominator. Gap.

## 4. Neck holds

- Idaho State Police SANE timeline sheet (undated; cites Kabat and Anderson 1943; Reay and Holloway 1982; Sauvageau 2011; Mitchell 2012; Stellpflug 2020). Carotid occlusion to unconsciousness 5 to 10 seconds, average 6.8. Petechiae 10 to 30 seconds. Loss of bowel control about 60 seconds. In a series of 14 (Sauvageau 2011), breathing stopped from 62 to 157 seconds. https://isp.idaho.gov/./wp-content/uploads/Forensics/SANE/Strangulation/Physiological-Consequences-of-Strangulation-Seconds-to-Minute-Timeline.pdf . Fair: a teaching sheet summarising the papers; some rows poorly labelled.
- Wikipedia "Chokehold": average about 9 seconds to unconsciousness across holds (citing Jits Magazine, 31 March 2021); air chokes slower, may stay conscious a minute or more; blood choke, consciousness returns after release; if air choke is held to unconsciousness, the person "may not regain consciousness". Cites Koiwai 1987 (judo chokes, J Forensic Sci) and Reay and Eisele 1982 without figures. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chokehold . Weak (secondary).
- ACEP forensic piece, 18 Sep 2018: pressure on the carotids gives a vagal response with a brief loss of consciousness; no timings. https://www.acep.org/forensicmedicine/newsroom/fm-newsroom-articles/sept2018/physiology-of-judo-choke . Qualitative.
- Snippet only: a study titled "Time to unconsciousness from sportive chokes in fully resisting highly trained combatants" (2020, Sports/Int J Perf Anal in Sport per RePEc) and PMC5457939, PMC6466005; I could not open them. Worth fetching from the PC.
- Not found: a firm seconds-past-unconsciousness threshold for brain injury or death. Sources only say harm follows if the hold is kept up; the sheet's 60 to 157 second respiratory-arrest range is the nearest number. Gap.

## 5. Knock-out

- Hånell and Rostami, "How Can a Punch Knock You Out?", Frontiers in Neurology 11:570566 (2020): onset almost instant, consciousness regained "within a few minutes or less"; no exact seconds. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2020.570566/pdf . Solid, no number.
- British Army Boxing ringside suspension table (JSP 950, marked Sep 15): knockout with loss of consciousness counted as "up to 1 minute" or "over 1 minute"; no-LOC KO means failing the 10-second count. So sport practice treats under 60 seconds as the normal KO band. https://www.britisharmyboxing.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/RINGSIDE-INJURIES-SUSPENSION-PERIODS.pdf . Fair for a banding rule.
- BrainFacts, 20 Nov 2018: under a minute is "brief"; over a minute needs hospital; severe injury can last days. https://www.brainfacts.org/Diseases-and-Disorders/Injury/2018/What-Happens-When-Youre-Knocked-Unconscious-112018 . Fair.
- Not found: skull fracture or death risk from a cosh or bar blow to the head (forensic series), and duration of unconsciousness in assaults specifically. Gap. Snippet only: PubMed 8914149 (acute subdural from professional boxing), not opened.

## 6. England and Wales law around 1990

- CPS Offences against the Person guidance (current version, updated 2022 to 2026; first charging standard date not shown; the original was issued in the 1990s, so check the 1994 text): common assault or battery: grazes, scratches, minor bruising, swelling, reddening, superficial cuts. ABH (s.47 OAPA 1861): more than transient or trifling, such as damaged teeth or bones, extensive bruising, cuts needing sutures, loss of consciousness. GBH (s.20, s.18): "really serious harm", life-changing injury or major treatment (intensive care, transfusion). s.18 needs intent. Maximums: common assault 6 months (summary only, Criminal Justice Act 1988 s.39); s.47 5 years; s.20 5 years; s.18 life. https://www.cps.gov.uk/legal-guidance/offences-against-person-incorporating-charging-standard . Solid on category lines, but it is the modern text; the 1990 maximums are the same as I know them, and the CPS strangulation page repeats them: https://www.cps.gov.uk/legal-guidance/strangulation-and-suffocation .
- Police Act 1964 s.51 (text in force 1 Feb 1991, legislation.gov.uk): assault on a constable in execution of duty, summary, up to 6 months and/or a fine at level 5; resisting or wilfully obstructing a constable, up to 1 month and/or level 3 fine. https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1964/48/part/IV/1991-02-01?view=plain . Solid (primary law). The 1 Feb 1991 snapshot is the nearest I opened to 1990.
- Strangulation as its own offence (s.75A Serious Crime Act 2015) did not exist in 1990; ignore for period rules.
- Not found: murder and manslaughter sentencing practice around 1990 (murder: mandatory life; manslaughter: discretionary up to life; both I know from memory, not sourced here). Typical actual sentences for ABH and s.20 around 1990 not reached. Gap.

## 7. Fingerprints and blood grouping, 1990

- Law Society of Scotland Journal, 18 Sep 2006 (D. Mulhern): the Home Office set the 16-point standard for England and Wales in 1953 (minimum 16 matching ridge points, no discrepancy), later relaxed in a few "dire and crucial" cases; no scientific basis found by reviews. England and Wales moved to a non-numeric standard in 2001. https://lawscot.org.uk/members/journal/issues/vol-51-issue-09/a-new-print-job/ . Fair-solid.
- Hansard written answer, 24 Oct 2001: ACPO changed from 16 points to a non-numerical system, effective Monday 11 June 2001. https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/2001/oct/24/fingerprint-evidence . Solid (primary).
- Wikipedia NAFIS: established 1986 (as a project), upgraded system operating April 2001. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Automated_Fingerprint_Identification_System . Weak-fair (secondary) and the 1986 date is the start of the programme, not a working national database; treat 1990 searches as manual, ten-print card collections by pattern class, with a scene mark compared by hand against named suspects. That last part is general knowledge, not sourced here.
- Allinson et al., IEEE Trans. Information Forensics and Security 2(3), 2007 (Lincolnshire trial): even in 2005 to 2006, latent-to-identification took about four days; with wireless transmission under two hours; identification rate from scene marks 14.8 percent (traditional) and 17.9 percent (wireless). https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/3378/1/allinsonn1.pdf . Fair; a later period, but it gives a modern ceiling for how slow and low-yield the older path was.
- DNA: Privacy International blog, 27 Jan 2006: DNA matching first caught an offender in 1987; PACE 1984 limited samples; a 1993 Royal Commission recommended a database; the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994 amended PACE. https://www.privacyinternational.org/blog/1269/birth-dna-database-uk . Wikipedia gives the National DNA Database as set up in 1995: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_National_DNA_Database . Fair.
- Not found: sourced detail on lifting methods (powder, tape lifts, ninhydrin), surfaces, and blood groups (ABO, enzymes) used in 1990. Gap; I have only general knowledge.

## 8. Bystander behaviour

- Philpot et al. 2020 (above): at least one bystander intervened in 90.9 percent of 219 conflicts; mean 3.76 interveners per clip; each extra bystander raised the odds of intervening by about 1.10 (OR 1.10, 95 percent CI 1.03 to 1.18). Same intervention rate in UK, Netherlands, South Africa. https://gwern.net/doc/crime/2020-philpot.pdf . Solid.
- Liebst et al., "Social relations and presence of others predict bystander intervention", Aggressive Behavior, 2019: 764 bystanders at 81 violent incidents on Copenhagen CCTV; the abstract describes a classic bystander effect for single bystanders (intervention less likely with more people present). No numbers in the abstract. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31359450/ . Solid, but the crowd-size direction conflicts with Philpot; the reconciliation is that individual odds fall with crowd size while the chance that someone acts rises.
- Levine 2011 (above): calming acts about 4 times the escalating acts, and growing with group size.
- Caution: all of this is modern CCTV, much of it in the Netherlands, South Africa and Denmark. Modern bystanders have phones and expect police; 1990 behaviour may differ. No 1990 source found.

## 11. Cold harbour water

- Tipton, "Immersion in cold water: sudden death and prolonged survival", Proc Physiol Soc 44 SA11, 2019 (University of Portsmouth). Time to cardio-respiratory arrest after submersion about 130 seconds; chance of successful resuscitation near zero by 27 minutes; below 6 C the window stretches to about 66 minutes. https://www.physoc.org/?p=81700 . Solid (conference abstract by the main UK researcher), but the 130 seconds is for a submerged person.
- RNLI cold water shock page: UK and Irish sea about 12 C average, under the 15 C cold-water line; cold shock passes in under a minute; "helpless in seconds" is the wording. https://rnli.org/safety/know-the-risks/cold-water-shock . Fair (charity page, no date). Seasonal range is not given (a harbour in winter is colder than 12 C; I did not get a source for it).
- The Conversation, Tipton and colleagues: cold shock about a minute; hypothermia takes at least 30 minutes to become fatal. https://theconversation.com/diving-into-cold-water-can-be-deadly-heres-how-to-survive-it-119341 . Fair (undated page).
- Band suggested by the sources: an unconscious person with the face under water is dead in minutes (about 2 minutes to arrest, resuscitation hopeless past about 27); a conscious person in cold water faces a first minute of gasping, then incapacitation, with hypothermic death not before about 30 minutes.
