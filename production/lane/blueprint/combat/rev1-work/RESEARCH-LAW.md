# Research: weapons and policing law in England, 1990

Read on 2026-11-25. All URLs below were reached by search then fetched. Fetch results were summarised by a tool, so quotation is short and paraphrase is marked. Confidence labels: CONFIRMED (read in a primary source) or UNVERIFIED.

## 1. Criminal Justice Act 1988 s.139 (blade or point in a public place)

- Rule: an offence to have with you in a public place any article which has a blade or is sharply pointed. CONFIRMED.
- Folding pocketknife: not caught unless the cutting edge of the blade exceeds 3 inches (s.139(2)-(3)). CONFIRMED.
- Defence: the accused must prove good reason or lawful authority (s.139(4)). Examples in s.139(5): use at work, religious reasons, or as part of any national costume. CONFIRMED.
- "Public place" includes any place to which the public have or are permitted access, on payment or otherwise. CONFIRMED.
- Penalty as enacted and still so in 1991: summary only, fine not exceeding level 3 on the standard scale. No prison in 1990. (The 1996 Act later substituted prison and a higher fine, in force 4 July 1996.) CONFIRMED.
- Stop and search: s.140 extended PACE 1984 s.1 so constables could search for such articles. CONFIRMED.
- Commencement: 29 September 1988, per the Home Office minister Peter Lloyd in the Commons, 7 December 1989. He gave 150 prosecutions and 130 convictions for the rest of 1988 in England and Wales. CONFIRMED by Hansard only. I did not find the commencement order itself. Commencement No. 2 Order (SI 1988/1676) is for 12 October 1988 and does not list ss.139-141; the order that does is UNVERIFIED.

## 2. CJA 1988 s.141 and the Offensive Weapons Order 1988 (SI 1988/2019)

- s.141 offence: to manufacture, sell, hire, offer for sale or hire, expose or possess for sale or hire, lend or give a specified weapon; also importation is banned. Firearms Act weapons and crossbows are excluded. Defences for Crown or visiting-force functions and for museums or galleries. CONFIRMED.
- Penalty: summary, up to 6 months, or a fine up to level 5, or both. CONFIRMED.
- Order made 17 November 1988, in force two months later, 18 January 1989. CONFIRMED.
- Weapons listed in the Order (antiques over 100 years old excluded):
  - knuckleduster (and any weapon incorporating one)
  - swordstick
  - handclaw
  - belt buckle knife
  - push dagger
  - hollow kubotan
  - footclaw
  - shuriken, shaken or death star
  - balisong or butterfly knife
  - telescopic truncheon
  - blowpipe or blowgun
  - kusari gama
  - kyoketsu shoge
  - manrikigusari or kusari
  CONFIRMED against the Order's Schedule as made. Caution: later amendment orders (for example SI 2004 and 2021 versions exist) added items. A search result listed the Order's point-in-time text of 18 Jan 1989 (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1988/2019/1989-01-18/data.pdf, not fetched). I used the "made" text, so the list above is the 1988 list, but whether every item was in the original and none was added by 1990 is UNVERIFIED in detail.
- Private possession: not banned. The offences are supply, manufacture and import; possession only when for sale or hire. Public carrying would fall under the 1953 Act or s.139. CONFIRMED from the s.141 text.

## 3. Restriction of Offensive Weapons Act 1959 (as amended)

- Flick knives (blade opens automatically by pressure on a button, spring or similar) and gravity knives (blade released by gravity or centrifugal force, locked by a device). CONFIRMED.
- Offences: manufacture, sell, hire, offer for sale or hire, lend or give; the 1961 Act added exposing, or possessing for sale or hire. Importation also prohibited. CONFIRMED.
- Penalty: summary, up to 6 months, or a fine (level 4 substituted, raised to level 5 by CJA 1988 s.46), or both. Date s.46 took effect is UNVERIFIED (likely 1989 to 1991).
- Private possession: not an offence. CONFIRMED. Carrying in public could still be an offence under the 1953 Act.
- Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Eliz2/7-8/37/1991-02-01 (read 2026-11-25)

## 4. Prevention of Crime Act 1953 s.1

- Offence: without lawful authority or reasonable excuse (proof on the accused) to have with you in a public place any offensive weapon. CONFIRMED.
- Penalty: point-in-time text at 1 Feb 1991 reads: summary, up to 6 months or a fine (read as £200 by the fetch tool), or both; on indictment up to 2 years or a fine, or both. The sums shown look un-updated and the level-5 effect of CJA 1988 s.46 is UNVERIFIED for 1990. Use "up to 6 months summary, up to 2 years on indictment" as safe.
- What counts: the 1953 Act definition (s.4) is any article made or adapted for causing injury, or intended by the person having it for such use by him or another. This definition is from my recall of s.4 and I did not fetch it: UNVERIFIED here. Case law (Petrie, Simpson) divides weapons into made, adapted, and intended; also not fetched.
- Source: https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Eliz2/1-2/14/1991-02-01 (read 2026-11-25)

## 5. Knife sales and age limits in 1990

- No law against selling ordinary knives to under-16s in 1990. Section 141A (sale of knives and certain articles with a blade or point to under-16s) was inserted by s.6 of the Offensive Weapons Act 1996, in force 1 January 1997 (SI 1996/3063 art.2). Age later raised to 18 (2007, not checked). CONFIRMED for the 1 January 1997 date.
- The 1989 Hansard debate does not mention under-16 sales. I found no age rule for adults. An adult buying a utility or kitchen knife faced no age or licence rule: CONFIRMED only by absence in the statutes read, so treat as strongly likely, not proven.
- Sources: https://legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1996/3063/article/2/made/data.html (read 2026-11-25); https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/33/section/141A (search result, not fetched)

## 6. Firearms Act 1968 as amended by the Firearms (Amendment) Act 1988

- (a) Revolver or other handgun: section 1 firearm needing a firearm certificate. Handguns were not prohibited in 1990. Handgun bans came in 1997 (two Acts). CONFIRMED by Wikipedia summary of the 1988 Act (secondary) and by the absence of handguns in s.5 as read; s.5 itself was not read. A self-loading or pump-action rifle, and some smoothbore revolvers, became prohibited under the 1988 Act. The 1988 Act: royal assent 15 Nov 1988, most provisions 1 Feb 1989, fully in force 2 April 1991.
- (b) Shotguns: s.1(3) excludes a shotgun from s.1 only if the barrel is at least 24 inches, the bore not over 2 inches, no magazine or a non-detachable one holding no more than two cartridges, and not a revolver gun. A short-barrelled shotgun therefore needs a section 1 firearm certificate, not a shotgun certificate. s.4(1): shortening a shotgun barrel below 24 inches is an offence (a dealer may do so only to replace a defective part, leaving at least 24 inches). Possessing a shortened shotgun without a certificate is the aggravated s.1 offence (s.4(4)). CONFIRMED. The text I fetched was a later consolidation (1992 version link, text includes 2016 amendments); wording on these points was stable but UNVERIFIED for 1990 to the word.
- (c) Penalties (Schedule 6 Part I, point in time 3 Feb 1995, https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/27/schedule/6/part/I/1995-02-03/data.pdf, read 2026-11-25). The 1995 changes are marked, so the pre-1995 values can be inferred:
  - s.1(1) no certificate: summary 6 months. On indictment 5 years in 1995 (substituted 3 Feb 1995); the 1990 figure was probably the same or lower: UNVERIFIED. The aggravated form (s.4(4)) 7 years from 1995; 1990 figure UNVERIFIED.
  - s.4 shortening a shotgun: summary 6 months; 7 years on indictment from 1995; 1990 figure UNVERIFIED.
  - s.16 intent to endanger life: life imprisonment, indictment only. Shown as unchanged since CJA 1972. CONFIRMED.
  - s.17(1) use to resist arrest: life, indictment only. s.17(2) possession in a Schedule 1 offence: life (amended by CJA 1988 per footnote; earlier figure UNVERIFIED). CONFIRMED for life on s.17(1).
  - s.18 carrying with criminal intent: life, indictment only. CONFIRMED.
  - s.19 loaded firearm in a public place: summary 6 months; 7 years on indictment from 1995; 1990 figure UNVERIFIED (likely 5 years or lower). Not applicable to air weapons on indictment.
  - Summary fines in the table read "£400"; that is an old CJA 1972 figure and the table shows it with a standard-scale flag. The actual 1990 summary fine level is UNVERIFIED.
- Sources: https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/27/body/1992-03-16/data.html (read 2026-11-25); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firearms_(Amendment)_Act_1988 (read 2026-11-25)

## 7. Beat constable equipment around 1990

- Wooden truncheon: most forces used the traditional 14-inch wooden truncheon until the mid-1990s; Home Secretary Michael Howard authorised batons on 20 June 1994. Male trousers had a sewn-in truncheon pocket until the mid-1990s. Secondary source (Wikipedia). UNVERIFIED against a primary or museum source.
- Handcuffs: rigid or hinged handcuffs are standard; Hiatt Quickcuffs (Speedcuffs) appear in the Science Museum's mid-to-late 1990s belt. Chain versus hinged in 1990: UNVERIFIED. Pre-1990 chain "Hiatt" handcuffs were common, from recall only.
- Whistle: replaced the rattle in the 1800s; whether still carried in 1990 UNVERIFIED (from recall, Metropolitan whistles were still issued, but I have no dated source).
- Personal radio: personal radios from the 1960s per Wikipedia; a 1990 beat officer carrying one is likely but the date of universal issue is UNVERIFIED.
- Pocketbook: standard equipment, no date found. Pocketbooks (for PACE-compliant notes) are standard; UNVERIFIED for date.
- Science Museum belt (1995-1999, "representative of 1990s Metropolitan Police equipment"): CS spray, friction-lock baton, Hiatt Quickcuffs, protection kit. This is after 1990 so it shows what had not yet arrived.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_uniforms_and_equipment_in_the_United_Kingdom (read 2026-11-25); https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co504411/metropolitan-police-equipment-belt (read 2026-11-25)
