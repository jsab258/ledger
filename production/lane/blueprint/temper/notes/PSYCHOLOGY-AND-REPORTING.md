# Psychology of honesty, silence and reporting: research notes

Read 24 Nov 2026, web only. Each claim has its URL; "(unverified)" means I did not read it in a source. Gaps are listed at the end.

## Part A. Personality

### A1. Models
- Big Five: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism.
- HEXACO adds a sixth factor, Honesty-Humility (sincerity, fairness, greed avoidance, modesty). Its Agreeableness is narrower than the Big Five version (it holds forgiveness, gentleness, patience), and Emotionality replaces Neuroticism (it holds fearfulness, anxiety, dependence, sentimentality, and lacks anger). Pletzer found HEXACO Emotionality and Big Five Neuroticism relate to deviance in opposite directions. Source: https://pure.eur.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/48180213/Pletzer-et-al.-2019-JVB.pdf (read 24 Nov 2026). Factor and facet lists are from my own knowledge (unverified here).

### A2. Honesty-Humility (HH) and wrongdoing: effect sizes
- Pletzer et al. 2019 (meta-analysis, workplace deviance, k = 12 to 15 samples, N about 3,200 to 4,200). Corrected correlations: HH -.48, Conscientiousness -.40, Agreeableness -.19, Emotionality -.14, Openness and Extraversion about zero. HEXACO explained 32% of the variance in deviance, the Big Five 19%. HH carried 57% of the relative weight. Source: https://pure.eur.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/48180213/Pletzer-et-al.-2019-JVB.pdf (24 Nov 2026). No HEXACO facet data existed to meta-analyse. All self-report.
- Cheating and lying in tasks (reanalysis of 16 studies, N about 4,900). Each 1 SD of HH roughly halves the odds of dishonesty (OR 0.53). Conscientiousness is weak (OR 0.86), and the other HEXACO factors do nothing. Nobody else adds anything once HH is in. About 26% of trials were dishonest overall. Source: https://www.cambridge.org:443/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5B40039F2A564AEC265CEF288D917D5D/S1930297500009232a.pdf/who_lies_a_largescale_reanalysis_linking_basic_personality_traits_to_unethical_decision_making.pdf (24 Nov 2026).
- Bribe-taking (lab task, N = 197). Higher HH, fewer bribes taken (Spearman -.15, borderline) and lower earnings (-.25). Bribe size mattered too. Other HEXACO traits did not predict. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6139364 (24 Nov 2026). Small student sample, so bribe-taking evidence is thin.
- Loyalty versus betrayal (covering up another person's lie for pay). Thielmann, Boehm and Hilbig 2021, N = 288. High HH was linked to lower odds of covering a lie (OR 0.48), but not to honest confirmation. Lying to protect others was "remarkably high" despite this. Source: https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3365192_1/component/file_3365193/1948550620905218.pdf (24 Nov 2026). Note this is a student game, not a real loyalty test.
- Review claim: HH maps onto "exploitation" outcomes (426 meta-analyses reviewed). Source: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691619895036/ (abstract only, 24 Nov 2026).
- Not found: a Zettler et al. meta-analysis number. The Pletzer and reanalysis figures above stand in for it.

### A3. Other traits
- Aggression, grudges, forgiveness. Meta-analysis (Fehr et al. 2010): Big Five Agreeableness with forgiveness r = .22, Neuroticism about -.24 (sign inferred, the text lost it). Source: https://ggsc.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Fehr_et_al_2010_The_road_to_forgiveness-_A_meta-analytic_synthesis_of_its_situational_and_dispositional_correlates.pdf (24 Nov 2026). HEXACO Agreeableness is built to hold forgiveness versus retaliation (Hilbig et al. 2016, title and journal confirmed at https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4NbMaXe4, 24 Nov 2026). I could not read their numbers. The Frontiers paper I fetched (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01137/pdf) is a different study: it found no general negative reciprocity effect in money games, and an Agreeableness effect that did not replicate. So treat "low Agreeableness means vengeful" as plausible but weakly evidenced in games. Low HH adds exploitation, not just anger (unverified for revenge).
- Workplace deviance: low Agreeableness (-.19) and low Emotionality (-.14, so fearful people deviate less) per Pletzer, link above.
- Risk taking and obedience to authority. I searched but did not read a usable source. Working hypotheses, all unverified: low Emotionality (low fearfulness) and high Extraversion go with more risk taking; Conscientiousness predicts rule-following and civic duty; Pletzer's -.40 for Conscientiousness with deviance supports the rule-following part only. Do not cite numbers for these.
- Fearfulness: HEXACO Emotionality, and in Pletzer it is protective against deviance (-.14).

### A4. Gossip
- Robbins and Karan (467 people, five studies, recorded daily speech, 4,003 gossip instances): gossip is about 14% of conversation, about 52 minutes a day. Almost three-quarters is neutral. Negative 604 instances, positive 376. Extraverts gossip far more, in every type. Women gossip more only in neutral gossip. Younger people gossip more negatively, but age does not change total gossip. Poorer or less educated people do not gossip more. Source: https://webarchive.ucr.edu/news.ucr.edu/articles/2019/05/03/uc-riverside-study-busts-myths-about-gossip.html (university press release, 24 Nov 2026). Original paper: Social Psychological and Personality Science 2020, not read; correlations not obtained. The 14% figure is repeated at https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10225341.
- Dunbar, Marriott and Duncan 1997 (about two-thirds of conversation is social topics): I located the paper (https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/bf02912493) and a 2024 revisit (https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/61BF5C91583F04053BE9471ED96FAE1D/core-reader) but did not read either. Figure unverified.

## Part B. Criminology of reporting and silence

### B1. Who reports, and why not
- Strongest predictor is seriousness, which is why most unreported crime is "not serious enough". I did not reach a 1988 or 1992 BCS table. Best dated figures I read:
  - BCS 1998, reasons for not reporting (multiple answers): not serious enough 44%; police could not do much 33%; police would not be interested 22%; deal with it themselves 11%; inconvenience 4%; fear of reprisals 4%. Reporting rates: car theft 97%, burglary 85%, wounding 45%, theft from person 35%, car vandalism 26%. Source: https://www.open.edu/openlearn/society-politics-law/sociology/the-meaning-crime/content-section-3.3 (24 Nov 2026). This is 1998, later than the game's window, but the pattern is stable. Treat as indicative for 1990.
  - BCS 1992: fear of reprisals cited for 2% of unreported incidents, 9% for assaults. Source: Maynard 1994 https://popcenter.asu.edu/sites/default/files/problems/witness_intimidation/PDFs/Maynard_FullReport_1994.pdf (24 Nov 2026).
- Victim versus witness is a large gap. In five high-crime estates, victims reported 70% of what happened to them, witnesses only 29%. Fear of intimidation kept 6% of unreported victim incidents unreported, but 22% of unreported witness incidents. Same Maynard source.

### B2. Intimidation after reporting (Britain)
- Maynard 1994 (Home Office Police Research Group paper 55), five high-crime estates, 1,077 respondents: later intimidation followed 13% of victim reports and 9% of witness reports. The author calls it an upper estimate for high-crime areas. 53 interviews: property damage (23 people), verbal abuse and threats (16), physical attack (9). Intimidation often started soon after police contact, such as a police visit or a name broadcast. Women were more likely to be afraid. Same URL as above.
- Elliott 1998, BCS 1994 questions: 17% of victims versus 4% of other witnesses reported intimidation (victim figure probably inflated). At least 6% of reported crimes were followed by victim intimidation. Verbal abuse was the commonest form, then threats. 19% of female victims versus 14% of male. The original offender was usually responsible. Victims who reported were more intimidated (20%) than those who did not (14%). Source: https://popcenter.asu.edu/sites/default/files/problems/witness_intimidation/PDFs/Elliot_1998.pdf (first 100,000 characters only, 24 Nov 2026).
- Tarling et al. 2000 (BCS 1998 analysis, via abstract): intimidation followed 8% of victim incidents (15% when victim knew offender); 26% of women victims of violence were intimidated, often domestic; the offender was responsible in 85% of cases; about three-quarters verbal; only 8% said the aim was to stop evidence. Among witnesses (about a third had seen vandalism, car crime or a serious fight in five years), 8% were intimidated. Source: https://ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/victim-and-witness-intimidation-key-findings-british-crime-survey (abstract, 24 Nov 2026).
- Use for the game: real intimidation is common enough to matter but is mostly verbal, from someone the victim already knows, and often domestic. Organised violent reprisal is the exception, concentrated on high-crime estates.

### B3. Community, "no grassing", trust, collective efficacy
- Sampson, Raudenbush and Earls 1997: 8,782 residents, 343 Chicago neighbourhoods. Collective efficacy (cohesion plus willingness to intervene for the common good) was linked to lower violence, controlling for individual traits and earlier violence, and largely explained the link from disadvantage and residential instability. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9252316/ (24 Nov 2026). American, not British.
- Britain, recent ethnography on two southern English estates (fieldwork 2009 onward): informing was sanctioned (a resident was hissed at as a "grass snake" for talking to police); trust in police was low and uneven; some residents said they would act themselves if the law failed. Fear of retaliation for informing was not explicitly discussed. Source: https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/141736/5/PoLAR%20-%202021%20-%20Davey%20-%20Everyday%20Authoritarianism%20Class%20and%20Coercion%20on%20Housing%20Estates%20in%20Neoliberal%20Britain-1.pdf (24 Nov 2026). Modern, so a loose guide to 1990. I found no 1980s to 1990s source on port communities or "no grassing" norms. Unverified for 1990.

### B4. Bystanders
- Philpot et al. (American Psychologist): 219 CCTV clips of public aggression (Netherlands 63, South Africa 61, UK 95). At least one bystander intervened in 90.9% of clips, with 3.76 interveners on average. No difference by country. Each extra bystander raised the odds of help by 1.1 (OR 1.10), the opposite of a classic bystander effect. Only these two predictors were tested; gender and personality were not. Inner-city sites only, no sound. Source: https://core.ac.uk/download/211246875.pdf (accepted manuscript, 24 Nov 2026).
- Darley and Latane 1968 and the bystander effect: classic finding that helping drops as perceived group size grows. I only reached a secondary source: Latane and Nida 1981 reviewed 56 studies, lone people helped more in 48 of 56 comparisons, about 75% alone versus under 53% in groups. Source: https://forrt.org/open-social-psychology/chapter13.html (24 Nov 2026). Original percentages unverified.
- Taken together: people help when the danger is plain and others are around; they stay out of quiet, ambiguous, or later-reporting situations.

### B5. Gossip spread
- Banerjee et al. field experiments (India, roughly 1,000-person villages): seeding information with people the village names as "good at spreading gossip" beat random or elder seeds. Karnataka: 11.7 calls per village versus 8.1 random and 6.9 elders. Haryana: a similar gain in a health campaign. Gossip nominees are those with high diffusion centrality. Seeds chosen for trust did no better than random. Source: https://economics.mit.edu/research/publications/using-gossips-spread-information-theory-and-evidence-two-randomized-0 (abstract page, 24 Nov 2026). Limits: pure information, not trust-dependent claims, and small communities.
- Speed in hours or days, and strong versus weak ties: not obtained. Unverified. Design hint only: spread runs through a few well-connected sociable people (Extraversion, Robbins and Karan above), and unlike a trusted-person channel.
