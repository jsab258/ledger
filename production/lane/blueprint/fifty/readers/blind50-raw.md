# BLIND-50 (outside list, written without the blueprint)

1. If you rob the grocer, she remembers your coat a week later and goes quiet when you walk in wearing it. [witness]
2. Change your coat and cap after a job and the same witness hesitates, then says "it might have been him" instead of pointing straight at you. [witness]
3. A witness who only saw you from behind a steamed-up bus shelter describes your height and your walk, but not your face. [witness]
4. Do something at night under a broken street lamp and nobody can describe you at all; do it under the chip shop's strip light and three people can. [witness]
5. Witnesses get details wrong: one tells the police your car was blue when it was grey, because it was raining and dark. [witness]
6. A woman who saw you threaten someone crosses the road when she sees you coming, days later, with no prompt on screen to explain it. [witness]
7. Tell a story at the launderette and watch it travel: by the next morning the newsagent is repeating it, slightly bigger. [gossip]
8. Gossip changes as it spreads: "he shoved a man" becomes "he put a man in hospital" two streets over. [gossip]
9. Two old men on a bench talk about something you did yesterday, by name, and stop talking when you walk past. [gossip]
10. A rumour you start on purpose about a rival reaches his own men before it reaches the police. [gossip]
11. Gossip has a geography: what is common knowledge on the dock road can still be news at the far end of the high street. [gossip]
12. The police do not know what you did; they only know what someone told them, and they knock on the door of whoever the witness described. [police]
13. A constable on foot stops you because your jacket matches a description on his radio, then lets you go because your face does not. [police]
14. Detectives go door to door after a serious crime, and you can watch them work down the terrace asking the same questions. [police]
15. If nobody saw it and nobody talks, the police simply never come, and the case stays open in the local paper. [police]
16. A frightened witness can be persuaded not to talk, and the police case visibly stalls; a brave one cannot. [police]
17. The street changes by the hour: dockers at dawn, shop shutters at nine, the dead quiet of mid-afternoon, the late taxi queue at night. [crowd]
18. Every shopkeeper has a routine: the baker arrives at five, the newsagent sorts bundles on the step, the café owner stacks chairs at six. [crowd]
19. Follow any passer-by and they actually go somewhere: home, work, the bus stop, a friend's door. [crowd]
20. People shelter in doorways when it rains and come back out when it stops, and the crowd thins on a wet evening. [crowd]
21. Your minicab radio crackles with real jobs: a pick-up at the hospital, a fare to the station, an old lady's weekly shop. [trade]
22. Drivers remember fares, and a driver who took a man home late one night can later tell you exactly where he lives. [trade]
23. Undercut a rival cab firm and their drivers start parking outside your office, staring, and their dispatcher stops answering your calls. [trade]
24. Your cab office's reputation shows in the phone: a good week and it rings constantly; after a scandal, it goes silent. [trade]
25. Customers pay in loose change counted out on the counter, including the brand new small five pence pieces and the old big ones side by side. [money]
26. Shopkeepers keep a tick book, and if you owe the corner shop too long, she stops serving you until you settle. [money]
27. A shopkeeper you protect gives you a little extra; one you squeeze raises his prices for everyone else to cover it. [money]
28. Prices are on paper tickets and sticky labels, rung up on a clattering till that prints a narrow paper receipt. [money]
29. Punches leave marks that last: a bruised cheek goes purple, then yellow, over several in-game days. [body]
30. People react to your injuries: the newsagent asks what happened to your eye, and a stranger avoids your gaze. [body]
31. Hurt your hand in a fight and you visibly favour it, and it slows the next fight. [body]
32. Fights are clumsy and short; people slip on wet cobbles, grab coats, and stop when someone shouts that the police are coming. [body]
33. Rain soaks your coat darker, and it stays damp-looking for a while after you go indoors. [weather]
34. October light: low gold sun at four o'clock, dark by six, sodium street lamps turning everything orange. [weather]
35. Sea fog rolls in off the docks and witnesses genuinely see less, so crimes in the fog get vaguer descriptions. [weather]
36. Puddles reflect neon from the chip shop sign and ripple when a bus goes through them. [weather]
37. Phone boxes take ten pence and twenty pence coins, the pips go when your money runs out, and some boxes only take phonecards. [period]
38. The local evening paper reports your crimes, sometimes days late and sometimes wrong, and people read it on the bus. [period]
39. Radios in shops and cabs play period-style pop and news bulletins that mention the poll tax and the weather for the shipping areas. [period]
40. Teletext pages flicker on television screens in shop windows, showing news and the weather. [period]
41. Posters on walls protest the poll tax, and they get torn, rained on and pasted over as the weeks go by. [period]
42. At the docks, cranes, containers and ships come and go on a real schedule, and a ship that docked on Monday is gone by Thursday. [docks]
43. Dockers notice who should not be on the quay, and a stranger near the containers gets asked who he is. [docks]
44. Gulls scatter when you run, settle again when you stop, and crowd around the fish quay in the morning. [docks]
45. Stand at the head of a room and people defer to you; stand at the back and nobody notices you came in. [position]
46. Your clothes say who you are: a good suit gets you served first, a donkey jacket lets you pass unnoticed on the docks. [position]
47. As your reputation grows, people start calling you by name, holding doors, and some stop meeting your eye. [position]
48. A man you humiliated in front of his friends turns up weeks later with friends of his own. [consequence]
49. Close a shop through pressure and it stays boarded up, and the neighbours remember who did it. [consequence]
50. Cigarette ends pile up in a doorway where the same man smokes every night, and they are gone after the street sweeper passes. [physical]
