# Design draft: property and premises

28 November 2026. Job 6, area 4 of 8. A first full draft for the property pass. Binding order: canon.md, Jafar's rulings, the blueprint. Sources: DEPTH-RESEARCH-PROPERTY-2026-11-28.md (job 3), the implication drill (job 2, area B3), the paper chaos test (job 4), the 1990 price book (job 5), the economy and docks drafts (areas 1 and 3) and the blueprint.

## (a) What exists and what is missing

What the blueprint has:
- Fronts: a share of a real business (the launderette, the caff, the empty unit once let), got by money, a favour or pressure; it washes through its till, hides men and things, has a named man with a temper; the fold rule for squeezed owners; protection at £5 to £15 a shop (2.17, EC-06).
- Frank's own rent: £18 every Friday to the landlord's agent; arrears lose the flat above, never the office (RT-16, 2.7.4).
- The flat above: Frank sleeps there and the game saves; letters on the mat, a tin under the floorboards; "named only" (4.3.2).
- His decision 16: any building can change hands, with rent as weekly takings (G20). Fire as one witnessed deed, with "insurers and police following the reading" (G18). The licence hearing for the cab firm (RT2-18).

What the research, the drill and the chaos test found missing:
- How any building or share changes hands: no seller, price or go-between (F04; chaos G05, G07, P12 failed Q2).
- Insurance as people: no insurer, adjuster, policy, claim or payment anywhere (F03; chaos G07).
- One debtor rule for tenants (F09; drafted in the economy draft as E10 and applied here).
- A front's week as the player sees it; trouble at a front as a choice; the council as a power; rates and the community charge; what the flat above is for (research P4 to P8).

## (b) The rules

### P1. Every building has an owner, and most have a tenant (law 2, things)
1. Every building in the Hook is a thing with a named owner (a landlord, a widow, the council, a company in the city that acts through an agent) and, if let, a named tenant with a lease or a rent book.
2. Ownership is a fact held in papers in places: the deeds at the owner's solicitor, the lease in a drawer, the occupier's name on the council's rating list (open to anyone at the council offices; a design choice). A fact in papers can be learned by whoever reads them (law 1).
3. Public buildings (the police station, the council offices, the chapel, the hospital, the library) are never for sale: asked, the owner refuses in character ("It's the council's, and the council isn't selling to you").

### P2. Four ways premises change hands (law 4, exchange)
Each is an act with a named seller and a go-between, and each takes game days:

| Way | From whom | Price | Go-between | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Take a new lease of an empty unit | its landlord | the weekly rent, a deposit of a quarter's rent, and a reference (a bank book in good order or a known name who speaks for him) | the landlord's agent | a week |
| Take over a sitting tenant's lease | the tenant | a premium the tenant names by need and greed | the landlord, whose written consent is needed and may not be unreasonably withheld; he must answer in reasonable time and give reasons (Landlord and Tenant Act 1988) | a week to two |
| Buy a building outright | its owner | the yearly rent times eight (a balance figure), haggled (economy draft, E3) | both sides' solicitors | four weeks |
| Take a share in a business | its owner | his weekly cut times 20 in money, or less money plus a favour or pressure (2.17) | none, or a solicitor if written | a day for a handshake, a week if written |

1. A seller may refuse at any price if attached (an old owner's pride, a widow's late husband's shop), by temper.
2. Each sale tells the town a little: the agent talks at the caff by his temper (2.4.1); the solicitor keeps a confidence unless pressed by a warrant (area 6). Each sale on the same parade raises the next owner's asking price by a tenth once he hears of it (the town adapts, G27).
3. Payment must come from somewhere that can bear it: book money (2.7.1) or washed money. A purchase of more than £1,000 in notes is a fact the solicitor remembers and the Act III inspector can ask about. Balance figure.
4. A handshake share has no paper: the owner can deny it, and it gives no claim on his insurance (P6). A written share is a fact in a drawer that anyone who reads it learns.

### P3. Use and the council (law 3, people)
1. Each unit has a use: a shop, a café, an office. A new business of the same use opens at once; a change of use needs the council's planning permission, decided by a named planning officer in four game weeks (balance figure). A café needs permission in a former shop; a launderette or a cab office fits no class and always needs it (Use Classes Order 1987, research).
2. A café open between 22:00 and 05:00 needs the council's late-night licence (1969 Act, research). The cab firm's licence stays as RT2-18.
3. The council's officers are people with a want and a fear: the planning officer, the licensing officer (Agar, RT2-18), an environmental health officer for food, and the rating officer. Each can refuse, inspect or close; each can be argued with, given what he needs, or squared at his law (bribing a council officer is a deed of corruption with a heavy weight in the town's talk).
4. Anyone can write to the council about a business: a rival, a neighbour, the newsagent. A complaint brings an inspection within two game weeks. Balance figure.

### P4. Rates and the community charge (law 4)
1. A business pays rates: its rateable value (set at 1988 rents) times 34.8p in the pound a year, shown as a yearly demand on the mat and paid in ten monthly instalments (research; instalments a design choice). The firm's own rates are inside its existing costs (RT-16 stands).
2. Every adult pays the community charge to the council; in the Hook it is £384 a year (as Great Grimsby, research). Frank's comes as a demand in ten monthly instalments of £38.40, the first due in week 4 so it never presses on the first week (question 2).
3. Here Frank is the debtor. A missed instalment brings a reminder after a fortnight, a summons to the magistrates after six weeks (a soft-edge scene), a liability order after eight, a bailiff's letter after ten. Paying ends it at any step, as RT-16 does. Balance figures. It never takes the office or the cars.

### P5. A front's week (law 4, law 3)
1. A front's week moves for causes, as any shop's (G20, EC-04): payday Fridays, weather, the other shops, a squeeze, a closure nearby, trouble.
2. Each week its man brings Frank's cut on its day in an envelope with the till roll. The cut is his share of what the till took after wages, rates and stock, short if the man is (2.17).
3. Its believable takings set what it can wash (economy draft, E6.2): a launderette about £180 a week, the caff about £300 (balance figures).
4. Trouble: a front has at most one matter a week (a broken window, a man asking for money, an inspector's letter, a hand off sick, stock gone missing), brought to Frank by its man. Frank deals with it (time, a talk, money) or lets it go, and a matter let go halves that week's cut and, the second week running, costs the man's regard. A cost that is a choice, as research P5; never a defence set-piece (crew draft, R9).

### P6. Fire and insurers are people (law 2, law 3; fix F03)
1. A business has a policy: a named insurer, a sum insured, conditions (locks, declared stock, no change of use without telling), and an owner who pays the premium as part of his own costs.
2. A loss over £500 (fire, burglary, a loss from cargo, docks draft D4) brings a named loss adjuster from the city within a week. He reads, in this order: the brigade's reading of the seat (G18), the owner's purse and debts, whether the cover was raised lately, who was where, and what neighbours say.
3. He pays in four weeks, delays eight weeks while he asks questions, or refuses. Insurers mostly settled suspected fraud between themselves and the insured, privately (research), so he goes to the police only when the brigade read the fire as deliberate and he finds a motive. Balance figures for the weeks.
4. The money goes to the policyholder only. Frank's share comes through the owner by their arrangement; a handshake share gives him nothing on paper (P2.4).
5. A fire is a deed by G18 whatever its purpose; a fire in a building where someone sleeps is among the heaviest deeds the town can talk about.

### P7. Frank as landlord (law 4; with E10 for F09)
1. Rent from a tenant is book money, paid on its day; the tenant is a person with a purse, a temper and a week (E12).
2. A tenant who falls behind follows the one debtor rule (economy draft, E10): pays from his purse, begs or refuses by regard, nerve and grudge.
3. Frank's answers, each with its cost in talk: wait; ask in person; a letter (courting draft's letter rule, C7, covers any letter); a notice to quit of four weeks, then the county court through the solicitor (a soft-edge scene, a fee, a date three weeks on). Throwing a tenant out by force or changing his locks is a deed the street hears and a crime.
4. A tenant asks for repairs; a repair not done in two weeks costs his regard and the street's (balance figures). Repairs are done by people for money and a day, as for cars (1.2.9).

### P8. The flat above is Frank's home (law 2, law 3)
1. The flat is where Frank sleeps (any bed he has the right to will do; the save is the same) and where his own letters land on the mat: rent, rates and charge demands, her letters (courting draft), a summons, a rival's note.
2. It holds things: the tin under the floorboards and what area 7 allows. A police search under a warrant reaches it (area 6); a burglar finds what he finds (area 7).
3. It is his rather than Mickey's by what is in it: Mickey's things go as the rungs rise (2.12.5), Frank's take their place, and the rent book carries Frank's name from the first Friday he pays himself.
4. She may visit for tea in the afternoon or the evening and leaves before the night (courting draft; affection only). A rival can put a brick through its window, a deed that tells the street.
5. Losing it under RT-16 sends him to sleep in the office; owning another flat gives him another bed.

## (d) The player's hands and eyes

- In the world: an agent's board in the empty unit's window; the agent at the caff; the solicitor's office in the Exchange (soft edge); the landlord at his door; the council counter; demands on the mat; the envelope and till roll on the front's day; the adjuster with his clipboard in the burnt shop.
- In talk: "Is it for sale?", "What would you take for a share?", "I'll want it in writing", "The rent's late", suggested lines for each (ruling 4).
- In the Ledger (area 8): one page per holding (building, lease, share or front) written as its papers: the lease or deeds, the rent or cut by week as till rolls and envelopes, the rates and charge demands, the matter of the week, and the man who runs it. No slider and no profit graph.

## (e) Edges

- Buying the whole parade: every owner is a person; prices rise a tenth per sale heard of; some will not sell; the town talks and Ellis hears of a cab man buying shops (law 1).
- Buying a public building: refused in character (P1.3).
- Letting a flat to a crewman rent-free: allowed; a gift with the debt and regard rules, not a special case.
- Burning a rival's front: a deed under G18 and P6; the owner's insurer pays him if the claim is honest, so the rival is set back weeks, not ruined; rival hits come from the ladder (2.10.1).
- Opening a business the town does not have (a launderette in the empty unit): allowed through planning (P3.1); it draws a rival newcomer in time only if it prospers (ruling 3).
- No mortgage, no stock market, no property values that move by themselves: prices come from rent and people.

## (h) Questions only Jafar can decide

1. When may Frank buy a building outright?
   - A. At any time, at a price that puts it out of reach in Act I (recommended: one rule, and the early path is a lease, as P12 shows).
   - B. Only after the ending (as the economy draft lists it).
   - C. Never; leases and shares only.
2. Should Frank's community charge be shown?
   - A. Yes, as a demand of £38.40 a month from week 4 with a slow ladder that never takes the office (recommended: true to 1990 and a small choice of what to pay first).
   - B. No; it is assumed paid.
   - C. Folded into the £18 rent.
3. May Frank burn a front for its insurance?
   - A. Yes, with the adjuster as the danger, as P6 (recommended: a gamble read by a person, never a menu).
   - B. Fire may happen, but no policy ever pays where Frank has a hand in it.
   - C. No insurance money anywhere in the game.

Decided here without asking: the four ways premises change hands follow the 1988 Act and the research; the Hook's charge is taken from Great Grimsby, the nearest port in size; years' purchase, share price and takings are balance figures matched to the economy draft; P7 reuses E10 rather than a new tenant rule.
