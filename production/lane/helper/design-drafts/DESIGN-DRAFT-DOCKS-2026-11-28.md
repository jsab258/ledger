Sources: DEPTH-RESEARCH-DOCKS-2026-11-28.md (job 3), the implication drill (job 2, area C1), the paper chaos test (job 4), the economy draft (area 1) and the blueprint.

## (a) What exists and what is missing

What the blueprint has:
- The dock road, the gate and its yard mouth, Customs and the Harbour Board; from rung 3 a tax on Frank's drivers, a man posted, loads seen; the Harbour Board's counter with an account and a clerk who sells shipment times; a Customs officer "who never reports, by trade"; a bonded store for tobacco and boxed goods (never drink) as a place of risk; a tea stall at the gate from 22:00 to 06:00 (4.2.3).
- Smuggled tobacco: £100 a case off the boat, £160 sold on; two cases a week pass, four with men at the gate (rung 4); more and Customs take the week's load and Ellis hears (EC-07).
- The dock gun seller, a deckhand at the fish dock on Tuesdays and Fridays (RT2-14); the cold-store contract and the Harbour Board's late men as accounts (2.15.2); the honest dock shift (decision 17); the water's edge (4.2.4).
- Customs officer's law is 0.15 or under (2.5.2): he does not go to the police.

What the research, the drill and the chaos test found missing:
- The hands of smuggling: the meet, carrying a case, where it is kept, who buys (drill C1; research D1, D2; chaos G03 failed Q2 on this).
- A cargo with an owner, a tally, a seal and an insurer, and who learns of a loss and when (research D3, D4).
- Customs as people and as a charging body; the bonded store's keeper (research D5 to D7).
- The Harbour Board and the dockers as powers with a want and a fear; laid-off dockers as a recruiting pool (research D8, D9).
- A learner for the repeated gate run (F02; drafted in the economy draft, rule E7, and given hands here).

## (b) The rules

### D1. Goods have bulk, and bulk fills the hands (law 2, things)
1. Every carried good has a bulk: pocket (a packet, a watch), coat (a carton, a gun), one hand (a holdall, a radio), two hands (a case, a television). Two hands means no fighting, no running, a walk at the slow pace (1.1.4), and a mark any watcher catches (2.2.5).
2. A case of cigarettes is 200 packets (20 cartons) and fills two hands. A car boot holds four cases; the office storage and other places hold what area 7 sets. A case can be set down, handed to a man, or left, and whoever finds it holds it (G13).
3. A case carries its owner while it is in the trade's hands (the boat's man until paid, then Frank) and a hot mark only if it was stolen; smuggled goods carry no owner's claim but are seizable by Customs anywhere until sold on.

### D2. The meet (law 3, law 4)
1. Smuggled goods come ashore through a named person: a crewman of a named coaster that berths on a known day (as the gun seller does, RT2-14). He buys abroad where a packet costs about 110p against 180p here (research) and brings what he has on that night, by the tide and the dark, to a fixed place (the fish dock, his berth).
2. The hand-over is an act with the seven slots (2.2.1): the tea stall's keeper, a docker on the night shift and the gate's special constable are watchers by their routines.
3. Frank pays cash on the spot; the crewman remembers who paid and what was said. A crewman who is short-changed or threatened takes his trade to a rival next voyage.

### D3. Who buys (law 4)
1. Buyers are named people, each with a weekly limit and a rate, following the buyer rule (economy draft, E4.2): a Thursday market stall (up to 2 cases a week), the caff's back counter (half a case), men in the yards (a case among them), a corner shop of low law (one case). Balance figures.
2. Each buyer is a listener who knows who sold to him (2.7.6). The newsagent who loses trade (EC-04) first knows only that cheap packets are about; he learns names through gossip like anyone else.
3. Each buyer's rate falls a tenth for each week in a row the same goods arrive (economy draft, E7).

### D4. A cargo has an owner, a tally, a seal and an insurer (law 2, law 1)
1. Every load on the dock road, in the bonded store or in a shed has a named owner (the shipper or receiver), a tally list (the count when loaded, research: 1970 practice), a seal where it travels sealed, and an insurer.
2. A loss becomes known at the first check that compares goods with paper: the tally clerk at his next count, the gate's special constable reading the list, the receiver checking seal against tally note on delivery (research D3).
3. Each who learns acts by his place:

| Who learns | When | What he does |
|---|---|---|
| Tally clerk | his next count (the same shift or the next) | tells the shed foreman and the carrier |
| Receiver | on delivery, the next morning or when the lorry arrives | signs "seal broken" or "short"; the carrier is charged |
| Carrier | when charged | asks his drivers and the gate; may suspect his own man |
| Gate special constable | when he reads a list that does not match | stops the vehicle; tells the Harbour Board and the police |
| Insurer | a week, for a loss over £500 | sends a loss adjuster, a named person (area 4, rule P6) |
| Harbour Board | when the receiver or carrier complains | rule D8 |

Balance figures for the times and the £500 line.

### D5. Customs are people (law 3)
1. Customs in the Hook are named officers with routines:
   - the gate officer (law 0.15 or under, 2.5.2): takes money to look away and never goes to the police, but seizes in his own name when a seizure profits him or when he is watched;
   - a rummage crew of three that boards ships on a rota and on signs (a tip, a ship from a known port, a pattern), as the law allows them to board and search any part of a ship in port (CEMA 1979 s.27, research);
   - a senior officer at the Custom House who counts seizures and reads complaints.
2. Each wants and fears by role: the gate officer wants money and a quiet gate and fears the senior officer; the rummage crew want seizures that count; the senior officer wants a clean record and fears outsiders from the regional office, who come after a big seizure or a complaint.
3. The gate officer is the learner for the gate run (economy draft, E7): after six runs on the same day, boat or carrier he watches that day, and the safe allowance falls by one case each four weeks the pattern holds; varying the pattern resets it. Bribing him buys one look-away a week at a price that rises with each case past two. Balance figures.

### D6. A Customs case has its own road (law 3, law 7)
1. A smuggling or duty case is Customs' own: the goods and any vehicle used are seized; the man is interviewed at the Custom House (a soft-edge scene under two real minutes); Customs either offer a penalty in place of court or bring the case themselves (research D6: Customs prosecuted their own cases in 1990).
2. A Customs charge joins the case list read at the ending (2.6.9) and reaches Ellis as talk; there is no court scene for it (decision 15). A seized car is returned only if the charge falls; car two is never lost for more than a day (RT2-06).
3. Paying the penalty ends the case and is a record the Act III inspector reads.

### D7. The bonded store's keeper (law 3)
1. The bonded store is an approved place with conditions; breaching one costs the keeper £100 and repeated breaches his approval (CEMA 1979 s.92, research).
2. Its keeper is a named man who fears losing his approval more than most things: he will hide a small loss by adjusting his count if he fears revocation more than he respects the law, which makes him a lever (a hook, a bribe, a fear) and a witness.
3. A loss from bond is found at the monthly Customs stock check, or at the next delivery out, whichever comes first.

### D8. The Harbour Board is a power (law 3)
1. The Board wants traffic, rent and quiet berths; it fears losing trade to roll-on roll-off ports (research: freight moved to such ports during the 1989 strike), strikes and a name for theft among shippers.
2. It has its own special constables at the gate (Tees had 21, research; the Hook's smaller port has 6, balance figure) and a clerk who sells shipment times (4.2.3), its weak point.
3. Repeated losses (three in a month, balance figure) bring answers by the Board's temper: more specials at night, a new check at the gate, a word to Customs, a firm it trusts favoured with work. Frank's firm, holding the Board's late-men account (2.15.2), is trusted or distrusted by what the Board hears.

### D9. Dockers as a power and a pool (law 3)
1. Since the Dock Labour Scheme ended on 3 July 1989, a third of the Hook's registered dockers have been laid off (Hull 36% by 21 July 1989, research; the share is question 3). The rest work without the old protection.
2. Working dockers want work and pay and fear more lay-offs. They notice cargo moving off-pattern and talk at the gate's tea stall (2.4.1). A docker who loses shifts because the Board cuts work after thefts holds a grudge against the thief once he knows him.
3. Laid-off dockers are the main need-road recruiting pool (2.11.1): men with nerve, ties to the gate and time. Each brings his ties (crew draft, R5).

## (d) The player's hands and eyes

- He meets a man at a berth at night, carries a case in both arms to the cab, opens the boot (seen like any act, GV-01), drives, stores, sells in talk.
- He sees the port's people at their routines: the gate officer in his hut, the rummage crew on a gangway, specials at the barrier, dockers at the tea stall, the clerk at the Board's counter.
- He learns of losses and suspicion in talk ("The Board's put two more on nights"; "Customs boarded the Grete this morning").
- The Ledger keeps the boat's days, the crewman's name, each buyer and his limit as Frank knows them, what is stored where, and any Customs case with its date (area 8).

## (e) Edges

- Bringing in a lorry load: the boat's man brings only what one man can bring ashore unseen; bigger loads need the Board's paper and draw Customs at once.
- Robbing the bonded store every week: the keeper's count, Customs' monthly check and the Board's answers all rise; the store is a place of risk, not a bank (4.2.3).
- Running the gate past the allowance: seizure within three weeks (EC-07's standard), now through a person, the gate officer or the rummage crew.
- Hurting a Customs officer: an officer is a townsperson under one rule set (G16); hurting one is a deed and draws the regional officers.
