# Depth research: PROPERTY (premises, fronts, the office and the flat)

28 November 2026. Job 3, area 3 of 5. All sources read on 28 Nov 2026 unless marked. Content rule: no pubs, drink or betting in anything the game would use, so bar and betting examples below are noted only as how a game works.

## The plain top

What to copy:
- GTA V (story): a business pays a fixed weekly sum; its manager phones every few game days with a job (a delivery, a robbery to chase, an attack). Ignore it and you lose that week's money but keep the building: a cost that is a choice.
- Red Dead Redemption 2: one ledger by one box lists what each member paid in and what each upgrade costs, paid from the shared box. A model for showing a front's week.
- Yakuza 0: shops bought in person and given a manager. Its warning is the screens: unlabelled numbers, five rating systems, owned shops unmarked, five clicks to the business map.
- Scarface and The Godfather: a front comes only after a job for its owner, and rivals hit what you hold (from job 1).

What players hate (critics read on property: 3, plus 1 UX study):
- Repetition once the first area is taken (GamesRadar, Yakuza 0).
- "Not as deep as it sounds" (GamesRadar).
- Menus and maps that hide what you own (the UX study, nine named problems).
- Business management as pouring money into upgrades until shops are "names in a list" (PC Gamer, Empire of Sin, from area 1).

The 1990 facts:
- On 1 April 1990 business rates became one national rate of 34.8p in the pound in England (36.8p in Wales), on new rateable values set at 1988 rents. The 1989-90 average poundage had been 258.3p. Northern towns tended to pay less, and a small shop's average bill fell 2% in real terms. A "small shop" was one rated £500 to £10,000 outside London.
- The community charge for 1990-91 averaged £357 an adult in England. In port towns it was Liverpool £449, Middlesbrough £429, Hartlepool £385, Great Grimsby £384, Newcastle £391, Hull £307 and Sunderland £310.
- A lease with "consent not to be unreasonably withheld" came under the Landlord and Tenant Act 1988. The landlord must answer in reasonable time, give consent unless refusal is reasonable, and give written reasons or conditions, and he bears the burden of proof.
- Planning: a shop is class A1, a café class A3. A coin-op launderette and a taxi office fit no class, so a change of use needs permission.
- A café open between 10pm and 5am needed a late-night refreshment licence from the council (1969 Act as enacted).
- Fire brigades attended 20,800 fires in business premises in 1990, 4,270 of them deliberate or possibly deliberate. A study of 1990-95 found 71 suspected fraudulent arsons worth £38.5 million, settled mostly between insurer and insured.

The design questions are numbered P1 to P10 in part C. They ask:
- Who sells a building, at what price, and through whom?
- What does a share in a front cost?
- How is a front's week shown?
- What list exists of what is stored at the office?
- What is the flat above for?

## C. The drill's gaps as design questions

From the implication drill, area B3, and his decision 16 ("any building can change hands, rent as weekly takings").

P1. Who sells (gap B). When a building changes hands, is the seller always a named person (the owner, a landlord, a widow, the council)? Does an agent or solicitor stand between, who notices and may talk? Should a lease be assigned only with the landlord's written consent, as the 1988 Act makes the rule, so the landlord becomes a person Frank must handle?

P2. At what price (gap B). Should a building's price follow its rent (decision 16), and the rent follow a rateable value set at 1988 rents? Would a weekly rates bill at 34.8p in the pound be shown as a real demand on the mat?

P3. The price of a share in a front (gap C). Is a share bought with money alone, or with money plus a favour or pressure (2.17)? Scarface sells a front only after a job for its owner. Should every share come after a job?

P4. How a front's week is seen (gap C). RDR2 puts every contribution and every cost on one ledger page by one box. Should each front have one page in Frank's Ledger with takings, wages, rates, trouble and his cut, written in 1990 objects (a till roll, a rates demand, an envelope)? Yakuza 0's nine faults are the warning. How many taps from the street to a front's page?

P5. A cost that is a choice. GTA V lets the player ignore the manager's call and lose that week's profit. Should a front's trouble (a broken window, a man asking for money, the council inspector) be a call Frank may ignore at a named weekly loss?

P6. What is stored at the office (gap B, counted under the Ledger holdings gap). Should the office have a written list of what is in the locked drawer, the shed and the tin, seen only by Frank? Who else could see it: the office staff (gap C, office staff seeing stored goods)?

P7. The flat above (only named). Is the flat a place to sleep and save, a store, a place a partner visits (COURTING), or a target? Does it carry its own rent and its own community charge (£307 to £449 a year in port towns)?

P8. Licences and planning as levers. A launderette and a taxi office need planning permission to open. A late café needs a council licence. Should the council be a power whose officers can refuse or close a front, and can they be squared?

P9. Rivals and fronts. Godfather and Godfather II let rivals bomb or retake businesses, and three critics hate repeated defence (area 1). Should a rival's hit on a front come only from his ladder (2.10.1), and be repaired by people for money and a day (RT-06)?

P10. Fire and insurance. About one business fire in five was deliberate in 1990, and insurers settled fraud claims privately. Should a burnt front draw a loss adjuster who doubts the claim, rather than the police? Should Frank be able to burn his own front for the insurance, with the adjuster as the danger?
