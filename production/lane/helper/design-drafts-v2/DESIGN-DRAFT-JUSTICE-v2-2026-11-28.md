# Design draft: justice (version 2)

28 November 2026. Helper job 8, area 6 of 8. Version 2 of DESIGN-DRAFT-JUSTICE-2026-11-28.md, revised from the blind check of 28 November. Same structure and numbering as version 1, with one section added at the end (J10). Binding order: canon.md, Jafar's rulings, the blueprint. Every finding was checked at its source; the change table at the end says what was accepted or rejected. The law below is as it stood around 1990; this is design for a game, not legal advice.

## (a) What exists and what is missing

What the blueprint has:
- Arrest needs a signed statement from someone who knew it was him, or an officer who saw it; a statement at the station at nine the morning after (2.6.3); a signed statement stays on file (WI-P07).
- Custody as hours; property booked at arrest (2.6.4, WI-P03).
- A charge joins Ellis's case and is read at the ending; a lesser charge has no court scene; a killing charge plays the magistrates' morning and remand visits (2.6.9, LH-07, his decision 15).
- Ellis's four stages (2.6.5); everyone who knows is leaned on (LH-04); the inquiry into a death (CS-27); a constable's killing and its roads (LH-01) and the squad (LH-02); 999 from a box (RT2-19); the hospital as a soft-edge scene (WI-P11); typed text that claims an act is talk (RT-15).
- The witness rule and its seriousness scale: damage 0.3, assault 0.5, wounding or robbery 0.7, a killing 1.0 (2.5.2). Officers are townspeople under one rule set, and anyone who treats a wound reports it by law dial and regard (G16).

What was missing or wrong (research, chaos test, blind check):
- Bail with a date; cash and goods at arrest; statements where the witness is; tips and warrants; deeds by talk; hand acts at the soft edge; a person in a seat; wounds; missing persons.
- Theft has no seriousness anywhere, though the deeds by talk rest on it (J-4, J-15, T06).
- Court dates misread (J-8); a fined charge's place unclear (J-5); a special limit on striking during an outing (J-2); a county squad as a new town-wide trigger (J-1, X-10); striking from the driver's seat (BK06); a man believed gone never missed (BK04); no road for a killed Customs officer (BK01).

## (b) The rules

### J1. Police bail: a date, a place, no conditions (law 3, law 5)
1. Bail after arrest is a duty to come back: to the station on a written date while inquiries go on (two to four game weeks, balance figure), or to the magistrates' court on a charge (PACE 1984 s.47, research).
2. The police set no conditions: in 1990 only a court could (Bail Act 1976 s.3(6), research). Anything Frank does to a witness meanwhile is a new deed (WI-P07, LH-04).
3. The station may write to say he need not come ("no further action"); the letter lands on the mat.
4. Failing to answer without good cause is an offence of its own (Bail Act s.6): a warrant is issued, the constable calls at the office the next morning, the charge joins the case, and the street hears that Frank has run.
5. A lesser charge at the magistrates (mends J-8, J-5, J-10). The first hearing falls about two weeks after charge (own knowledge; research gives only that cases took 64 days from first listing to the end in 1990, with 2.5 adjournments of 26 days, and that 22% ended at the first appearance). The date is an Open matter; the morning passes off screen (decision 15: no court scene for a lesser charge) and costs Frank that morning. He chooses his plea by one line, said to the solicitor beforehand or on the court steps that morning:
   - plead guilty: the case ends that morning in a fine set by the deed's seriousness (£50 under 0.3, £100 from 0.3 to under 0.5, £200 at 0.5 or more; balance figures), paid from the pocket or the book on the day; the charge leaves the list read at the ending and stays a conviction the town and the council officer can hold (property P3.3);
   - plead not guilty: the case is adjourned about four weeks at a time, so it is almost always still open at the close, where 2.6.9 reads it.
   An unpaid fine brings a letter from the court; ignored, a warrant as in J1.4. Never prison for a lesser charge.
6. A Customs charge follows the same rule (docks D6.2). A man remanded in custody goes before the magistrates every eight days until his case ends, as LH-07 does for a killing charge.

### J2. What is taken at a search or arrest and what comes back (law 2, things)
1. Cash follows the economy draft's one rule for every police search, with or without an arrest (economy E1.3; mends J-6, E-7): what the fare book explains for a week (last week's washed takings plus £20) stays with him or comes back; the excess is held against a receipt and comes back when no charge stands at the close, or when Frank shows where it came from. (PACE s.19 is sourced; ss. 21, 22 and 54 as applied to a detained man are own knowledge.)
2. Lawful things not needed as evidence come back on release; a photograph will do for many (PACE s.22, research). A weapon is kept. Frank's Ledger is listed and handed back: it is his notes and proves nothing by itself (things T7.5, Ledger L6.3).
3. Stolen goods are kept until the owner is traced. The owner gets them back through the police or a magistrates' order (Police (Property) Act 1897, research), and in getting them he learns where they were found. That is a fact he holds, read by his temper (law 1).
4. A Customs seizure is Customs' own road (docks D6).

### J3. A statement is taken where the witness is (law 3)
1. The default stays: the station at nine the morning after the call (2.6.3).
2. A witness who cannot come (in hospital, laid up, too frightened to leave the house) is visited by the officer the report brings, on his next morning round, and signs at the bed or the door.
3. A witness in hospital signs once she can talk: the next day for a serious hurt, at once for a light one. Balance figures.
4. Hurting a witness to stop her is a deed of its own and adds to the case (WI-P07); it delays her statement by a day at most.

### J4. Tips and warrants (law 1, knowing)
1. A tip (a 999 call from a box, a word to the beat constable, a letter, courting C7) is a claim weighed by G03 against what the station holds. Pad players make a tip about a named person and deed with the Ledger's claim builder (Ledger L5.4, X-7).
2. Fit decides the answer:

| Fit | Answer |
|---|---|
| None: names no one the station knows of, or a deed it has no sign of | noted; Ellis hears of it at her next look at the book |
| Some: names a person or place the station already wonders about | the beat constable calls or asks round the next day |
| Strong: fits a reported deed and names a locked place | a magistrate's warrant the next day (PACE s.8) and a search that day |
| A deed in progress at a spot | a constable to the spot that night (RT2-19) |

3. A tip that proves false lowers the next tip from the same box or voice one step; two false tips from one box and the station stops sending anyone on its word. Balance figures.
4. Anyone acted against on a tip or a told fact (a man searched, a rival whose boat was boarded) is told nothing of the source, and his suspicion falls on whoever his own facts point to (law 1, G01), which may be Frank or someone else.

### J5. Deeds done by talk, and theft (law 3, people)
1. G19's deed table gains three rows:

| Deed | 1990 offence | Seriousness | Street reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Getting money or goods by a lie | obtaining property by deception (Theft Act 1968 s.15) | theft of the same value (J5.3) | "a con man" |
| Posing as a police officer, in talk or in uniform | impersonating a constable (Police Act 1964 s.52) | 0.3, and the station takes it as against itself, so Ellis's interest rises one stage | "pretending to be the law" |
| A false promise for money | obtaining by deception | theft of the sum (J5.3) | "took his money and never did it" |

2. The victim holds the deed once he learns the truth (G03: a lie found out): the real police call, the promised thing never comes, a neighbour says. He reports it by 2.5.2's rule at the seriousness above.
3. Theft has a seriousness, by value, in 2.5.2's scale (mends J-4, J-15, T06): under £20, 0.15; £20 to £200, 0.25; over £200, 0.35 (always under assault at 0.5). A lift, a car taken and a deception all read from this row. Balance figures.
4. Offence names are own knowledge, to be checked against the 1990 texts before the build.

### J6. Hand acts at the soft edge (law 8)
1. Soft-edge scenes allow hand acts as acts (a key or a pad button, as on the street), never typed; typed text that claims an act stays talk (RT-15).

| Scene | Hand acts allowed |
|---|---|
| A hospital visit (WI-P11), a caff table, a front room, a call on a friend, a courting outing (courting C4) | strike, grab, leave |
| The Custom House interview (docks D6), the station interview (2.6.11) | none; leave only when let go |
| The solicitor, the council counter, the magistrates (LH-07) | leave |

2. At the soft edge a hand act resolves as one act by the body rule (G19), shown as a short cut and a line, not as a walked fight (mends J-9: no fight space or animation set per scene).
3. A deed done in a scene is seen by everyone the scene holds, at face distance. A companion (a woman he is walking out with, a crewman) reads it by her or his temper (courting C9.1). There is no special limit for any kind of scene (mends J-2, BR07).

### J7. A person in a seat (law 8)
1. One rule for anyone sitting (a cab seat, a bench, a bed, a chair): Frank can grab, pull out and strike at short reach. The seated person has a chance to get up or out first, by nerve and how near the door is; a person in a bed has none.
2. A passenger driven off his way first asks, then protests, then tries the door when the car stops or slows under about 10 mph, by nerve. At speed he stays in and remembers, and shouts to the first person he sees. Balance figures.
3. Frank at the wheel: a strike or grab input while driving first brings the car to a stop at the kerb, then the act happens; nothing is refused on screen (1.6.15) and nothing is struck at speed (mends BK06, J-12; canon: a car hurts and never kills).
4. Pulling a passenger out of a cab is a deed by G19 like pulling a driver out (1.2.10).

### J8. Wounds and who may report them (law 1, law 3)
1. One rule (G16; mends J-3): anyone who treats a wound reports it by his law dial, his regard for the patient and the wound's seriousness (2.5.2's scale; a gunshot wound reads at 1.0, so nearly every treater reports it). In 1990 no rule made a doctor report a stab wound or a beating (research: knife wounds from 2009). That before 2009 only gunshot wounds carried a duty is in the research, but its start is not dated, so a 1990 duty is not confirmed (mends J-11).
2. The casualty doctor is also more likely to tell when the patient names a person or the police are already asking about that night. Ambulance crew and nurses are witnesses of what they saw and heard.
3. Hospital costs days, never money (ruling 6). A back-street patch-up (a named person who once nursed) costs a favour or a few pounds and the same days; she knows, and can tell.

### J9. A missing person is missed (law 1, law 3, law 5)
1. A person who vanishes is missed by those whose routines cross his: a landlady or family after a day or two, workmates after three days, a man alone after a week. The first to miss him reports him. Balance figures.
2. A person believed to have left town is expected somewhere (mends BK04, J-14, BF07): a letter or call that should come (G21) does not; after about two weeks his kin or the people he was going to ring the station, and the inquiry runs from his last known sighting or fare. Balance figure.
3. The police read his ties and last steps: who last spoke to him, whom he owed, who had threatened him, where he was last seen, the last fare or cab that took him (G13, LH-04). Those people are visited. Where those facts point at the dock (last seen on the quay, a cab on the quay road at night), police divers search that berth, and a body there is found that day (things T5.2, BF23).
4. Each further disappearance raises Ellis's interest one stage and brings CID visits to the missing men's ties; never the county squad (X-10, settled: no new town-wide events).
5. Whether and when a body comes back otherwise is things T5.

### J10. Officers on duty (law 3)
1. The killing of a Customs officer on duty takes LH-01's road for who knows, the manhunt, arrest and remand (LH-07), exactly as a constable's killing does, but without the squad: in its place Ellis's interest goes to its top stage and the regional Customs office sends officers who visit the dead man's ties and read the Custom House daybook (docks D5.4). This keeps his ruling that no new trigger brings a town-wide event (mends BK01, J-13; BF05 in part).
2. Any other officer (a council officer, a Harbour Board special) is a townsperson under G16; his killing is a killing like any other (CS-27).

## (d) The player's hands and eyes

- In the world: the custody sergeant's counter with his property on it; a bail sheet with the date; letters on the mat ("no further action", a court date, a fine); the constable at the office door; the doctor in casualty; the landlady asking after her lodger; divers' boat at a berth.
- In talk: suggested lines for every act above, and the claim builder for tips (ruling 4).
- In the Ledger: open matters as papers, each with its date; what Frank knows of each witness's state.

## (e) Edges

- Hiding in the flat on the bail date: J1.4.
- Pleading guilty to every lesser charge before the close: each is a fine and a conviction the town holds (J1.5).
- Tipping off the police about everyone, every night: J4.3 wears the box out.
- Putting every witness in hospital: each is a deed and a day's delay at most (J3).
- Striking a passenger at speed: the car stops first (J7.3).
- Killing a man as he leaves on the last ferry: J9.2; he is expected somewhere.
