## (b) The rules

### P1. Every building has an owner, and most have a tenant (law 2, things)
1. Every building in the Hook is a thing with a named owner (a landlord, a widow, a company in the city acting through an agent) and, if let, a named tenant with a lease or a rent book.
2. Ownership is a fact held in papers in places: the deeds at the owner's solicitor, the lease in a drawer, the occupier's name on the council's rating list (read at the council counter in the Exchange, at the soft edge). A fact in papers can be learned by whoever reads them (law 1).
3. A public body never sells: asked, its officer says so in character (mends P-3). The council's own premises and the bank are among them (WI-P12).

### P2. Two ways to hold premises in the story: a lease and a share (law 4, exchange)
Each is an act with a named person on the other side and takes game days:

| Way | From whom | Price | Through | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A lease | the landlord, for empty premises; or a sitting tenant, who hands over his lease | the weekly rent (rates included), a deposit of four weeks' rent, and a reference (a bank book in good order or a known name who speaks for him); a sitting tenant also names a premium by need and greed | the landlord's agent or the landlord himself (P2.2); a sitting tenant's lease needs the landlord's written consent, not unreasonably withheld, answered in reasonable time with reasons (Landlord and Tenant Act 1988) | a week |
| A share in a business | its owner | his weekly cut times 20, on the business's usual week before any cause Frank made; or less money plus a favour or pressure (2.17) | none, or a solicitor if written | a day for a handshake, a week if written |

1. No building and no whole business is bought outright in the game. At 1990 prices (a terraced house about £25,000, 2.7.8; a parade shop at eight years' rent about £10,400) nothing is within the money curve (EC-12: about £1,200 at week 13), so a purchase row would be dead content (mends P-4, P-9, X-3). Frank holds premises by lease and businesses by share; he becomes a landlord by letting on what he leases (P7). Decision 16 and G20 stand: buildings and businesses change hands between townspeople (E2.5's newcomer, a closure), and Frank as landlord is reached through P7.
2. The estate agent is a person (mends P-13, BF11). The Hook's only estate agent is Agar's office on the cold-store road (RT2-08). Its clerk has a want and a fear like anyone (2.14) and serves Agar first:
   - every deal through the office is a fact Agar's office holds the same day (law 1);
   - taking premises on a rival's ground, by any route, is a sighting of Frank's firm on that ground for that rival's ladder (RT2-07) once any of that rival's people learn of it;
   - on Agar's word, by his rung and his want, the office may delay, decline to put Frank forward, or tell the landlord something about him;
   - a private landlord can be dealt with directly (a card in the window, the landlord at his door), without the office, at the price of a reference the landlord himself accepts.
   Which buildings are let through the office and which privately is data in places.json.
3. A seller may refuse at any price if attached (an old owner's pride, a widow's late husband's shop), by temper. Each share sold on the same parade raises the next owner's asking price by a tenth once he hears of it (G27).
4. Payment comes from book money or washed money (2.7.1). Any payment over £1,000 in notes is a fact the solicitor or landlord remembers and the Act III inspector can ask about. Balance figure.
5. A handshake share has no paper: the owner can deny it, and it gives no claim on his insurance (P6). A written share is a fact in a drawer that anyone who reads it learns.

### P3. Use, the council and a business Frank starts (law 3, people; law 4)
1. A business Frank starts (mends P-12, BF19). Frank holding a lease may start a business of the premises' use:
   - it is a front with its man (2.17): someone stands behind the counter, a townsperson hired by 2.11.1's need road;
   - fitting out is bought from named sellers (the ironmonger, the lot) at their price and takes a week; stock is ordered by phone or letter from a supplier off the map (G21) and arrives by van the next week, or is Frank's own goods (stolen goods carry their owner, economy E4.1);
   - its week follows EC-04's row for its trade, starting at half and rising a tenth a week to its usual week as the town hears of it; it takes trade from the existing seller of that trade by economy E2.3, and that seller reacts by temper (economy E2.4).
   Balance figures.
2. A change of use (a café in a former shop; a launderette or cab office, which fit no class: Use Classes Order 1987, research) needs the council's yes by letter.
3. One council officer for licensing and planning (mends P-2, P-10), a person with a want and a fear and Agar's man (canon: Agar's machine is corruption and lawyers). An application or a complaint about a business brings his letter within two game weeks: yes, no with a reason, or an inspection. He can be argued with, given what he needs, or squared at his law (bribing him is a deed of corruption with a heavy weight in the town's talk). The cab firm's licence stays as RT2-18, with one change (E-3, settled): a second stop while suspended lengthens the suspension by a fortnight and brings a fine; a licence is never revoked for good, so the firm is never lost whole.
4. Anyone can write to the council about a business: a rival, a neighbour, the newsagent. The officer answers by P3.3.

### P4. Rates and the community charge (law 4)
1. A building's rates are part of its rent or its owner's costs and are never a separate demand (mends P-1). The firm's own rates stay inside its costs (RT-16, 2.7.4).
2. Frank's community charge follows decision 18: letters and one bailiff's knock. A demand comes to the flat in week 4 for £384 for the year, payable in ten monthly sums of £38.40 at the council counter or by post. A missed month brings one reminder letter; a second missed month brings the bailiff's knock once, a scene the street sees (regard). Paying at the door or the counter ends it. It never takes the office, the cars or the tin. (Figure: Great Grimsby's charge, property research.)

### P5. A front's week (law 4, law 3)
1. A front's week moves for causes, as any shop's (G20, EC-04): payday Fridays, weather, the other shops, a squeeze, a closure nearby, trouble.
2. Each week its man brings Frank's cut on its day in an envelope with the till roll, short if the man is (2.17).
3. What a front washes is one figure (mends P-5, X-2): £60 a week for each front, held in EC-04's shop data as each shop's wash column and added to the count's limit (economy E6.2; RT-01 to quote the same limit). A front's real takings (EC-04) are its own business and not a wash figure.
4. Front matters come to the firm, not to each front (mends P-15, BF20): at most one front matter a week in all, from the front with the strongest cause (a broken window, a man asking for money, an inspector's letter, a hand off sick, stock gone missing), brought by its man. Frank deals with it (time, a talk, money) or lets it go; let go, it halves that front's cut that week and, the second week running at the same front, costs its man's regard. Never a defence set-piece (crew draft R9).

### P6. Fire and insurers are people (law 2, law 3)
1. A business, and any townsperson who insures his car, has a policy: a named insurer, a sum insured, conditions (locks, declared stock, no change of use without telling), and an owner who pays the premium as part of his own costs.
2. A loss over £500 (fire, burglary, a cargo loss) brings a named loss adjuster from the city within a week. He reads, in this order: the brigade's reading of the seat (G18), the owner's purse and debts, whether the cover was raised lately, who was where, and what neighbours say.
3. He pays in four weeks, delays to eight while he asks questions, or refuses. Insurers mostly settled suspected fraud privately (research, a 1996 abstract only), so he goes to the police when the brigade read the fire as deliberate and he finds a motive. A threat to him, or his death, is itself such a motive: the claim passes to the police (mends BK05). Balance figures.
4. The insurer is a role two deep (BF04): if the adjuster is hurt, gone or dead, his firm sends a second adjuster within a week; a second loss brings the claims manager by letter. The claim file stays at the insurer's office (things T7) and the next man reads it.
5. The money goes to the policyholder only. Frank's share comes through the owner by their arrangement; a handshake share gives him nothing on paper (P2.5). A building rebuilt on the money follows things T3.2.
6. A fire is a deed by G18 whatever its purpose; a fire in a building where someone sleeps is among the heaviest deeds the town can talk about.

### P7. Frank as landlord (law 4)
1. Letting on (mends P-14, BF21): whoever holds a right to premises (a lease) may let them, or part of them, to a tenant, with the head landlord's consent where the lease asks for it (consent not unreasonably withheld; that the 1988 Act covers underletting as well as assigning is own knowledge, the research row covers assigning). Rent from Frank's tenant is book money, paid on its day; Frank still owes his own rent to the head landlord.
2. A tenant is a person with a purse, a temper and a week (economy E12). One who falls behind follows the one debtor rule (economy E10).
3. Frank's answers, each with its cost in talk: wait; ask in person; a letter (courting C7); a notice to quit of four weeks, then the county court through the solicitor (a soft-edge scene, a fee, a date three weeks on). Throwing a tenant out by force or changing his locks is a deed the street hears and a crime.
4. Where tenants come from (mends P-11): premises standing empty to let draw a named outsider within four game weeks, two weeks at or under the usual rent (economy E2.5, the same rule); and anyone in the town who needs a roof may ask (a laid-off docker, docks D9.3; a crewman; a newcomer to a trade).
5. A tenant asks for repairs; a repair not done in two weeks costs his regard and the street's (balance figures). Repairs are done by people for money and a day.

### P8. The flat above is Frank's home (law 2, law 3)
1. The flat is where Frank sleeps (any bed he has the right to will do; the save is the same) and where his letters land: rent, the charge, her letters (courting), a summons, a rival's note.
2. It has hiding places like any premises (things T2); the one tin is at the office (2.7.2; mends P-6, X-1). A police search under a warrant reaches the flat (justice J4.2); a burglar finds what he finds.
3. It is his rather than Mickey's by what is in it: Mickey's things go as the rungs rise (2.12.5), Frank's take their place.
4. She may visit for tea in the afternoon or the evening and leaves before the night (courting; affection only). A rival can put a brick through its window, a deed that tells the street.
5. Losing it under RT-16 sends him to sleep in the office; a flat he leases gives him another bed.

## (d) The player's hands and eyes

- In the world: a card in a window or the agent's board; the agent's clerk at Agar's office on the cold-store road; the landlord at his door; the council counter at the Exchange (soft edge); demands and letters on the mat; the envelope and till roll on the front's day; the adjuster with his clipboard in the burnt shop.
- In talk: "Is it to let?", "What would you take for a share?", "I'll want it in writing", "The rent's late", suggested lines for each (ruling 4).
- In the Ledger (area 8): each holding as its papers (lease, share, front, tenants), the matter of the week, the man who runs it. No slider and no profit graph.

## (e) Edges

- Trying to buy a building or the parade: the owner will not sell to him; a lease or a share is offered where the owner would (P2.1).
- A public building: refused by its officer (P1.3).
- Letting a flat to a crewman rent-free: allowed; a gift under the debt and regard rules.
- Burning a rival's front: a deed under G18 and P6; the owner's insurer pays him if the claim is honest, so the rival is set back weeks; rival hits come from the ladder (2.10.1).
- Opening a business the town does not have: P3.1; it draws a rival newcomer only if it prospers (ruling 3).
- Avoiding Agar's office: a private landlord, or a sitting tenant's lease with the landlord's consent; Agar still hears if a rival's ground is involved and anyone sees.
