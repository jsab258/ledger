# Design draft: the docks and smuggling (version 2)

Binding order: canon.md, Jafar's rulings, the blueprint. Every finding was checked at its source; the change table at the end says what was accepted or rejected.

## (a) What exists and what is missing

What the blueprint has:
- The dock road, the gate, Customs and the Harbour Board; the Board's counter and a clerk who sells shipment times; a Customs officer "who never reports, by trade"; a bonded store for tobacco and boxed goods (never drink); a tea stall at the gate from 22:00 to 06:00 (4.2.3).
- Smuggled tobacco: £100 a case off the boat, £160 sold on; two cases a week pass, four with men at the gate (rung 4); more and Customs take the week's load (EC-07).
- The dock gun seller (RT2-14); the Harbour Board's late men as an account (2.15.2); the honest dock shift (decision 17); the water's edge (4.2.4). The Customs officer's law is 0.15 or under (2.5.2).
- The story needs Ron's Customs charge before the magistrates every eight days, once in the last week (BLUEPRINT-03, the story of 24 Nov for week 7; conflict C-335). Canon of 24 Nov: Ron goes inside unless Frank pays to stop it.

What was missing (research, drill, chaos test, blind check):
- The hands of smuggling: the meet, carrying, keeping, buying.
- Customs as people with fallbacks and a book (BK01), and a Customs charge with a court road the story can use (D-2, X-4).
- A learner that a rota of drivers or a skipped week cannot reset (D-3, BP08).
- A check at the bonded store that reads papers, not the keeper's own count (D-10, BS09).

## (b) The rules

### D1. Goods have bulk, and bulk fills the hands (law 2, things)
1. Every carried good has a bulk: pocket (a packet, a watch), coat (a carton, a gun), one hand (a holdall, a radio), two hands (a case, a television). Two hands means no fighting, no running, a walk at the slow pace (1.1.4), and a mark any watcher catches (2.2.5).
2. A case of cigarettes is 200 packets (20 cartons) and fills two hands. The boot holds what 1.6.9 rules (the envelope, a spare coat or a box: one case), unless Jafar answers the boot question otherwise (summary, question 1). Other places hold what things T1 sets; a case off the boat waits in the office shed or a lock-up (T1). A case can be set down, handed to a man, or left, and whoever finds it holds it (G13).
3. Smuggled goods carry no owner's claim once paid for, but are seizable by Customs anywhere until sold on; stolen goods carry their owner and a hot mark (economy E4.1).

### D2. The meet (law 3, law 4)
1. Smuggled goods come ashore through a named person: a crewman of a named coaster that berths on a known day (as the gun seller does, RT2-14). He buys from ship's stores or a duty-free shop abroad at less than the untaxed British price (180p less 131p tax is about 49p a packet, docks research, so 200 packets are about £98 untaxed; what ship's stores charged is own knowledge and lower), and makes his margin on the £100 a case he asks of Frank (EC-07). He brings what he has on that night, by the tide and the dark, to his berth at the fish dock. (Mends D-4: the 110p Belgian shop price is dropped from the hands.)
2. The hand-over is an act with the seven slots (2.2.1): the tea stall's keeper, a docker on the night shift and the gate's special constable are watchers by their routines.
3. Frank pays cash on the spot; the crewman remembers who paid and what was said. A crewman who is short-changed or threatened takes his trade to a rival next voyage.

### D3. Who buys (law 4)
1. Buyers are named people, each with a weekly limit and a rate, following the buyer rule (economy E4.2): a Thursday market stall (up to 2 cases a week), the caff's back counter (half a case), men in the yards (a case among them), a corner shop of low law (one case). Balance figures.
2. Each buyer is a listener who knows who sold to him (2.7.6). The newsagent who loses trade (EC-04) first knows only that cheap packets are about; he learns names through gossip.
3. Each buyer's rate falls a tenth for each week the same goods arrive from Frank's firm; four weeks without them halves the fall, never wipes it (economy E7.2; mends D-3's skipped-week hole).

### D4. A cargo has an owner and a count (law 2, law 1)
1. Every load on the dock road, in the bonded store or in a shed has a named owner and a count on paper. A loss is found at the next check, which reads the goods against the paper (things T7.2): the tally clerk at his next count tells the shed foreman and the owner. A loss over £500 brings the owner's insurer by property P6. (Mends D-7: the receiver, carrier and seal steps are cut; theft from cargo stays small, a box or a few cases, so they never fired.)
2. The tally and count practice is own knowledge for 1990; the research's source is from 1970 and containers had changed much of it by 1990 (D-6).

### D5. Customs are people (law 3)
1. Customs in the Hook are named officers:
   - the gate officer, with a routine at his hut (law 0.15 or under, 2.5.2): takes money to look away and never goes to the police, but seizes in his own name when a seizure profits him or when he is watched;
   - one rummage pair with a routine, boarding ships on a rota and on signs (a tip, a ship from a known port, a pattern), as the law allows any part of a ship in port to be searched (CEMA 1979 s.27, research);
   - the senior officer at the Custom House, who counts seizures and reads complaints, and who is also the VAT inspection's first fallback (economy E13.1, X-4). He is a name in talk with no routine until a loop calls him (D-8).
2. Each wants and fears by role: the gate officer wants money and a quiet gate and fears the senior officer; the rummage pair want seizures that count; the senior officer wants a clean record and fears the regional office.
3. The gate officer is the learner for the gate run (economy E7). He remembers Frank's firm, not the man carrying (mends D-3, BP08, BF22): after six runs by any of the firm's men on the crewman's day he watches that day, and the safe allowance falls by one case each four weeks the pattern holds; four weeks without a run halves what he has learned, never to nothing. Bribing him buys one look-away a week at a price that rises with each case past two. Balance figures.
4. Every Customs role names its fallback two deep (BF04): the gate officer's place goes to the next officer on the Custom House rota from the next shift, and then to a rummage man; the senior officer's to the regional office's officer. What a role has learned (the firm's pattern, a tip, a face) is written in the Custom House's daybook (things T7.1) and read by the next holder, so killing or buying the man does not wipe it. The killing of a Customs officer on duty takes LH-01's road for who knows, the manhunt, arrest and remand, without the squad (justice J10.1, BF05 in part: no new trigger for a town-wide event).

### D6. A Customs case has its own road (law 3, law 7)
1. A smuggling or duty case is Customs' own: the goods and any vehicle used are seized; the man is interviewed at the Custom House (a soft-edge scene under two real minutes). Customs then either accept a penalty in place of court, paid at the Custom House, which ends the case, or lay a charge before the magistrates themselves. (That Customs prosecuted their own cases in 1990 and could take a penalty in place of court is own knowledge; the research states the first as a premise without a source row, D-5.)
2. A Customs charge follows the same court rule as a police charge (justice J1.5, mends D-2, X-4): bail with a date, and no court scene, unless the man is remanded in custody, when he goes before the magistrates every eight days (a soft-edge scene each time) until his case ends. This is the road Ron's charge takes in the story (C-335), and paying his penalty before his last hearing is the way Frank pays to stop it (canon, 24 Nov).
3. A charge that stands joins the case list read at the ending (2.6.9) and reaches Ellis as talk. A seized car is returned only if the charge falls; car two is never lost for more than a day (RT2-06).
4. A penalty paid is a record the Act III inspector can read.

### D7. The bonded store's keeper (law 3)
1. The bonded store is an approved place with conditions; breaching one costs the keeper £100 and repeated breaches his approval (CEMA 1979 s.92, research).
2. Its keeper is a named man who fears losing his approval more than most things: he will alter his own count to hide a small loss if he fears revocation more than he respects the law, which makes him a lever (a hook, a bribe, a fear) and a witness.
3. The monthly Customs stock check counts the stock against the receipt and dispatch papers (things T7.2), not the keeper's own count. An altered count is a false entry found when papers and stock disagree; the keeper is then a knower Customs can lean on (LH-04). (Mends D-10, BS09, BF12: the hook buys time until the check, never past it.)

### D8. The Harbour Board is a power (law 3)
1. The Board wants traffic, rent and quiet berths; it fears losing trade to roll-on roll-off ports (research), strikes and a name for theft.
2. It has special constables at the gate (Tees had 21, research; the Hook's 6 is a balance figure); one is on duty at the barrier at any time, with a routine, and the rest are names in talk (D-8). Its clerk who sells shipment times (4.2.3) is its weak point.
3. Repeated losses (three in a month, balance figure) bring answers by the Board's temper: more specials at night, a new check at the gate, a word to Customs, a firm it trusts favoured with work. Frank's firm, holding the Board's late-men account (2.15.2), is trusted or distrusted by what the Board hears.

### D9. Dockers as a power and a pool (law 3)
1. Since the Dock Labour Scheme ended on 3 July 1989, about a third of the Hook's registered dockers have been laid off (Hull 36% by 21 July 1989, research).
2. Working dockers want work and pay and fear more lay-offs. They notice cargo moving off-pattern and talk at the tea stall (2.4.1). A docker who loses shifts because the Board cuts work after thefts holds a grudge against the thief once he knows him.
3. Laid-off dockers are the main need-road recruiting pool (2.11.1) and among those who ask Frank for a roof (property P7.4). Each brings his ties (crew R5).

## (d) The player's hands and eyes

- He meets a man at a berth at night, carries a case in both arms to the cab, opens the boot (seen like any act, GV-01), drives, stores, sells in talk.
- He sees the port's people at their routines: the gate officer in his hut, the rummage pair on a gangway, a special at the barrier, dockers at the tea stall, the clerk at the Board's counter.
- He hears of losses and suspicion in talk ("The Board's put two more on nights"; "Customs boarded the Grete this morning").
- The Ledger keeps the boat's days, the crewman's name, each buyer and his limit as Frank knows them, what is stored where, and any Customs case with its dates.

## (e) Edges

- Bringing in a lorry load: the boat's man brings only what one man can bring ashore unseen; bigger loads need the Board's paper and draw Customs at once.
- Robbing the bonded store every week: the keeper's count holds until the monthly check (D7.3); the Board's answers rise.
- Running the gate past the allowance: seizure within three weeks (EC-07), through the gate officer or the rummage pair.
- Rotating carriers: the gate officer remembers the firm (D5.3).
- Killing the gate officer: his pattern is in the Custom House daybook; LH-01's road (D5.4).
