### Research (Jafar's research rules)

Questions written first:
1. Was a 1990 minicab operator likely to be registered for VAT, and at what turnover?
2. Did Customs and Excise make routine VAT visits to small traders in 1990, and did they announce them?
3. Is a VAT visit a cause in the world (an institution's routine) or a story device?

Findings (primary sources read in full where reachable; each page read 28 Nov 2026):
- The VAT registration limit rose from £23,600 to £25,400 of taxable supplies a year with effect from 21 March 1990 (explanatory note to SI 1990/682, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1990/682 ; the same figure in Finance Act 1990 s10 as enacted, https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1990/29/section/10/enacted , read as a search extract only, so marked unverified in full).
- VAT control visits were made by appointment, to make selective checks on a trader's records and accounts; about 350,000 were made in 1983 to 1984 (HC Deb 13 Feb 1985 vol 73 c200W, https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1985/feb/13/value-added-tax ).
- The 1991 to 1992 management plan envisaged 540,000 control visits; plans for 1993 to 1994 were 300,000, the fall put down to fewer, longer visits (HC Deb 2 Nov 1992 vol 213 cc38-9W, https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written_answers/1992/nov/02/value-added-tax ).

What this settles: an appointment letter for a VAT visit is a period-true routine of an outside power, so Act III's letter can be a cause in the world, not a story timer (A8 below). Whether Mickey's turnover passes £25,400 depends on how drivers' takings are booked (drivers keep half, 2.7.1 as cited by the crew draft R6.1); this pass treats Mickey's as registered (Mickey registered it years ago), a design fact, not a sourced one.

Could not be reached or confirmed: the notice a VAT officer gave before a visit in 1990 (unverified; the design keeps a week, as 3.1.3 has); the 1976 Act's power to suspend an operator's licence on a charge (unverified; C-340 below is designed as a game rule, not claimed as 1990 law).

## 1. The engine in nine rules

These are the few general rules everything else in this file uses. Each builds on a part that exists; nothing here is a new system beyond the story director the blueprint already lists as a gap (7.4, S15).

SE1. A beat is a card, never a date (law 7; reuses the beat card format of S16 quoted under 3.1.2, G22, GD-03). Each card holds: the states that make it ready; the chair that carries it (SE5); its sign (3.1.5); its three routes (ghost, talker, schemer; section 7); its first fallback (the same beat, the next person in the chair); its second fallback (the beat's purpose met another way); and what it leaves if it lapses (a fact the town holds, never a block).

SE2. Causes come only from the world (law 7). A beat may become ready only when a person holds a reason to act (a want, a grudge, a duty, a step in a routine) or when a dated public fact on the calendar arrives. G30's list holds the display Saturday, Remembrance Sunday, the gate chained on Saturday 1 December and rent each Friday; this pass proposes adding three it does not yet name: Bonfire Night (already in the town's calendar, S44, covered under chapter 02), Mickey's funeral in week 1, and an outside power's appointment letter (A8). Nothing becomes ready because the story wants it.

SE3. Slow pressures are routines with real ends (G23 applied to the powers; law 5). Four routines keep the story moving when the player does nothing, and each can be changed by what he does: Ellis's fire inquiry works down a list of places, one call a game week, reordered by what she hears (A1); each rival's want, read from his temper and the docks' decline (A5); the Customs officer's appointment (A8); the docks' own end (the pen's count falling, the last late boat, the gate chained; gap 3.7, S32). These are causes, not timers: kill Ellis and Hebden starts from where her list stood; burn the book and the list finds ashes.

SE4. The director picks, it never makes (S15 as quoted in the gap under 7.4). Among cards already ready it picks one, by these preferences in order: one heavy beat a game week; a card that tests the town's reading of Frank (G26); a card whose carrier is near Frank in time and place; variety of shape. It may hold a ready card back up to its window; it may not bring one forward whose states are unmet.

SE5. Roles are chairs (reuses RT2-05, WI-P02, and the three chairs of S33 named under RT2-05: the opponent's place, the bond's place, the book's place). Every role the story uses is a chair with an ordered list of occupants, two named deep, then the role's generic holder from cast.json (WI-P02's last sentence). Beats name chairs, never people. Section 3 lists the chairs.

SE6. Fallbacks are two deep and fail forward (law 6; G22; FF-06 under GD-03). First the next occupant carries the same beat, with lines for that person. Second the beat's purpose is met another way (a letter instead of a visit, a rumour instead of a confession). Third, and only if both fail, the beat lapses: it leaves a fact the town holds and the next card reads it. No beat waits on a person who cannot come.

SE7. Signs first, for every chair that changes hands (3.1.5; crew draft R7). When a chair passes, the town says so within a game day: the new man introduced at the office, the old one's absence asked after (2.16), a line in the Ledger.

SE8. One loud deed a week is a slot the player fills (reuses the frame's deed cards F1 to F7 as quoted under 3.1.2). Each week the director offers one deed card through a person on a state, as SE1. But the slot is filled by the first deed of weight Frank does that week, authored or not, seen and retold (2.4.4's "it comes back"). A player who kills a principal in week 1 has filled week 1's slot himself; the card offered stays open as an ordinary job, and the next week's card reads the killing.

SE9. The engine reads deeds, never the input (law 8; pad players get every power typists have). Routes, chairs and fallbacks read what Frank did and said, by keyboard, pad or chosen line alike (Ledger draft L5's claim builder).

### 3.2 The five fates, as one rule each

PF1. Killed. The chair passes at once to the next occupant (SE5); the deed never cools (2.4.2); the person's ties gather by G08. Beats the person already carried stand as facts. The killing fills the week's deed slot (SE8). If the person held a hook or a hold, it dies with them (LH-15: "the holder's fate"); if they held a confidence of Frank's, it dies too.

PF2. Hurt and in hospital. Absent for the days their hurt sets (RT2-05). A beat they carry waits for them if its window is longer than their days; otherwise the next occupant carries that beat alone, and the chair goes back when they return. Hospital costs days, never money (his ruling).

PF3. Jailed. Held for hours: as PF2 for those hours. Remanded: present only by visits, by LH-07's scenes, widened from every killing charge to every remand in custody (C-336 below). Their talk, holds and confidences still work through a visit; their hands do not. A remanded bond is the frame's "Ron inside".

PF4. Bought. By Frank, a rival or the police. The person keeps the chair but acts for the buyer within named limits (2.6.8's pattern: once, a price, a limit). The buyer becomes a party to every beat they carry: a bought Ellis warns, a bought driver carries for a rival. Signs first (crew draft R7).

PF5. Turned or gone. Turned to a rival: keeps the chair, carries the other side's purpose, and holds what he knows as a hold on Frank (LH-15); Mickey's people never turn to the police (ruling of 1 Oct), only to a rival, and only past grudge. Left the Hook: the chair passes as PF1 without a deed; the person can come back by their own rule (Pauline when paid, Sheila on a call after a clean week, RT2-06).

## 6. Part (e): story length when the player skips nights, and what keeps the acts paced

The clock: 48 real minutes a game day, 2 real minutes a game hour (his ruling of 24 Nov). Seven game weeks are 49 days, 39.2 hours played in full. Real hours depend on what the player skips, never on the calendar:

| Player | Game hours skipped a day | Real minutes played a day | Seven weeks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Never sleeps or waits | 0 | 48 | 39.2 h |
| Sleeps midnight to 07:00 | 7 | 34 | 27.8 h |
| Sleeps 23:00 to 07:00, waits out two empty 3-hour stretches | 14 | 20 | 16.3 h |
| Waits to every appointment, plays about 6 game hours a day | 18 | 12 | 9.8 h |
| Sleeps through everything, woken only by the town (about 1.5 game hours) | 22.5 | 3 | 2.5 h |

(Computed: real minutes = (24 minus skipped) x 2; hours = minutes x 49 / 60.)

What this means: "about 20 hours" sits between the second and third rows. Game time is the same for all five; the acts end on the same dates and states. So the danger is not a story that runs long or short in game time; it is a heavy skipper who meets the beats thinly, and a never-skipper who meets empty stretches.

The rule that replaces CLK-06's withdrawn fix (law 5; reuses 7.11, 7.12, 3.5.6, WQ-01):
1. A sleep runs until 07:00 or the first thing the town has for him; a wait offers only known appointments (7.11 as written). There is no "skip a day".
2. Every card the director has ready announces its window at least 5 game hours ahead (7.11), so a sleeper is woken into it by a person (a man at the door, a message), never by a screen.
3. The week's heavy beat cannot be slept past: its carrier comes to Frank's door if Frank does not come (SE6 fallback 1, applied to place).
4. The never-skipper's floor stays the director's 30-minute rule (WQ-01) and never five minutes with nothing new (3.7, canon's target).
5. Measured by five bots, one per row above. Pass: the third row lands between 16 and 28 real hours; every row meets every week's heavy beat; no row meets a beat it cannot act on. If the median is far off 20, change what counts as an empty hour for the sleep (the earliest wake), never the calendar (CLK-06: a longer calendar is never the fix).
6. A player who sleeps through everything gets a short story: the town tells the close in guesses and the name it calls him (decision 38). That is fair: nothing was hidden from him.

What keeps the acts paced: Act I ends on Sunday's answer or its states (RT-04); Act II ends at a sit-down, caused in the world by A5's rule; Act III is the calendar's seventh week with the appointment letter (A8). The WQ-02 stages ("each ending in a rung change at most every 4 real hours") are measured in real hours, so they move with skipping; this pass proposes measuring them in game days instead (a rung change at least every 8 game days), which holds for every row.
