## G1 Data ready for more districts (law 2 things, law 1 knowing, law 5 time)

Why: his rulings say the game grows to more districts later and nothing is tied to the Hook's size (RULINGS 28 Oct: more districts in updates after release). The audit found rung 4 defined as "fronts and rounds across the Hook" (2.12.2), the squad of LH-02, which BLIND-REVIEW X-10 reads as changing "the whole town's nights", the Hook's only estate agent being Agar's office (P2.2), and no rule for a save made with content the install lacks (BP11 checklist A49.09).

The rule.
1. Every record with a place carries a district: places, people (home and work), businesses and their boards, ground held by any power, police beats and stations, routines' stops, deeds, facts and Ledger lines. The Hook with its docks is district 1. Nothing else changes in this build: one district is installed.
2. Wherever a rule says "the Hook" or "the town" as a scope, it reads "the district it happens in", unless a ruling or canon names the Hook itself (the story's beats, its people, the ferry). A town-wide event in a rule is a district-wide event; his ruling of no new town-wide events then holds per district.
3. Gossip needs no change: it already travels by schedule crossings (2.4.1), so it crosses into another district only through people whose routines cross (drivers, fares, workers, kin) or through the paper and letters (2.4.5, G21).
4. Standing (rung names, 7.7) is counted per district from what is held in that district (G2 below).
5. Saves record the content version and the districts present. A save made before a district existed loads with that district at its starting state on the save's date: its people know nothing of Frank but what crossing routines carry; nothing in an existing district changes because a new one arrived, and no fact is written into the past. A save that holds a district the install lacks keeps that district's records asleep: its people, holdings and Ledger lines are kept and marked "out of town"; the road to it behaves as the soft edge does today; reinstalled, it wakes where it slept.
6. A road to a district not installed stays the soft edge (BP04: "comes back as time passed and money paid"); a road to an installed district is driven and walked.

The data. One new file, districts.json: id, name, its roads out and where they lead, its fixed days (market day), its powers (station, rivals present, the other firm or firms, agents, Customs where a quay exists), its paper. One new field, district, on every placed record. The paper map gets a sheet per district.

The player's hands and what he sees. Nothing new in this build. Later, a district is entered by its road, as Phantom Liberty's district is reached from an old save by a call from someone who lives there and a story gate, never by a menu; the Ledger's People and Holdings say the part of town in words ("over in Copper Row").

Numbers. None to tune. Test: the foundation build's test door (9.13) carries a stub second district (three places, five people, one road) behind a flag; golden rows prove every placed record has a district, a save made without the stub loads with it, and a save made with it loads without it and back.

Edges. Districts not installed answer by letters and addresses (G21) as now. A rival or person whose routine leads to an uninstalled district leaves by its road and comes back as time passed.

Stage. Stage 1, the foundation: the key is cheap in the first data files and dear to add later.

Combination test. Frank's debtor Darren works on the Thursday market in Copper Row (2.1 names the market off the map). In week 5 a district update installs Copper Row. Loading the week-5 save: Darren is on his market stall on Thursday, still owes his money, and holds everything he held; the Copper Row stallholders, who never crossed into the Hook, do not know Frank; one who buys from the Hook's grocer on Mondays knows his name by gossip. Nothing in the Hook moved.

## G2 Growth past rung 4: wants that grow with wealth (law 4 exchange, law 3 people, law 5 time)

Why: EV 1 (money grows with the empire; the player must be able to get rich, invest, buy and expand), EV 3 (paper needs clean money; each front raises the wash: the growth loop) and EV 4 (trouble grows with holdings; money never stops mattering) meet a blueprint where rung 4 is the top (2.12.2), rivals "stay on their rungs and climb no more" after the ending and the town "repeats its ordinary weeks" (RT2-21, 7.15), and money stops mattering by weeks 10 to 12 (economy design F3). Empire of Sin is the warning: once one neighbourhood was held the reviewer "never had to think about cash again" and businesses "became names in a list". Scarface is the lead: standing bought by fronts and by visible purchases, each level opening more.

The figures belong to the revision in hand (EV 1's research and EC-12's re-run). This design gives the shape the figures fill.

The rule.
1. Rung names stay as ruled (7.7) and are counted per district (G1). Past rung 4 a district holds no higher rung; growth is breadth (another district, when installed) and depth (what money does in a district held).
2. Every holding brings a want with it, so wealth makes its own uses. Each want is a person, a place or a thing in the world, never a menu sink, and each changes a choice:
   - Wash: every front adds to what Sheila can wash (EV 3, E6.2), and every paper buy needs clean money (G6), so more fronts make bigger buys reachable, and bigger buys (a building, a share) pay rent or takings into the book. This is the loop.
   - Defence: each holding is a target (EV 4): men to stand at it (R9), repairs by people for money and a day (RT-06), the insurer (P6). Upkeep only where it makes a choice (E11).
   - People with prices: a brief on call (one of rung 4's holdings, 2.12.2), Ellis's retainer (economy design: about £800, then £100 a week), the council officer (P3), a nominee's cut (G6), a crew that expects more as the firm grows (R6).
   - Standing bought in the open: the good overcoat and a better car (1.6.10, the lot), a street collection or flowers (E9), paying a family's debt (G04): each moves readings (G4) and opens doors (2.12.3), as Scarface's purchases raise standing, but each is seen and told, and spending beyond what the books explain draws the inspector and Ellis (EV 4).
   - Newcomers: an opening draws a newcomer (E2.5); a newcomer is a business or a small outfit that sets up against a holding, never a fourth rival and never a town-wide event. After the ending the three rivals stay where they were (7.15 kept); newcomers and the town's learners (E7, E8) are what keeps pressing.
3. After the ending the register opens. From 3 December 1990 any person may inspect the land register for a fee (SI 1990/1359; Estates Gazette): any registered building held in Frank's own name becomes a fact anyone who pays can learn (Ellis, Agar's office, Alison for the paper). Held through a nominee, the register shows the nominee (G6). This is a real change of the period that lands the day after the calendar ends (CLK-06), so the after-ending town has a new way of knowing without a new event.
4. Money never idles unseen: a large tin is a thing found by an office search (2.6.9, E1), and a large book is read at the inspection (E13) and by the open register's readers. Holding wealth is itself a choice with risk.

The data. On each holding: the wants it brings (defence men, insurer, upkeep, wash added). On each district: its newcomer slots (E2.5). On each registered building: registered or not (the Estates Gazette's 9 million unregistered properties mean some are not); its registered owner's name.

The player's hands and what he sees. The Holdings page (L2) by place, with each holding's week and what it needs; Sheila says what the book can carry; the agent's window and the paper's small ads show premises that exist (no new places, decision 19); people with an ask come to where Frank is usually found (G5).

Numbers. To be run, by the revision's money run: (a) in every after-ending week to week 26, at least one want the player can see costs more than one week's clean income; (b) the richest style never holds idle more than four weeks of its weekly income without a want offered (a test of offers, not a cap); (c) no route pays more than twice the middle an hour (7.17).

Edges. Past the installed districts: letters (G21). A want refused by the paper gate is answered by a nominee or by waiting until the book can carry it (G6). The firm is never lost whole (RT2-06), however far growth overreaches.

Stage. Act II for the loop (first front at rung 3, 2.12.2), after the ending for the open register; build stage 3.

Combination test. Week 11, after the ending. Frank holds £4,000 in the tin and £600 in the book. He wants the caff's building. The agent asks how it is paid (G6): the book will not carry it, so Frank pushes Sheila's count harder for three weeks (EV 5: she warns; each week over is recorded), then buys in his own name. On 3 December the register opened; Alison pays to search it after a tip (J4) and writes that the new owner of the caff is the minicab man (2.4.5). Readers hold it at her strength; the inspector's next look at the books (E13) asks where £3,400 came from; the pushed weeks are there. Money mattered at every step, and nothing new was invented.

## G3 The three routes as he ruled them, and a step met by any hand (law 8, law 7)

Why: his ruling names three routes for every job and story beat: ghost, talker, schemer. G25 names "talk or menace, stealth or cunning, force" and 7.17 says on paper "the schemer does not" work. The drafts have since built the schemer's tools (orders R10, plants R15, tips J4, planted stories L4, the claim builder L5.4), but nothing lets a job's step be met by a deed someone else did because Frank arranged it. Hitman's Opportunities show what designed routes are for: Jensen calls them "an onboarding experience" so players "see the number of different approaches".

The rule.
1. The three routes are his: ghost (unseen: stealth, cunning, timing, a borrowed coat or tunic; leaves things moved, doors forced and glimpses), talker (face to face: talk, a bargain, menace, a lie; leaves people who heard him and what he promised) and schemer (through others: an order, a tip, a planted story, a bought third party, a frame; leaves go-betweens who know and told facts that can be traced back). G25's "talk or menace" is the talker; "stealth or cunning" the ghost; its third route becomes the schemer.
2. Force is not one of the three designed routes; it stays open to everyone at every step by the fight model (1.3.4), with its own traces. Where a beat has only force today (G25: the yard in week 6, week 7's fight, each rival's go at his men in week 5), its ghost and schemer routes are written first; the story job writes them.
3. A step is a state of the world, never a method. "Lee's debt paid by Friday" or "the Widow's boat stopped this week" is met by that state, whoever brought it about: Frank's hand, his man's, or a stranger acting on a story Frank planted. GD-03 already lets any route meet a step; this adds that a step met by another's deed counts the same.
4. Credit follows what people hold. The giver credits whoever he believes did it, by what he holds (G01, G03): a scheme done well can leave the giver crediting the third party; Frank can tell him otherwise, and his claim is weighed. The step still settles; only regard and reading move by belief.
5. A scheme that fails (the third party does nothing, the tip is not believed) fails forward: the step stays open and the job settles by the clock (3.5.6).

The data. Each job card and beat: its steps as states; for each of the three routes, one authored lead (the obvious way a person in the town would suggest) tagged ghost, talker or schemer, with the traces it leaves; each step's "needs a face" flag (G25 kept). The style bots of G29 become: ghost, talker, schemer, brute, clean firm and a mixed one.

The player's hands and what he sees. The Ledger names a job's step as a state ("the debt paid by Friday"), never a method; the three leads reach him as people's suggestions in talk ("You'll not get past his dog. Mind, his brother-in-law can't stand him"), as Hitman's Opportunities reach the player through overheard threads; nothing is listed on a screen.

Numbers. To be run in the economy run with routes (7.17's test, C-549): each route within twice the middle route's pounds an hour; each pure route bot reaches every ending its style allows (G29).

Edges. Steps that need a face say so in the Ledger before the week (G25 kept). A scheme the Core cannot resolve leaves the step open, never stuck.

Stage. Stage 1 for steps as states (the job graph's data); routes authored with each stage's jobs.

Combination test. Ron's job in week 3: Lee Drewery's debt paid by Friday. Ghost: take goods from his yard at night to the debt's value and leave them at the pawn (Wayne pays in goods, 2.8). Talker: front him at the rank with dockers watching. Schemer: tell Lee's brother-in-law, who holds a grudge (G08, 2.14), that Lee boasts he will never pay Mickey's nephew; the brother-in-law pays it to stop the shame and tells Lee why. The step "debt paid" is met each time; Ron credits Frank only in the first two unless Frank tells him; Lee holds a grudge against his brother-in-law, not Frank, until someone tells him who started it.

## G4 The town's reading of Frank (law 3 people, law 1 knowing, law 6 recovery)

Why: G26 is ruled in its core (five words, per person, never a number, from deeds held, fading with those who saw nothing new for two game weeks, never with those who hold a deed first hand) but has no working values, no rule for how a told deed weighs against a seen one, no amends, and "no floor and no ceiling" with nothing that keeps a player from a dead end (fail forward). EV 7 needs "no lie she holds unforgiven". No people draft exists to answer it. KCD shows standing kept per place and per group within it; KCD2 shows it mended by amends, but "Forgiveness is not a universal remedy"; CK3 shows the structure: an opinion that is a sum of named marks, each with a value and a duration, "most decay to 0".

The rule.
1. Each person holds a reading of Frank on five words (feared, trusted, quiet, cruel, generous), each from 0 to 1. A reading is made of marks: one mark for each deed the person holds that moves a word, recording the deed, the word, its weight and whether it is held first hand or told.
2. A mark's weight is the strength at which the deed is held: first hand 1.0; told, the strength 2.4.1 already gives (0.8 a hop, floor 0.2). Nothing new: the reading reuses transmission.
3. Each word's value is its heaviest mark plus a third of the rest, at most 1. One great deed dominates; many small ones add.
4. Told marks fade by half over two game weeks in which the person sees or hears nothing new of Frank (G26's figure), and go at 0.1. First-hand marks never fade.
5. Amends (G07). A first-hand cruel mark, or a lie found out (G03), can be answered by an amends deed to that person: paying back what was taken, a spoken apology with something given, a public act on their behalf. Amends add their own mark (generous or trusted) and mark the old one answered, which halves it. A mark is forgiven when it is answered and no new mark on the same word has come from Frank for two game weeks. Forgiving never deletes: those he hurt never forget (G26 kept); it lowers the weight. EV 7's friendship read counts a lie as unforgiven until it is answered and forgiven.
6. What the reading does is G26's (menace works on those who read him feared; trust opens confidences, credit and witnesses; quiet keeps him unsuspected; cruel brings lies to please and avoidance; generous lowers prices for the low-greed and invites asks from the high-greed). A word counts for a person once it reaches 0.5. Menace still meets nerve (2.14): it works when feared is at or above that person's nerve.
7. No dead end. Whatever the readings, three ways always remain with anyone: money for a small act by the fold rule (G04, by greed); a go-between the person reads better (his decision 33, R14); and the ghost route, which needs no reading. Strangers and newcomers read him neutral until told.

The data. Per person: up to 24 marks (deed, word, weight, first hand or told, answered, date), oldest told marks dropped first. Per person the five values are computed, not stored.

The player's hands and what he sees. Never a number. The town shows it: greetings and names (2.12.4, 7.7), posture (2.4.3), who crosses the road, lines in the five heaviest scenes by reading (his decision 36). Frank's own reading at the back of the Ledger (6.2.5) sums it by place in his words: "On Quay Street they take me for a soft touch. On the fish dock they cross over."

Numbers. To be run in temper_sim (2.14) with a seeded week of deeds: the same deeds give at least three distinct readings along one street (tempers differ); a told deed three hops out (0.8 cubed, about 0.51) just reaches a word on its own; the third-of-the-rest rule and the 24-mark cap are balance figures.

Edges. A deed Frank did not do but is believed to have done (a frame, a rumour) makes marks the same way; the truth, told and believed (G03), answers them as amends do. A person who has never heard of Frank holds no marks.

Stage. Stage 1: first-hand marks and rule 6 (the opening's menace and trust need them). Stage 2: told marks and fading. Stage 3: amends and forgiveness, EV 7's read.

Combination test. For two weeks Frank pays Julie Kettlewell's debt and carries the grocer's boxes on Quay Street, and on the fish dock beats Lee Drewery in front of six dockers. Julie holds a generous mark at 1.0. Lee's beating reaches her three hops out: a cruel mark at about 0.51; her reading is generous, a little cruel; she still confides. A docker who saw it holds cruel at 1.0 for good; menace works on him only if his nerve is 1.0 or under, and he lies to please. Frank sends Lee £10 and says sorry in front of the same dockers: the cruel mark is answered and halved for Lee; two quiet weeks later it is forgiven, though Lee never forgets.

## G5 What people hold of Frank's questions, company and habits (law 1 knowing)

Why: the earlier drill found that Frank's asking is held by nobody and that there is no general "seen with" fact; the drafts answer only outings (C6.1: an outing seen is told) and rivals' joint acts (R1.3). G23's "people with an ask come to his corner table at ten" has no fact behind it. Dwarf Fortress spreads rumours of events but keeps no record that anyone asked; LEDGER goes one step further, by the same memory it already has.

Depth follows play: three new kinds of held fact, no new system.

The rule.
1. Asked about. When Frank asks a person about a person, a place or a deed (RT-14 already reads a question and its subject), the person holds "Frank asked me about X" and so does anyone in earshot (2.2.4). It is told on by the ordinary rules (2.4.1), most of all to X's own people (ties, G08) and to anyone who asks the holder about X. Small talk makes no such fact.
2. Seen with. When Frank and a named person are seen together by someone who knows either (sharing an act, talking more than a game minute, at one table, riding in his car other than as a fare), the watcher holds "Frank seen with Y". C6.1's outing rule becomes the case of this rule; R1.3's joint acts another.
3. Usually found. When someone has seen Frank at the same place in the same two-hour band on three days within a game week, he holds "Frank is usually at P at H". Broken for a week, it lapses.
4. These facts move no reading on their own (G4: readings move by deeds). They change what others do: X, told that Frank asked, may change his routine, move goods or tell his firm (G27, G10); a man seen with a believed grass is avoided by his own crew (G07); people with an ask, rivals and Ellis know where to find him (G23, G10).
5. Asking through a go-between makes the go-between the asker (R14).

The data. Three fact kinds in the memory schema: asked (asker, subject, place, time), seen-with (two people, place, time), usually-at (person, place, hour band, last seen). Cooling: asked facts cool as the lightest deed band (2.4.2); seen-with facts do not cool for the two people or their ties; usually-at lapses as above.

The player's hands and what he sees. He learns it as talk, by the overheard rule (G02): "Somebody's been asking after you, Frank. Your name came up at the market." On the People page, what he thinks a person knows of him (L2) gains "knows I asked about" and "has seen me with" where he has reason to think so.

Numbers. Decided, to check in the first loops: one game minute of talk for "seen with"; three days in a week for "usually at"; a two-hour band.

Edges. A typed question the classifier cannot place makes no fact (claim check, 3.6.2). Asking a constable about a crime is held like any other, by the station's rules (G16).

Stage. Stage 1 for asked and seen-with (the memory schema); stage 2 for usually-at.

Combination test. Frank asks a stallholder where his grown son works; the son is one of the Widow's men. The stallholder's mouth is high; on Sunday the son visits (ties) and hears that Mickey's nephew was asking after him. The son tells the Widow's lieutenant and changes the hour he walks home (G27). On Wednesday Frank waits at the old hour and the lane is empty: the town answered his question with a changed routine, and he can ask why.

## G6 The paper gate and the nominee (law 4 exchange, law 1 knowing)

Why: EV 3 makes clean money the gate for big purchases: anything with paper (a lease, a building, a written share, a car on terms, the solicitor) needs book money or a nominee, and every front bought raises what can be washed. No draft says what counts as paper, what happens when notes are offered, or what a nominee is. BS07 (BLIND-CHAOS) passed a nominee only as a go-between who "may keep the place". In 1990 there was no general offence of washing the proceeds of non-drug crime (the CJA 1988 s.93A, "transfer to nominees or otherwise", came in on 15 February 1994; the DTOA 1986 s.24 reached only drug money, and drugs are off-screen), so the gate in 1990 is people who ask where money came from, not a law against washing. From 3 December 1990 the land register was open to anyone for a fee and showed the owner's name.

The rule.
1. Paper. A deal is a paper deal when a written record names the buyer: a lease or freehold, a written share, a car on terms, a bank or building society book, a solicitor's retainer, the cab licence. Everything else (a stall's stock, a share agreed by handshake, a second-hand coat, a bribe) is not paper.
2. The gate. A paper deal is paid from the book (2.7.1) or washed money, never straight from the tin (EV 3). The other side asks how it will be paid, in its own words. Offered notes, he answers by temper (2.14): with law at 0.4 or more he refuses, or asks for it through the bank; below that he may take notes for a price set by his greed and holds "paid in notes" as a fact; over £1,000 in notes is also a fact the inspector can ask about, as P2.4 has it. The world answers in character; the rule has no exception list.
3. The nominee. A nominee is a named person who signs a paper deal in his own name for Frank. Three things decide it:
   - Belief: the other side weighs the nominee's means like a claim (G03) against what he holds of them: a shopkeeper buying a share is believed; a laid-off docker buying a building is not, and the refusal is told by mouth.
   - Consent: the ask is a G04 obligation weighed by the fold rule and R11's weight of an ask.
   - Holding: on paper the holding is the nominee's. He can keep it, sell it, tell, ask a cut each week (G04, greed) or be squeezed by others; what holds it for Frank is his regard, nerve and reading of Frank (G4). The money Frank hands him is cash he must explain in his turn, so a nominee can front only what his own book could carry.
4. Who can see. Paper names whoever signed. After 3 December 1990 a registered building's owner can be learned by anyone who pays to search (Ellis, Agar's office, Alison): the nominee's name, not Frank's. A nominee whose means do not fit his holdings is a question Ellis can ask.
5. Growth. Each front raises the wash (EV 3, E6.2); each believable nominee adds a second set of means. A schemer's empire is held through people, each a knower with a temper.

The data. On each paper deal: the signer, how it was paid, the record that names him. On each person: means a seller would believe (a band from his work and holdings in the people data). On each building: registered or not, registered owner.

The player's hands and what he sees. The agent, solicitor or lot asks in a line how it will be paid; Sheila says what the book will stand ("The book'll carry four hundred, Frank, not a penny more"); the nominee is asked in talk (the pad's claim builder style picker names who and what, L5.4); the Holdings page lists "the caff, in Rita's name"; Money in words keeps the book's limit in one line.

Numbers. The law threshold 0.4 and each person's believed means band are balance figures to be run with the revision's money run; P2.4's £1,000 in notes is kept.

Edges. A nominee who dies leaves the holding to his next of kin (a tie, G08), who may not know or care; Frank deals with the heir by any route. A nominee arrested keeps the holding; his book is examined, and he may tell by nerve. Unregistered buildings are not on the open register.

Stage. Felt lightly at rung 2 (the third car on terms from the book, EC-03), in full at rung 3 (the first front and the solicitor, 2.12.2); build stage 2 for the gate, stage 3 for nominees and the register.

Combination test. Frank wants the empty unit and his book shows £600. He asks Ron's widowed sister to sign; Agar's office (P2.2) does not believe a pensioner buys a unit, refuses, and tells Agar that someone is behind her. Frank asks Rita instead, who runs a front and is believed; the unit is bought in her name with money washed through her shop over three weeks. Rita, greed 0.7, asks £15 a week. On 3 December Alison searches the register on a tip and finds Rita's name; she asks Rita, who lies; Alison holds the lie found out when Rita's shop cannot explain it, and the next paper says only that "a local businesswoman" bought the unit.

## G7 Holding a person (law 3 people, law 1 knowing)

Why: G17 is a rule with his decision 1 (one night at most in a locked room, nothing shown tied, the boot only if he says yes, police never give way), and 1.3.9 lets a downed man be "held one night", but there is no state, place list, ask, release or charge. In 1990 false imprisonment was the unlawful and intentional or reckless restraint of a person's freedom of movement from a place (Law Commission 355, unverified as read); kidnapping the taking or carrying away of a person by force or fraud without consent (Law Commission); holding a man and threatening to keep him to make someone else act was hostage-taking under the Taking of Hostages Act 1982 s.1, for life.

The rule.
1. Held is a person state: who holds him, where, since when, and the latest end: dawn (06:00) of the next day or 12 game hours, whichever comes first (one night at most). The held man keeps every slot and memory.
2. Where: any room the holder can lock (a lock in G14's map data and a key the holder has): the office's back room, a lock-up, a yard shed, a cellar. Never the boot unless he agrees (his decision 1). Nothing is shown tied: he sits, the door is locked; a door left open, he leaves.
3. How: walked under threat (G17: the walk is a menace decision; onlookers weigh it by temper; with high nerve he runs when a moment comes, by the yielded man's rules in 1.3.9), or carried while out (BP01 bodies part).
4. Who notices: anyone who sees the walk holds the deed (2.2.1). His routine misses him; his ties (G08) ask round from his next missed stop; a man not home by morning is a missing person by J9.
5. What it is: holding a man is serious (2.5.2's scale, at 0.5); holding him to make someone else pay or act is grave (at 0.8, with hostage-taking's weight); a held constable is grave. Police never give way to it (G17).
6. Talk while held is talk (the talker route); a demand to a third party is a G04 ask with the held man as the stake, answered by that party's temper and by who answers with force (R13), never at once.
7. Release: Frank opens the door or says go, or the night ends and he walks out. He tells on release by law and grudge (G17) and holds the deed first hand (G4: feared and cruel marks).
8. Anyone can hold anyone by this rule (G10): a rival who holds one of Frank's men sends word by note or call (RT2-19); Frank finds his man missing on Open; the man comes back by morning with what he saw.

The data. On each person: held (by, place, since, ends). In G14's map data: which rooms lock. On the deed table: the two seriousness rows.

The player's hands and what he sees. Lock the door with the key; say what he likes; open it to let him go. The People page and Open show "Lee, in the back room, until morning"; a held man of his shows as "did not come in".

Numbers. To be run: the two seriousness values; how soon ties report (the next morning by default, J9's two weeks for a man nobody expects).

Edges. Hurt or dying while held falls to the body table (G19). Frank himself can be taken by a rival after a lost fight (1.3.9): the same rule, dumped by morning, in control within 60 real seconds (1.3.9).

Stage. Stage 2 (a week that holds).

Combination test. Frank holds a witness overnight in the office's back room so he cannot speak to Ellis. The witness's wife reports him missing at midnight; a tip naming a locked place (J4.2, strong fit) brings a warrant next day. Sheila arrives at nine and hears him (a staff witness). He is let go at dawn, has high law, and reports: the charge is now false imprisonment on top of what he saw. Holding bought one night at a high price.

## G8 Agreements kept in time and place (law 8, law 5 time)

Why: G04 makes promises one ledger, and R1 gives rivals' arrangements a date, but "meet me at the caff at noon tomorrow" makes nothing: no stop in his routine, no line on Open, no cost for missing it. Majora's Mask's notebook shows the idea: each person with a schedule row, each promise marked, each kept promise marked again. LEDGER's Open page already is that notebook.

The rule.
1. When a talk ends with both saying yes to a time and a known place for a meeting, a delivery, a payment or a job, the Core (never the language model) records an appointment: who, what, where, a window of at least two game hours (7.11's rule), and its weight as a promise (G04).
2. The other person adds a stop with that window to his routine and keeps it unless something he holds gives him reason not to: fear, a better offer, a rival's word, low regard (his temper decides, 2.14).
3. It shows on Open (L2) and "wait until" offers it (7.11). He repeats it back in his own words ("Noon at Pat's, then"), which is how the player knows it took.
4. A missed appointment is a broken word, held by the other and told on (G04, R1.2); it moves regard (G07) and lowers trusted by a mark (G4). The one who waits holds the miss, and can ask why.
5. Pad parity: a suggested line "Meet me..." opens the claim builder's picker: a place both know and an hour.

The data. One talk output (appointment: who, what, place, window) and one routine stop type.

The player's hands and what he sees. Said in talk or picked on the pad; repeated back; on Open with the hour; "wait until" offers it.

Numbers. The two-hour window is the blueprint's (7.11); the chance to keep it by temper is a balance figure to run.

Edges. A vague promise ("some time") is a G04 promise with no appointment. A place off the map is answered by a letter or a call (G21). A held man (G7) keeps no appointment, and the miss tells.

Stage. Stage 1 for the talk output; stage 2 for misses and their weight.

Combination test. Frank and Darren agree noon at Pat's caff. At eleven the Widow's man leans on Darren (G10); his fear outweighs his regard, so he stays away. Frank waits until two, holds the broken word, and goes to Darren's door, where Darren's excuse is weighed (G03) and lets slip that the Widow's man was round: a fact Frank learned because a meeting was missed.

## G9 Teaching after the first hour (law 8; fail forward)

Why: the first hour is staged by the minute (7.1) and hints fire on first need or hesitation (BP06), but nothing says how the verbs that open later are first shown: ordering a man, a front's week, pushing the count, tips, plants, the claim builder, a nominee, arrangements. WHERE-PLAYERS-QUIT puts RDR2's larger loss after the opening (12.7 points in the opening against 31.2 in chapter 2; 7.1). Hitman added its guided stories as "an onboarding experience" so players see the approaches, and experienced players then "find their own ways around it".

The rule.
1. Every verb that opens after the first hour has one first-use row: its opening state (often a rung), a named person who would plausibly know, one line or short scene, and a small job that uses it. Within a game day of the verb opening, that person offers, asks or shows it, once.
2. Ignored, the hint rule (BP06) fires once at hesitation, and Open keeps one line in Frank's words. Never a pop-up, never twice.
3. A verb Frank uses first on his own marks its row done; no teaching follows.
4. Dark verbs (killing, holding a person) have no teacher: they are found, not taught.

The data. A first-use table, one row per verb: verb, opening state, teacher, line, first job. Rows the drafts imply: ordering a man (Ron at rung 2: "Want me to see to Darren?"), a front's week (Rita, L3), pushing the count (Sheila, EV 5), a tip (Darren, J4), a plant (Garbutt, R15), the claim builder (the first lie line on the pad, L5.4), an arrangement (Garbutt's sit-down, R1), a nominee (the solicitor at rung 3, G6), the Board's counter (rung 3, 2.12.2), a gun (Rita's introduction, RT2-14).

The player's hands and what he sees. A person, a moment and a job; the Ledger keeps the line.

Numbers. At most one first-use scene a game day; each under 60 real seconds (5.x's scene rule). To be measured in playtests: the share of players who use each verb within two game days of its opening.

Edges. A player who refuses every offer still has the verb; nothing blocks.

Stage. Every stage builds the first-use rows for the verbs it builds: this is what makes it build-shaping.

Combination test. At rung 2 Ron says at the rank, "Want me to see to Darren?" Frank says no and drives off. Next evening Frank stands at the debt book without acting; the hint fires once ("Ron would go round to Darren if I asked"), and Open keeps the line. Two days later Frank sends Wayne to Darren instead; the row is done, and Ron, who offered first, notes he was passed over (regard, G07).

## G10 Story length and skipped nights (law 7, law 5): a simple fact

Why: CLK-06 says the old fix (fewer nights skipped if the median run is short) no longer applies and "its replacement is not written (gap)".

The fact. Nothing adjusts at run time. The calendar is fixed (seven game weeks from 15 October to about 2 December 1990, CLK-06); real hours vary with how much a player sleeps and waits. Any adjustment (a shorter calendar for a fast player, extra beats for a slow one) would be the story pushing the world (law 7) or a new town-wide event. The aim of about 20 hours is the median of the measured playthroughs, reported with its range (three bot playthroughs per style, CLK-06). If the median misses, the fix is content density (7.12, 3.7) and what waiting offers, never the clock.

One test. Bots that sleep at every chance and bots that never skip both finish, and every ending each style allows stays reachable (G29).
