# Depth research: CREW (crew, rivals, alliances)

28 November 2026. Job 3, area 1 of 5. All sources read on 28 Nov 2026 unless marked. Steam reviews were not reachable; critics were coded by hand instead.

## The plain top

What to copy:
- Shadow of War: every rival man has a name, rank and memory; when a captain dies another takes his place; a beaten captain can be turned. The clearest model for a vacuum and for taking a beaten firm's men in.
- Empire of Sin: friendships, rivalries and lovers move loyalty; a rival who hires your man's friend or lover can pull him away.
- Empire of Sin: one relations figure per boss (-500 to 500); wars can draw in other bosses, who may refuse; a war ends with a boss dead or a truce where the loser pays. The plainest model of an alliance.
- Mount and Blade II: a lord defects after spoken persuasion steps and a bargain, bringing his whole clan.
- The Godfather II: made men with specialities on a family tree you can see, the rival's too.

What players hate (critics read: 5 pieces on crew and rivals):
- Defending holdings over and over (3: GameSpot on Shadow of War and on Godfather II, PC Gamer on Shadow of War).
- Crowded menus with no way to sort your men (2).
- Killing the boss outright beats all the management (2).
- Stupid crew in a fight (1 critic, plus 1 guide).
- Random, generic deal menus with rivals (1).
- No critic read discussed betrayal; one guide says betrayals come with no warning sign.

The 1990 facts:
- In 1994 the National Criminal Intelligence Service told MPs that Britain had no "traditional" organised crime, only "enterprise" crime. In 1993 its 403 registered informants were paid £48,000 between them.
- Home Office research (2001) found small networks of four or five core people. One or two hold the money. Runners are paid by a weekly wage, by the job, or as junior partners. Trust comes from family, neighbourhood, school and prison.
- It found violence "bad for business", few turf wars, disputes sometimes settled by a powerful third party, and an arson a week after two women went to the police.
- Manchester: Cheetham Hill took a "tax" from the Moss Side dealers. After Cheetham Hill's leader was shot dead, some time before May 1991, Moss Side accepted the new balance, and Cheetham Hill and Salford had a pact by 1991. A later case against a Salford leader collapsed because no one would testify.
- Glasgow, 1991: a boss's son was shot dead. The boss "declared war" on his old enforcer, and two of the enforcer's friends were found dead on the day of the funeral.

The design questions are numbered C1 to C12 in part C. They ask:
- Is common cause a state, and who sees it?
- What shows standing arrangements?
- Who fills a vacuum, and can Frank take men in?
- What breaks nerve?
- What is unpaid under the split?
- Does a recruit's family and workplace notice?
- Should rivals ever settle through a third party?

## C. The drill's gaps as design questions

The gaps come from the implication drill (A3), checked against BLUEPRINT-02 parts 2.10 and 2.11 on 28 Nov 2026.

C1. Common cause (gap B: no state for Frank in common cause with a rival). Should an alliance be a state of its own on the rival ladder, beside the ladder, or only an agreement made at the sit-down (accept, defy, counter, 3.5.5)? Empire of Sin uses one figure from -500 to 500. Would a spoken agreement with a term (like the two-week hold in 2.10.1) suit canon better?

C2. Who notices (gap B). When Frank and one rival act together, does the third rival learn it the same way he learns of Frank's deeds (sightings and tellings, 2.10.1)? Or does Garbutt sell it?

C3. Showing arrangements (gap B: nothing shows standing arrangements). Where does the player see that a rival is held, owed or allied? Is it a line in the Ledger's People pages, Garbutt's read, or only what men say? Two critics complain of menus that cannot be sorted. How few screens can this live on?

C4. The vacuum (gap B). RT2-05 hands a fallen head's ladder to his lieutenant. In Manchester, a head's death brought a new balance and a pact. In Glasgow, a son's death brought a war. In 1995, a death split a firm and the brother formed a new crew. Should a fallen head lead to one fixed outcome, or to one of a few, chosen by the lieutenant's temper?

C5. Taking a beaten firm in (gap B, story conflict C-342). Shadow of War turns broken captains; Mount and Blade moves a whole clan after persuasion and a bargain. Should Frank take men in one at a time, through the recruit rule (2.11.1, rung 3 recruiting in WI-P06), or as a block? Does a taken man keep his grudge?

C6. Nerve pressure (gap B: "the pressure figure for past his nerve is not yet set", 2.11.2). What adds up to breaking? The research names fear of the police, debt, threats to family, and what happens to grasses (an arson a week later). Should pressure come from named events, each with a size, rather than one figure?

C7. Unpaid under the split (gap B, 2.11.4). If a driver keeps half his takings, is "unpaid" the firm missing fuel and cover? A night cut not paid from the tin? Or both? Research shows wage, per-job and junior-partner pay side by side. Should crew differ in which kind they are on?

C8. A recruit's family and workmates (gap C). When a laid-off regular joins, who notices, and what does each say? Empire of Sin's rival hiring a crewman's lover is one model. Is the family a lever for rivals, for the police, or only talk?

C9. Betrayal signs. 2.11.2 already says a sign comes before a break. No critic read praised surprise betrayal, and the guide read says Shadow of War gives no warning. Does LEDGER keep the sign rule as written for every case?

C10. Defending holdings. Three critics hate repeated defence. How often may rivals hit what Frank holds before it becomes a chore? Should a rival's hit always come from a rung on his ladder, never at random (San Andreas counter-attacks are random)?

C11. Killing the boss. Two critics say killing the rival head beats the management layer. Does a killed head in LEDGER (RT2-05, CS-18) always cost more than it gains, and how is that shown?

C12. Third-party settlement. Research shows disputes settled by a stronger third party, and NCIS paying informers small sums. Should Garbutt, or another named figure, be able to settle a war between Frank and a rival, and at what price? (Content rule: settlement in a café or office, never a pub.)
