# Catching invented details in townspeople's speech

Research note, 28 September 2026, by a separate research helper given the problem (about thirty minutes): the live claim check (ClaimCheck.cs) catches almost every detail the player named and the character confirmed, but only about half of the single invented details a character offers unprompted (17 of 30 in the third independent check of 25 September), and some wrong times; it must answer within a second or two.

The baseline first. Catching 17 of 30 is 57%, but with only 30 items the real rate could be anywhere from about 39% to 73% (95% Wilson interval; the helper's arithmetic). A small change cannot be proven until the test set is larger (section 4).

## 1. Detection

- LLM judges usually miss more than they wrongly flag. In one 2026 study GPT-5-mini as a judge had 85.3% precision but 45.3% recall [20]. On a code benchmark, Claude Haiku 4.5 as a judge scored 0.934 precision and 0.836 recall, and the authors say recall is the bottleneck for every judge [21] (a different domain; transfers weakly). The instruction sets the balance: in RAGTruth, a broadly prompted GPT-4-Turbo judge caught 97.9% of hallucinated replies, but only 46.9% of its flags were correct [8].
- Invented, unsupported information is the most common hallucination type in RAGTruth; subtle cases are caught less often than obvious ones [8].
- Small trained checkers: on LLM-AggreFact, MiniCheck (770M parameters) 75.0% balanced accuracy, Bespoke-MiniCheck-7B 77.4%, Claude 3.5 Sonnet 77.2% [9][10]; MiniCheck costs about 400 times less than GPT-4. LettuceDetect (MIT) 79.2% F1 per reply on RAGTruth, 30-60 examples a second on a GPU [11]. HHEM-2.1-Open about 1.5 s per 2,000 tokens on a CPU, 64-74% on RAGTruth [12]. Lynx (70B) 87.4% on its own benchmark, too big to run live [13]. None was tested on dialogue or on details a speaker offers unprompted.
- Claim-by-claim pipelines (FActScore, SAFE, VeriScore) were built for long text checked against web search; SAFE cost $0.19 per response [18]; VeriScore took 35 minutes per 100 claims without parallel calls [17]. MiniCheck found splitting into atomic claims did not reliably help and cost 2-4 times more [9]; another study found splitting helps weak checkers on simple claims but adds noise for strong ones [16].
- SelfCheckGPT needs 5-20 extra samples (8-62 s in one measurement) [19] and tests consistency, not support: a model can invent the same van every time. It does not suit this case.
- Distrust headline numbers: on hard, natural cases every detector scored 45-62% balanced accuracy [14]; a 2025 re-evaluation found some detectors' scores fell by up to 45.9% under human-aligned judging [15]. Only a test on our own turns counts.

## 2. Prevention

- Anthropic's guide recommends allowing "I don't know", restricting answers to the supplied material, quoting first, and removing any claim with no supporting quote; no measured effects [6].
- "Don't make it up" prompts raised GPT-5-mini's faithfulness from 74.1% to 89.8% at some cost to extraction; on Claude Haiku 4.5 they changed little either way [22]. Prompting alone is unlikely to be enough.
- Chain-of-Verification (draft, check, revise) raised a factuality score from 55.9 to 71.4 while facts per answer fell from 16.6 to 12.3; it needs several calls [23].
- A detective game fed its suspect only facts permitted by a knowledge tree, with separate steps for retrieving, speaking and checking: hallucinated turns fell from 17.8% to 6.3% [24], at the cost of some forced, awkward reveals (one small study, 33 players, Llama 3.1 70B).

## 3. Role-play findings

- RoleFact splits a character's answer into facts, checks each against the character's material, deletes unsupported ones and rewrites the answer in voice: precision on adversarial questions rose from 0.61 to 0.72, time-related hallucination fell 44%; deleting unsupported facts without the other steps reached only 0.65 [25].
- TimeChara: characters are worst at knowing what they should not know (every baseline 51% or less on events after the character's moment; events the character missed also hard). Asking "could this person know this?" before answering helped a lot [26]. This is our "who else was there" case.
- RoleBreak: questions that conflict with the role drive invention, which is the player's leading statement [27].
- CharacterEval scores "knowledge hallucination" but offers no fix [29]. Refusal methods that edit the model's internals need weights the API does not give [28].

## 4. How to evaluate

- Run the old and new checker on the same turns and count the turns where they disagree (a paired McNemar test) [7]. At 5% significance and 80% power (the helper's arithmetic): about 45 invented-detail turns can show recall rising from 57% to 80%; about 120 are needed for 57% to 67%; about 75 clean turns pin a 5% false-alarm rate to within 5 points.
- Label each detail, not each reply, with one rule: a detail is invented unless a memory states it or directly implies it. Tag the kind: person, vehicle, time, clothing, place, count, presence. Two labellers independently, then settle differences; expect some disagreement (RAGTruth's annotators agreed 91.8% per reply, 78.8% per span) [8].
- Report results on natural drafts; planted inventions are useful extra material but easier to catch [9][14].

## 5. Anthropic facts (checked in the docs on 28 September 2026)

- Claude Haiku 4.5 (claude-haiku-4-5-20251001): $1 in, $5 out per million tokens, "fastest", no effort setting; retirement "not sooner than October 15, 2026", no deprecation notice yet, at least 60 days' notice promised [1][3].
- Claude Sonnet 5: $2 in, $10 out, "fast". Claude Opus 5.5: $4 in, $20 out. Cached input a tenth of the normal input price [2].
- A check of about 1,500 tokens in and 150 out: about $0.002 on Haiku, $0.0045 on Sonnet 5 (the helper's arithmetic). No published latency figures: measure.
- Structured outputs are generally available on Haiku 4.5 and Sonnet 5: they guarantee the reply's form, not that it is right; the first call with a new form is slower, then cached for 24 hours [5].
- Citations work on all current models: each memory its own block, the reply points to the blocks it used; pointers guaranteed valid, cited text not charged as output. Citations and structured outputs cannot be used in the same call [4].

## What looks most promising for this case (the helper's reading)

1. Make the checker list every detail before it judges: one structured call lists each specific in the draft (people, objects, vehicles, times, clothing, places, counts, who was present), each with the id of the memory that states it, or "none"; any "none" fails the draft. Evidence: RoleFact [25]; judges lean to high precision, low recall, and the instruction moves that balance [8][20]. Risk: splitting can add noise [16], so one call.
2. Make the speaker cite: each memory a citable block; plain code flags any sentence carrying a specific but citing nothing, before the checker runs. Free and deterministic. Evidence thin: Anthropic's docs [4][6], no published dialogue recall figures.
3. Give the speaker a list of permitted facts each turn, plus a "was I there, could I know?" step. Evidence: the detective game, 17.8% to 6.3% [24]; TimeChara [26]; warnings in the prompt alone did little on Haiku 4.5 [22].
4. Try Sonnet 5 as the checker, or two checks where either can fail a reply: misses dominate, so trade a few false alarms for more catches. Twice the cost; Haiku's retirement date is close. Evidence thin [21]; measure latency.
5. A small local checker (LettuceDetect, MiniCheck or HHEM) only after that: fast, but trained on news and question answering, about 75% on general benchmarks, untested here, and competing with Unreal for the PC.
6. Build the test set first: at least 45 (better 60) invented-detail turns and about 80 clean turns, run paired.
