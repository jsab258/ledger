# Do real witnesses work the way our ladder says?

Research topic 10. You asked for this as validation. Mostly it validates, in one
case more strongly than I expected, and it turns up one thing we are missing that
I think is genuinely good.

## The ladder holds up

The research gives real numbers. Identification accuracy drops from 96 percent at
five metres to 42 percent at twenty. In ideal conditions, daylight, no
distractions, a twenty-second look, accuracy is only about 50 percent at five to
ten metres. At forty metres it is about 30 percent. Past a hundred metres, a
line-up adds nothing at all. And in poor light, past twenty metres,
identification is no better than guessing.

Our ladder detects people at forty metres, reads a face at eight, and recognises
someone you know at twenty-five, with every one of those shrunk by how dark it
is. That sits comfortably inside what the research allows, and where it differs
it errs toward "they could not have seen you", which for a wet sodium-lit town is
the right way to be wrong.

## The bit I expected to find missing, and did not

I went looking for the well-known finding that confident witnesses are not
actually more accurate, expecting to report it as a gap.

It is already in our code, with the reasoning written out. The comment reads:
"MEMORY HARDENS AS IT DECAYS: accuracy falls, confidence rises. A hesitant 'a big
man in a long coat' becomes, after a week of telling it, a certain 'it was Tom
Novak', with no new observation, purely from retelling." Every four retellings
the witness climbs a rung, and when they climb to naming somebody without any new
evidence, the name comes from whoever they already suspected.

That is right, and the research supports it. Whoever wrote that had either read
this literature or guessed it correctly, and either way it is the best-founded
piece of the perception system.

## One number that now has something to check it against

When a witness is unsure, our code gives them a 25 percent chance of naming the
wrong man. The research says that at the distance our code is describing, real
witnesses get it wrong about half the time.

So our witnesses are roughly twice as reliable as real ones.

I am not proposing we change it, for two reasons. The studies measure a stranger
picked out of a line-up after one sighting, which is not quite our situation. And
one summarised figure is not a series, which our own rules say a number should be
set from. What I am recording is that the number was set without any evidence,
and now there is evidence to set it from.

## The thing worth taking, and it is a good one

**The weapon focus effect.** When someone pulls a weapon, witnesses lock onto the
weapon and lose the rest: they "remember the weapon clearly but forget the
attacker's face, clothing, or actions".

Our system already has separate slots for the weapon appearing, for who did it,
and for what happened. Our own file says they are "filled independently". The
research says three of them compete.

This is worth more than a correction. Most crime games ask "did the witness see
it", which cannot express this at all. Ours is already the right shape, and the
missing piece is one link between two slots we already have. And the result is a
real decision for a player: **pull a weapon and you buy a witness who is certain
about the weapon and vague about you.**

## One more gap, and it is the same one as last week

The research says that witnesses who discuss an event with each other corrupt one
another's memories, and a correct memory can be pulled toward a wrong one.

Our gossip passes belief between people but never touches the original
observation underneath. So two witnesses who talk each keep their own version.

This is the same hole topic 9 found from the other end. There I reported that
hearing the same thing from three people is worth no more than hearing it once.
Here it is the other half: not only can our mill not build belief through
repetition, it cannot corrupt the evidence it carries. For a game about what
people believe, that is the same missing mechanism seen twice.

## One thing I am not touching

There is a large, well-replicated finding that people are worse at identifying
faces of a different race, and it accounts for a very large share of real
wrongful convictions. It is true, it is period-appropriate, and building it into
a perception system means writing code that makes a character less accurate
because of somebody's race.

Your content rule allows racism as a fact about characters, never voiced and
never rewarded, which neither clearly permits nor clearly forbids this. I am not
deciding it, and I do not think it is a research question. I have handed it to
the ethics topic later in my queue, because it is the sharpest example that topic
will get: a mechanic that is scientifically correct, thematically apt, and
capable of reading very badly in a review.

## What I could not get

The single most load-bearing number in our whole ladder is the twenty-five metres
at which you recognise someone you know, which our own file calls "the most
characteristic number in the project". **Every study I found measures
identification of a stranger.** Recognising someone familiar is a different task,
almost certainly much better, and I could not find it measured anywhere. That is
the gap I would most want closed on this topic.
