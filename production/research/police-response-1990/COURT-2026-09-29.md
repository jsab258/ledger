# The day he answers: a first appearance for criminal damage, 1990 (research note, 29 September 2026)

For town list bv. A separate helper was given the problem, not a theory, with about thirty minutes and web sources only. Its note is kept here as it gave it. "Inference" marks the helper's own reasoning. This note carries on from CUSTODY-2026-09-29.md, which covers the charge and police bail.

**The question.** The player, a man in his thirties with no record, is charged with putting in a shop window (damage well under £2,000) and bailed by the police to the magistrates' court. What happened on that day in England and Wales in 1990, and what happened if he stayed away? The answer must be plain enough to show a player in a few lines.

**Sources.** The law comes from legislation.gov.uk's point-in-time versions dated 1 February 1991, the earliest the site holds. Nothing found suggests these sections changed between autumn 1990 and then.

## Findings

1. **A small charge, for magistrates only.**
   - Damage of £2,000 or less could only be tried by magistrates (Criminal Justice Act 1988 s.38).
   - The maximum was three months' prison or a £1,000 fine (Magistrates' Courts Act 1980 s.33).
   - The standard scale of fines, levels 1 to 5, was £50, £100, £400, £1,000 and £2,000 (Criminal Justice Act 1982 s.37; SI 1984/447).
2. **The date.** The custody officer set "such time and such place" for the appearance (PACE 1984 s.47(3)). No 1990 rule required the next sitting, so "the next weekday" is lawful. No source was found for the usual gap.
3. **Most such cases ended on the day.**
   - A Home Office survey of time intervals (June 1992) found 70% of summary non-motoring cases finished at the first appearance. That was four points more than in 1991, so about 66% then.
   - Indictable cases went slower. Only 19% finished at the first appearance, with about 2.6 to 2.7 adjournments a case, averaging 26 days (February 1991) and 27 days (February 1992).
   - Source: the NCJRS abstracts of the Home Office Statistical Bulletins.
   - No 1990 figure was found for how long a summary trial took to come on.
4. **Lawyers.**
   - Criminal legal aid was granted only "in the interests of justice". The main test was a likely sentence that would "deprive the accused of his liberty or lead to loss of his livelihood or serious damage to his reputation" (Legal Aid Act 1988 s.22). *Inference:* a first offender facing a fine usually failed that test.
   - Duty solicitors at magistrates' courts were paid for "advice and assistance by way of representation" (Duty Solicitor Remuneration Regulations 1989, SI 1989/341, in force 1 April 1989).
5. **Sentence.**
   - The Magistrates' Association guidelines of 1989 or 1990 were not found online, so there is no guideline fine.
   - 80% of offenders were fined in 1989 (the Home Secretary, David Waddington, Hansard, 20 November 1990).
   - **Compensation.** The court "shall give reasons" if it ordered none. It had to consider his means, and "give preference to compensation" when he could not pay both a fine and compensation (Powers of Criminal Courts Act 1973 s.35, as amended in 1982 and 1988). Magistrates could order at most £2,000 compensation per offence (Magistrates' Courts Act 1980 s.40).
   - **Costs** could be ordered as "just and reasonable", but not normally where the fine was £5 or less (Prosecution of Offences Act 1985 s.18).
6. **Paying.**
   - Instalments were the norm: a £200 fine paid at "£3 a week—or, in the case of a particularly hard bench, £4", with "come back in three weeks" for those who could not manage it (Mr Porter, Hansard, 20 February 1991).
   - The clerk had to serve written notice of the amount, the instalments, the dates, and where and when to pay. No enforcement warrant could issue before that notice (Magistrates' Courts Rules 1981 r.46).
   - Prison for not paying could not be ordered on the day, except where he:
     - could pay at once;
     - was unlikely to stay at an address; or
     - was going to prison anyway.
   - Later, prison for not paying needed a means inquiry with him present and a finding of "wilful refusal or culpable neglect" (Magistrates' Courts Act 1980 s.82).
   - 16,985 people were jailed for fine default in 1989 (Mr Sheerman, Hansard, 20 February 1991).
7. **Not turning up.**
   - Failing to surrender "without reasonable cause" was an offence, and it was for him to prove his reason. The penalty was up to three months or £2,000 (level 5), or up to twelve months if sent to the Crown Court (Bail Act 1976 s.6).
   - The court could issue a warrant for his arrest (s.7(1)).
   - **Endorsed for bail:** the arresting officer released him with a new date, and he need not go to a police station unless sureties were required (Magistrates' Courts Act 1980 s.117).
   - **Not endorsed:** he was held and brought to court. No source was found on how quickly, or on how the police pursued such warrants in practice.
   - **Back in court:** bail "need not be granted" to someone arrested under s.7 (Bail Act Sch. 1 Pt I para 6), and his record on earlier bail counted against him (para 9).
   - **In his absence:** the court could try him, but could not jail him (Magistrates' Courts Act 1980 s.11).
   - No failure-to-appear rate for 1990 was found.

## What it suggests for LEDGER (inference)

- **A guilty plea is dealt with that morning:** a modest fine, compensation for the glass first, possibly costs, paid weekly at a few pounds, with a written notice of how much, when and where.
- **Not guilty:** bail is renewed and a trial set some weeks off. "About a month or more" is safe; a precise figure is unproven.
- **Staying away brings a warrant:**
  - endorsed for bail, it means a knock at the door and a new date;
  - not endorsed, it means a night in the cells and the next court.

  Both are true to 1990, and either way he is less likely to get bail again.
- **The only lawyer he meets** is probably the duty solicitor on the day.
- **No exact fine figure** until the Magistrates' Association guideline is found (a library copy, or Home Office Research and Planning Unit Paper 59, "Unit fines: experiments in four courts", 1990). The statutory ceilings above are firm.
