# Who tells the police, and what the police do: Humberside, 1990

Research note, 29 September 2026. Sources are online only; each claim gives its source and that source's date. The detention clock and the tape-recording rules are in `../british-policing-1988-1992/SUMMARY.md`.

## What the game should do

- **Most offences are never reported.** Give each offence its own chance of being reported. Victims reported about a quarter of vandalism and common assaults, about half of woundings and robberies, and nine in ten burglaries where something was taken.
- **Witnesses report far less than victims:** 29% against 70% on high-crime estates. Fear of reprisal lies behind a fifth of witnesses' silence. A bystander tells the pub, not the police.
- **A shopkeeper reports a smashed window for the insurance.** That brings a uniformed constable and a crime report, not a detective.
- **Four ways to tell the police:** 999, a call to the station, walking in, or stopping an officer. A sighting at the landing becomes an intelligence card, not an inquiry.
- **Response is graded.** Urgent calls get a car quickly; the rest are attended later.
- **The detective sergeant walks the street only after serious crime** (a wounding, a robbery, above all a killing). For a killing there is an incident room, then house-to-house enquiries and statements.
- **Talk can justify an arrest but cannot convict.** A conviction needs a signed statement from a witness who will turn up in court, an identification, physical evidence, or what the suspect says after a caution. In 1990 his silence could not be held against him.
- **"Grassing" carries a cost, and violence between people who know each other is often settled privately.** The town's gossip should always know more than the police do.

## Findings

### Who reports, what, and how

- **Reporting rates, 1991 survey year:** vandalism 27%, burglary with loss 92%, attempted burglary 48%, common assault 25.5%, wounding 47.7%, robbery 47.2%. Violence by an acquaintance was reported 29% of the time and violence by a stranger 37.5%. Overall, 43% of crime was reported, against 31% in 1981. Source: Home Office Statistical Bulletin 18/01, *The 2001 British Crime Survey* (October 2001), Table A2.4.
- **Why victims stayed quiet, 1992 survey:** too trivial or nothing lost, 55%; the police could do nothing, 25%; fear of reprisal, 2% overall but 9% for assaults. Source: Maynard, *Witness Intimidation*, Home Office Crime Detection and Prevention Series 55 (1994).
- **Five high-crime estates:** victims reported 70% of incidents and witnesses 29%. Fear of intimidation lay behind 22% of the incidents witnesses kept quiet about. After reporting, 13% of victims and 9% of witnesses were intimidated. Source: Maynard, 1994.
- **How people reached the police:** 999, a call to the local station, a visit to a station, or an officer in the street. Crimes between relatives, friends and neighbours often went unreported, and 20% of those victims feared reprisal. Insurance drove the reporting of minor property crime. Over 80% were satisfied with how fast the police came; a third felt they showed too little interest. Source: Skogan, Home Office Research Study 134 (1994), pp. viii–ix and 2.
- **About 85% of recorded crime came from the public** (Clarke and Hough, Home Office Research Study 79, 1984, NCJRS summary).

### What the police do first

- **Graded response:** people usually took 10 minutes or more to report, so speed rarely caught anyone. Forces sent a car quickly to urgent calls and scheduled the rest. Panda cars had cost the police "local news and gossip". Source: Clarke and Hough, 1984.
- **Unit beat policing dates from 1967** (Hansard, 22 June 1967). A station "collator" kept index cards on local criminals (Adler, ex-officer, Substack, 18 January 2026; secondary).
- **Criminal damage of £2,000 or less** was triable only by magistrates (Criminal Justice Act 1988, s.38).
- **At a burglary, uniformed officers made the first visit** and the house-to-house enquiries. Source: Home Office Research Study 264 (2003), which describes practice after our period.

### When CID comes, and what a detective needs

- **Most crimes are solved because someone names the offender "from the outset"** (Clarke and Hough, 1984). In detected burglaries, 43% of offenders were caught at or near the scene and 34% were found by questioning witnesses there. Only 18% of detections came from enquiries lasting over 24 hours without leads from the scene. Source: Coupe and Griffiths (1996) and Burrows (1986), both cited in HORS 264.
- **Killings:** ACPO's standard incident-room procedures date from 1985. "By the mid-1980s all UK forces were using" the HOLMES computer system. A senior investigating officer leads, and the early "golden hours" count most. Source: Mooney, *Journal of Homicide and Major Incident Investigation* 6(1), Spring 2010.
- **Identification:** Code D covered identification parades, confrontations and group identifications, run by an officer not on the case. At parades in July 1993, 18% of witnesses were afraid of meeting the suspect. Source: Maynard, 1994.
- **The caution before 1994** said the suspect need not say anything, but that what he did say might be given in evidence. No inference could be drawn from silence at the station. Source: Hansard, House of Lords, 25 April 1994. The codes were revised in April 1991 to tell suspects that legal advice was free (HORS 129, 1992; seen only as a search abstract).

### How far talk moves the police

- **Suspicion can rest on hearsay:** suspicion is "a state of conjecture or surmise where proof is lacking", and it may rest on hearsay or a tip-off (*Hussien v Chong Fook Kam*, Privy Council, 1969).
- **Frightened witnesses:** a statement made to the police could be read in court if the witness stayed away "through fear" (Criminal Justice Act 1988, s.23(3), as enacted).

### Willingness to go to the police

- **The cost of grassing:** on a Salford estate, whom a person trusted shaped their daily life, and being known as a grass carried risk. Source: Evans, Fraser and Walklate, *Sociological Review* 44(3), August 1996. I saw only the abstract; the detail of injury and shaming comes from a search summary.
- **Confidence was falling:** 86% rated the police good in 1988, 81% in 1992 (HORS 134 abstract).

### Humberside

- Formed on 1 April 1974 with 1,700 officers; headquarters in Hull. Chief Constables: David Hall, 1976–1991; Tony Leonard, 1991–1999. Source: Wikipedia, read 29 September 2026.
