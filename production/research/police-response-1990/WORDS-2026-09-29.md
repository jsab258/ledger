# The words said to him, 1990

Research note, 29 September 2026. Code C, first edition (1986 to March 1991), from a 1985 printing [1], checked against 1991 [2] and Zander 1990 [3]; quotes matched end to end in scan snippets. "Inferred" marks my reasoning.

## Arrest and interview

- There are no set words for the arrest. He must be told he is under arrest, and why, "at the time of, or as soon as is practicable after" [5, s.28].
- **The caution**, Code C para 10.4 [1, 3], unchanged in 1991 [2]: "You do not have to say anything unless you wish to do so, but what you say may be given in evidence." It may be voiced.
- It is given on arrest (10.3) and before questions (10.1). "Minor deviations" are allowed if "the sense of the caution is preserved". If he does not understand, the officer explains it "in his own words" (Note 10C). No short version was found.

## Rights at the station

- The custody officer tells him of three rights "and that they need not be exercised immediately": to have someone told, to see a solicitor, and to read the codes (3.1) [1].
- A written notice gives these rights, a copy of the custody record, and the caution; he signs "to acknowledge receipt" (3.2). In writing: the duty solicitor "will always be free of charge" (section 6) [1].
- The Met's form before April 1991, read aloud [4]: "You have the right to:- 1. Have someone informed of your arrest. 2. Consult a solicitor. 3. Consult a copy of the Codes of Practice. You may do any of these things now, but if you do not, you may still do so later." Humberside's own form: not found; same content (inferred).

## Charge

- Para 16.2 (numbered from its position) [1]: "When a person is charged with or informed that he may be prosecuted for an offence he shall be cautioned in the terms of paragraph 10.4 above."
- 16.3: a written notice states the offence "in simple terms" and "the precise offence in law", and begins: "You are charged with the offence(s) shown below. You do not have to say anything unless you wish to do so, but what you say may be given in evidence." [1, 2]
- The sergeant reads the charge and the caution, then writes down any reply (inferred).

## Bail

- Bail is a duty to come to court, or back to the station, when the custody officer says [5, s.47(3)].
- He is "under a duty to surrender to custody" [6, s.3(1)]. Failing "without reasonable cause" is an offence; he must prove his reason (s.6). At February 1991: three months or a level 5 fine, or twelve months if committed for sentence.
- The record gave the place, date and time [7]; he could ask for a copy (s.5). The form's words were not found; he signed it (inferred).

## A formal caution for an offence

- No statute. It needed enough evidence to prosecute, an admission and informed consent [8, 9]. He had to "understand the significance of the caution" [9].
- A senior officer in uniform gave it at a police station (Circular 59/1990, annex B, cited in [8]); a later text says an inspector or above [10]. It was recorded and could be "cited in subsequent court proceedings" (1994 circular, in [9]).
- The words and form were not found. Likely: the offence, a warning it may be raised in court, his signature (inferred).
