# Arrest or summons: England, 1990

Research note, 29 September 2026. Online sources, each with its date.

## Summary

- **Criminal damage, window under £2,000: arrest.** It carries ten years, and PACE still counts it as arrestable when small value makes it summary-only (my reading of the statute).
- **Common assault: summons, normally.** Six months at most, so not arrestable. Arrest only under the general arrest conditions or for breach of the peace; once the attacker has gone, a warrant.
- **Section 20 wounding / GBH: arrest** (five years). **Section 18: arrest** (life).
- **Robbery: arrest** (life).
- **Murder: arrest** (sentence fixed by law).
- **A detective needs reasonable suspicion, not a witness statement.** Hearsay and information from others can found it.

## Findings

**Arrestable offences.** PACE s.24(1), as enacted (1984), covers offences "for which the sentence is fixed by law" and those carrying five years for an adult with no record "(or might be so sentenced but for the restrictions imposed by section 33 of the Magistrates' Courts Act 1980)", plus a short list. A constable may arrest anyone he reasonably suspects (s.24(6)); any citizen may arrest someone committing or guilty of one (s.24(4)-(5)). Unchanged on 1 February 1991 (legislation.gov.uk).

**Criminal damage.** Ten years (Criminal Damage Act 1971 s.4, enacted). The Criminal Justice Act 1988 s.38 raised the summary-only limit in MCA 1980 s.22 from £400 to £2,000, and MCA 1980 s.33 then caps the sentence at three months. PACE's words about s.33 cover exactly this case: my reading, as no secondary source I found says it outright.

**Common assault.** CJA 1988 s.39 (enacted): summary only, six months. In the Bill committee on 24 June 2004 (TheyWorkForYou), the minister Chris Leslie said that if the offender "has left the scene, the police must obtain an arrest warrant". Dominic Grieve said it "has not been an arrestable offence in the past", though an officer seeing a breach of the peace may arrest.

**General arrest conditions (PACE s.25, enacted).** For a non-arrestable offence, a constable may arrest if a summons "is impracticable or inappropriate" because:
- the name is unknown and not readily found, or is reasonably doubted;
- no satisfactory address is given, or it is doubted;
- arrest is needed to prevent injury to anyone, damage to property, an offence against public decency or obstruction of the highway;
- arrest is needed to protect a child or other vulnerable person.

**Wounding, robbery, murder.** Section 20: five years (Wikipedia, "Grievous bodily harm"). Section 18: "penal servitude for life" on 1 February 1991 (legislation.gov.uk). Robbery: life (Theft Act 1968 s.8, enacted). Murder: mandatory life (1965 Act s.1).

**Reasonable suspicion.**
- Hussien v Chong Fook Kam (Privy Council, 1969; [1970] AC 942): suspicion is "a state of conjecture or surmise where proof is lacking". It may rest on matters that are not admissible in evidence (vLex).
- Castorina v Chief Constable of Surrey (Court of Appeal, 10 June 1988): detectives arrested a sacked employee for a burglary at her old firm, on the managing director's account and signs of an inside job, without questioning her first. Lawful, though they "could have made further enquiries" (vLex; Revision World).
- O'Hara (House of Lords, 12 December 1996, so after 1990) applied Hussien: hearsay, a tip-off or other officers' information can found suspicion; a bare order from a superior cannot (Irish Times, 14 April 1997).
