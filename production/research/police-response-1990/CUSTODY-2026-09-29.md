# After the arrest: custody, charge and bail, 1990

Research note, 29 September 2026. PACE 1984 as enacted; Code C revised 1 April 1991 [6]. "Inferred" marks my reasoning.

## For the game

- Arrest costs hours, not the game (inferred): a shop window, home that evening and a fine later; wounding or robbery, perhaps kept for court; a killing, kept.
- Silence is free in court but blocks most cautions, which need an admission: 5% of silent suspects were cautioned against 20% who answered [8].

## The caution

- From memory, unconfirmed: "You do not have to say anything unless you wish to do so, but what you say may be given in evidence." Looser versions circulate [12]; voice nothing yet.

## At the station

- The custody officer, at least a sergeant and not on the case [1, s.36], records "everything which a person has with him". Clothes may be kept against injury, damage or escape, or as possible "evidence relating to an offence" [1, s.54]: the coat can go.
- Rights: someone told "as soon as is practicable" [1, s.56]; a solicitor privately "at any time" [1, s.58]; a superintendent may delay either to 36 hours, serious arrestable offences only. Before April 1991, 24% asked for a solicitor; after, 32%, once told advice was free. A fifth asked for someone to be told [6].
- Fingerprints: written consent, a superintendent's authority, or after charge [1, s.61].

## How long

- 24 hours from arrival [1, s.41]. A superintendent may extend to 36, magistrates to 96, only for a serious arrestable offence [1, ss.42, 44]: murder always; others causing death, serious injury, substantial gain or serious loss [1, s.116]. A shop window: 24 at most (inferred).
- Mean hold just over 5 hours, median 3 h 19 min; 1% still held at 24 [5]. By offence [5]: criminal damage 4.9 (charged 5.9, cautioned 2.0); violence 6.7 (charged 8.2); robbery 10.3 (charged 12.3). Very serious offences, 1993–94: about 22 [7].

## Bail

- Uncharged: bail to return [1, s.47]; 12% were [5].
- Charged: released unless identity is doubted or detention prevents injury, damage, absconding or interference [1, s.38]. No conditions until the 1994 Act [7].
- Kept: before magistrates at "the first sitting after he is charged" [1, s.46]; weekend arrests wait for Monday; mean hold after charge nearly 16 hours [5]. Custody remands: eight clear days at most [2].

## Outcomes

- Adults held for crime: 56% charged, 10% summonsed, 7% cautioned [5]. Criminal damage, 1993–94: 50% charged, 19% cautioned, 22% no further action [7]. Cautioning standards: Circular 59/1990 [9].
- Criminal damage under £2,000: magistrates only, at most three months or £1,000 (February 1991) [2]. First offender: a fine and paying for the glass (inferred). In 1990, 17% of adult men sentenced for indictable crime got immediate custody [9].
- Police bail refused after charge, 1993–94: 28% overall, 40% for robbery and violence, over 60% for very serious offences [7]. Only a 2009 Act reserved murder bail to Crown Court judges [4], so 1990 magistrates could grant it, rarely (inferred).

## How the town hears

- A police car at the cab office (inferred). From arrest the case is "active" for contempt [3]: "a man is helping police" until charge, then name, age and address in the court column (inferred; today's practice [11]). Papers were dropping full court coverage [10]; a column is likely (inferred).

Not found: Code C 1986's wording; Humberside figures; a 1990 Hull or Grimsby court column.
