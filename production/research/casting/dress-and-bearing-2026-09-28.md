# What eleven people in a 1990 northern port town wore, and how people moved

Research note, 28 September 2026, by a separate research helper given the problem (about thirty minutes): written casting sheets for the eleven principals without one need what each wears day to day and how people of that kind carried themselves. The project's earlier clothing research (production/art/atlas-02/research/adult-clothing-by-occupation.md) names as holes men's business dress, priests outside a service, teachers, and women's everyday dress by age.

How to read it: [S n] means the point comes from source n; [I] means the helper's inference. "Summary only" in the source list means the claim was seen in a search summary but the page could not be opened. No garment makers are named (canon: every brand is fictional).

1. **Tom, 32.** Nothing specific to a man of Polish descent. By the post-war years the two-piece suit had become the norm [S14], so his funeral suit is a plain dark two-piece with a white shirt and black tie [I]. Day to day: jeans (young men wore tapered, high-waisted cuts [S8]), a plain jumper or sweatshirt, leather shoes or trainers, a zip-front nylon or cotton jacket for rain [I]. In the 1980s and 1990s a clean shave signalled a man could be trusted [S11]. With one suitcase he should be seen in the same few clothes again and again [I]. About three men in ten smoked in 1990 [S25].

2. **Detective sergeant, woman, 40.** Uniformed policewomen wore skirts and carried handbags until the skirts were phased out in the early 1990s [S21]. Trousers could still draw disapproval: a teacher recalled in 1998 being told "teachers didn't wear trousers" [S22]. Navy, grey or pastel skirt suits with shoulder pads stayed common at work into the mid-1990s [S8], the pads smaller by the late 1980s [S8]. So: a navy or grey jacket and skirt, blouse, tights, low court shoes or flat boots for walking, a shoulder bag, a belted raincoat, short practical hair [I]. Prime Suspect (first shown April 1991) is the nearest screen reference [S24]; no written description of its costumes found.

3. **Solicitor and alderman, 62.** The waistcoat had been fading since the war [S14], so a three-piece at 62 marks him as old-fashioned and old money [I]. The bowler "disappeared" in the 1980s [S13]; a man born in 1928 might keep a felt hat as a holdover, but bareheaded with a black umbrella is the safer default [I]. Outdoors, a knee-length dark wool overcoat: the velvet-collared Chesterfield was standard for smart men from the 1920s to the 1960s, the years he learned to dress [S15]. Leather gloves, black lace-ups, perhaps a watch chain [I].

4. **Trawler-owner's widow, 57.** In 1990 fur was a provocation: an anti-fur campaign cut UK fur sales from about £80m (1985) to about £4m (1989), and Harrods closed its fur department in 1990 [S5, S6]; a journalist wearing fur in November 1989 got "a torrent of abuse" [S7]. Her fur collar belongs on an older wool coat, bought years earlier and worn without caring [I]. Showy gold jewellery was common among both sexes in 1980s working-class style [S3], and gold sovereign rings and pendants came back in the 1970s and 1980s [S4]. Big, heavily styled hair carried into the early 1990s [S8], so a lacquered blonde set fits [I]. A 1977 Hull photograph shows fish-factory women in winter coats, white headscarves and wellingtons [S19]; her tailored suit and gold show how far she has come from them [I].

5. **Young criminal, 27, Grimsby.** The mullet was "everywhere" in 1980s Britain [S1] and still worn in 1990 (Chris Waddle kept his for most of that summer's World Cup [S2]), but the word "mullet" only caught on in 1994 [S1], so no one in the game should say it [I]. He grew up in the football-casual scene of expensive European sportswear and showy gold, fading into rave by 1988 [S3]. A soft leather blouson, a polo shirt, pleated trousers, loafers, a heavy gold chain and a sovereign ring are plausible for loud money in 1990; no dated source for the leather blouson itself [I].

6. **Old widow, 78.** A headscarf tied under the chin; in the wet a clear plastic rain hood over it or over set hair, a habit of that generation [S18]. Coronation Street's Hilda Ogden (on screen until 1987) wore a mac, a pinny and a headscarf over curlers most of the day [S17]. In 1988 a fifth of British adults had no natural teeth, about a third of the unskilled class [S20]; at 78 and working class, very probably full dentures [I]. Indoors: a nylon overall over a skirt and cardigan, slippers. Outdoors: a wool or tweed coat, fur-lined ankle boots, a shopping bag [I].

7. **Woman returning, 38.** In an office job, the navy or grey skirt suit with modest shoulder pads [S8]; a nurse off duty dresses like any professional woman [I]. For the funeral trip: a dark wool coat or belted raincoat, a dark suit or dress, tights, court shoes, a holdall [I]. Her hair shorter and less big than the local women's, marking her as from away [I].

8. **Priest, 61.** The cassock was everyday clerical wear until the 1960s, then largely replaced by a black suit with black shirt and clerical collar [S16], a full ring or a white tab, the "dog collar" [S16]. A man trained in the 1950s wears the collar all day [I]. Round the parish: black trousers, a black or grey pullover or cardigan over the clerical shirt, black shoes, a dark raincoat, perhaps a black beret [I]. No British source for 1990.

9. **Reporter, woman, 30.** A journalist recalling 1980s newsrooms: "big helmet hair, big earrings and even bigger shoulder pads", smoking at the desk, contact books a reporter's most precious possession [S23] (Australian, matching the British type [I]). On the glasses: by the late 1980s fashionable frames were smaller and rounder, often tortoiseshell [S8]; huge frames in 1990 look a few years behind, fine for a provincial reporter if chosen on purpose [I]. A spiral-top shorthand notebook, a biro, a shoulder bag, flat boots, a belted mac [I].

10. **Teacher, man, 33.** A 1998 article records the stereotype: a cord jacket with leather-patched elbows and faded cord trousers [S22]. At work that jacket, shirt and tie, V-neck jumper [I]. Off duty, with the school shut: jeans or cords, a knitted jumper, an anorak or quilted jacket, desert boots or trainers [I].

11. **Go-between, 48.** The 1970s and 1980s were the moustache's heyday; the 1990s preferred a clean upper lip [S12], so a neat moustache in 1990 says he settled his look in the 1970s [I]. A car coat falls between a jacket and a three-quarter coat; in Britain the sheepskin version belonged to football managers and to the detectives in The Sweeney, cheaper ones were cotton suedette or brushed nylon lined with acrylic pile [S9]. The TV wheeler-dealer's camel coat and trilby (Minder, 1979 to 1994) [S10] is the cliché to steer away from [I]. Driving gloves and slip-on shoes [I].

**How people moved and stood.** In 1990, 31% of men and 29% of women smoked, against about 20% of adults in 2011 [S25]. A 2006 British Council test found city walking about 10% faster than early-1990s measurements [S26] (big cities, not northern towns). People using a smartphone while walking bend their heads about 31 to 39 degrees, against roughly level without one [S27]: in 1990 heads were up and people looked at each other [I]. On Grimsby fish dock in 1990 most workers "hid behind fish boxes" or ran off from a photographer [S28]. Nothing written and dated on hands in pockets, stance or gait: that has to come from looking at the photographs themselves, Alec Gill's Hessle Road archive, 1971 to 1987 [S29], and Thornton's 1990 Grimsby series [S28]. The best next source, not read: a scanned Grattan autumn/winter 1990 catalogue [S30].
