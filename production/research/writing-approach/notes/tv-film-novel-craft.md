# How long-form stories get made outside games (researched 2026-11-23)

Method note: only pages actually fetched are cited. Fetches were summarised by a small model, so quotes are as reported by that summary, not checked against originals. Not reached: Mantel's Paris Review interview (fetch failed), Gilligan interviews with detail on index cards, Creativity Inc itself, Egri's text, McKee's text. Nothing was found on the proportion of first drafts kept.

## What separates great from competent

1. The great work is rewritten far more, and the rewriting is organised. Armstrong says Succession scripts all pass through him for tone, often with "significant reworking" (Chortle). A Succession production-notes piece quotes an unnamed speaker: "every other episode ... at least 30 drafts, usually 50" (Ashore; speaker unattributed, treat as anecdote). Pixar's Catmull: "all of our films start out as disasters" and the job is to go "from suck to not-suck" (Salon; Fast Company). Competent work stops at the first workable draft.

2. A named, candid, authority-free check on the work. Pixar's Braintrust meets roughly every few months per film; its rule is candour and that it "should have no authority", so the director is free to ignore notes (Fast Company; First Round). Pixar tried copying it for technical teams and it failed, because the group could not direct the director and so produced fewer ideas (First Round). Lesson: the value is a trusted, non-controlling peer group of storytellers, not a committee.

3. One person with final say, but argument is encouraged. Simon made the final call yet "wanted to be persuaded"; Simon and Ed Burns argued episode construction, one row lasting over 90 minutes in front of the room (Looper/Slashfilm). Armstrong rewrites everything himself for consistent tone. Great shows are single-voiced even when many people write.

4. Real-world material is the source of specificity. Simon hires novelists and reporters rather than TV aspirants; named: Zorzi (Sun reporter), Evan Wright (embedded in Iraq), Lolis Eric Elie, Bourdain for restaurant scenes (BuzzFeed News). The Wire's team consulted active detectives and journalists and spent months on subjects like the port; Simon turned down a Season 6 idea for lack of Spanish, local contacts and research time (Slate). Burns called attention to detail a "down payment to the audience" that buys later suspension of disbelief (Looper). Price immersed himself in Jersey City housing projects before Clockers, on the belief that "talent travels" (Paris Review).

5. The whole is designed before the parts. Simon and Burns settled on five seasons in Seasons 1-2; the room fixes each season's theme first, then where each character ends (Slate). Weiner wrote a separate season-long arc per character, overlaid them for thematic clash or echo, and charted checkpoints by episode (Slashfilm on Weiner). Ellroy writes a 300 to 400 page present-tense outline before the novel (Granneman/Paris Review). Counter-case: Better Call Saul's final season was reverse-engineered from characters and what already existed, not planned events (Yahoo UK); Armstrong kept Succession scripts open ("so many maybes") until forced to close (Ashore). Planning the theme and ending, while leaving the plot loose, is common to both.

6. Waste is reused. Sopranos writers kept a wall of ideas and "ended up making soup out of everything" (LitHub). Pixar's early versions show whole premises discarded and fragments kept (Den of Geek). Great rooms discard fast and salvage.

7. The weakest-seeming problem is fixed at the structure level, not by polish. Toy Story 2 "didn't work emotionally" and was restarted (Salon). Inside Out worked only after Joy's companion changed from Fear to Sadness; Ratatouille's director was replaced and the story re-centred (Den of Geek). Brave's director changed too. Competent teams polish; great teams are willing to rebuild.

8. Testing on people who have never seen it. Catmull: Pixar holds at least one recruited-audience screening per film and about 90 percent of the useful feedback comes from "sitting with somebody who's never seen it before" (Salon). Jamin: in TV, a script with trouble shows when "the energy will leave the room" at the table read (Jamin).

9. Editing as subtraction and judgement. Lish cut Carver stories to "three lines left"; some readers call it genius, Stephen King "a cheat" (Paris Review on Lish). Disagreement is live, so an editor's power is a risk as well as a gift.

10. Daily, modest, finished output. Greene aimed at about 500 words a day, stopped mid-scene, later 300, and reread each morning's work before sleeping on it (Open Culture). Output stayed small and the novel took about a year including revision.

11. Character-led structure rather than plot-led. Egri: "Character is all" (Narrative Magazine blurb). Truby's model starts from a weakness and need (psychological and moral), a desire, a true opponent who attacks that specific weakness, and a moral argument shown through choices under pressure (scriptreader.ai summary). McKee: each scene must turn on a consequential value change; a beat you can remove without damage "causes no change" (Shortform).

12. The scene as a unit that turns. Story Grid: every scene has an inciting incident, progressive complications, a crisis (choice between two goods or two bads), climax and resolution, with a literal action on top of an "essential action" (Creative Penn). Same idea as McKee, different words; the agreement across systems is the evidence, not any one system.

13. Real structure is flexible, and the best writers say so. See the disagreement list below.

## Where experts disagree with formula

- Charlie Kaufman: "just not interested in the formula." Guillermo Arriaga: three acts a "one trick pony." Godard: beginning, middle and end "but not necessarily in that order" (Script Mag).
- John August: gurus "manipulate the examples to prove" the theory; McKee may teach well but there is no "One True Way"; "internalize what makes sense ... and chuck the rest" (John August).
- Defenders: Moreno says theories describe patterns writers use by instinct, but forcing events onto set page numbers "gives theory a bad name" (Script Mag).
- No hard data was found on whether formula-following films perform better or worse. Script Mag states it has none.
- Best use: as a diagnostic after drafting (does this scene turn? does the hero have a need?), not a template before it. Inference from the sources, not a published finding.

## Process facts

TV rooms
- Sequence (Script Mag, Haywood): pitches approved by showrunner; then beats, breaking, blending; then outline. Stories lettered A, B, C, D (D may be a "runner"). Beats go on a whiteboard; "best-idea-wins". The showrunner is often away while stories are broken, then hears each pitched beat by beat and may send writers back.
- Jamin (28 years a TV writer/showrunner): breaking a story takes a full staff 3 to 5 days; one writer or pair writes outline, first draft, sometimes a second; most notes come from the showrunner; after draft two the room rewrites together into a "Table Draft" which is the one read by actors; rewritten again after the read.
- Armstrong (Succession): 8 or 9 writers, "four or five months" in the room, first month open exploration; "A writers' room is not a place to do any writing in"; any writer can draft any episode; all scripts reworked by him; writers attend shoots (Chortle). Ashore reports a writers' room also ran on location during filming.
- Simon: same writers reused; novelists preferred; Pelecanos, Price, Lehane named in the room with Mills, Zorzi, Overmyer (Slate, Slashfilm). About 95 percent of finished dialogue matches the script; a writer is always on set to judge ad-libs; actors are not told the larger arc (Slate). Simon learned episodic TV on Homicide (Slashfilm).
- Gilligan (Breaking Bad): stays several episodes ahead, gives notes on every episode, directed the pilot, trims director's cuts that run 10 to 12 minutes over (No Film School). Better Call Saul: post-production is "the final rewrite", most common change is cutting dialogue; 50 prior episodes to stay consistent with in the last season (Yahoo UK).
- Sopranos: ideas pitched through a chain (writer to senior writer to Chase); wall storyboard; Chase made hiring calls (LitHub).
- Mad Men: Weiner's per-character arcs and episode checkpoints (Slashfilm).

Film
- Up Close and Personal: Dunne and Didion wrote 27 drafts over about eight years, plus a few more by others, driven by executive, producer, director and star demands (Reason). Top screenwriters paid six figures a week for key rewrites; a few hundred in the top tier (Reason, 1997 figures). Final Draft says nearly every script that becomes a film goes through "10+ rewrites" (unsourced claim by a software vendor, weak).
- Coverage: a paid reader writes summary, characters, themes, structure, style, then a verdict: Recommend, Strong Consider, Consider, Consider with Reservations, Pass; purpose is to let executives decide whether to read in full (Final Draft). A Pass may reflect market fit rather than quality.
- Pixar: Braintrust grew from the five Toy Story leads (Lasseter, Stanton, Docter, Unkrich, Ranft); meets about every few months per film; Catmull attends nearly all and guards the candour agreement; meetings are not recorded and notes "never catch what's in the room" (Fast Company; Salon). Disney Animation was given the same mechanism, with directors told they need not take any note (First Round). Story-reel screenings: not confirmed by any page read; only the recruited-audience screening and the every-few-months Braintrust review are.
- Early Pixar versions: Toy Story's lead began as Tinny; Woody was an early villain; Toy Story 2's 1996 first draft had no Jessie; Dory began as male Gill; Up began as two princes in a floating city (Den of Geek).

Novels
- Lish: Esquire fiction editor 1969 to 1977, Knopf until 1995; drastic cutting and rewriting of Carver; Carver's Collected Stories (2009) printed some stories in both versions (Paris Review).
- Ellroy: shorthand synopsis (six days for Blood's a Rover), character summary, design statement, chapter outline in present tense; outline for Blood's a Rover about 397 pages, eight months; Cold Six Thousand outline 345 pages; five outline pages a day, about eight novel pages (Granneman).
- Greene: 500 words a day five days a week for about twenty years, small black notebook, more than 50 books, dream journal for blocks (Open Culture; the source partly paraphrases a character in The End of the Affair, so treat the habits as secondhand).
- Price: immersion, orange notebooks, nearly 100 typescript pages at interview time (Paris Review).
- Not reached: Perkins, Mantel, Pelecanos.

## Judging a script before an audience
Ranked by what the sources support: showrunner notes and room pitching; table read (watch the room's energy, per Jamin); coverage by readers with a tiered verdict; Braintrust candid review every few months; recruited-audience screenings ("90 percent of useful feedback", Catmull). Test-screening card percentages were not found.

## Gaps
- Proportion of first drafts kept: no publication found.
- Typical number of rewrites for great scripts: only two data points, 27 (Dunne and Didion) and "30 to 50" (Succession, unattributed), plus a vendor's "10+".
- Story-reel screening cadence: not found beyond the Braintrust interval.
