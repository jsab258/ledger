# A story to stand with the best, with no human writers: the method

24 Nov 2026

## Summary

**The answer.** The bar may be reachable, and nobody can honestly promise it. No source reached shows an AI-only team writing a long story that players rank with the best, and on short prompts recent models tell stories that are 80 to 90% alike (Hamburg study). Nobody has tested a researched, many-times-rewritten story against a checked judge. So the method does not ask for trust: it builds a fair judge first, proves the hardest parts small, and only then writes the whole. The odds of reaching the bar set by Red Dead Redemption 2 (RDR2) are low and unknown: the draft guessed one in ten to one in four, the fresh reviewer says well under one in ten, and the lead sides with the reviewer. The early checks can show that the bar is out of reach; only outside players can show that it is reached.

**What matters most, on the evidence.** People the player cares about, far more than plot: 35% of story praise in the project's reading of 400 Steam reviews, against 4% for a surprising turn (project study, small samples). A lead who wants something and pays for it. Detail from real life. Many more rewrites than a competent team bothers with. And a checked judge: an AI reader giving marks picks the better of two scripts barely more often than a coin (53.8 to 55.8%, DramaGrow).

**The approaches, ranked.**

1. Measure first, slice first (recommended). About two weeks (estimate) to build the judge and check it against real works, and by week 3 one heavy scene staged in the real game with its synthetic faces and voices, the cheapest test that could end the bar early. Then theme, ending and the main people, then the first hour and one heavy moment, finished and played in the browser. Only if those pass is the full story written. The only route that shows early whether the bar is out of reach.
2. Whole story first. The classic order: full plan, full script, then test. The same ceiling, but no proof for four to five months (estimate), and plans are what an AI judge is likeliest to flatter.
3. Many versions, machine picks. Quick, cheap, and the likeliest road to competent and dull: the versions come out alike (Hamburg study).

**Cost and time.** All estimates, at API list prices, built on the project's own US$115 heavy day. To the main gate: about nine weeks and US$4,500 to 7,000. Fable, the strongest model, writes story on 25 to 32 of those days and builds nothing else on them. If the gate passes: a further 14 to 20 weeks and US$9,500 to 18,500 for the full script, tested as a browser game. Putting it into the real game is not counted and may well cost more than the writing.

**The stop rule.** Four checks, each a measured result. Weeks, counts and thresholds are estimates.

0. Week 3. If the staged heavy scene does not move the owner in the real game, the story is rewritten for that ceiling: more through action, voices and distance, fewer faces in close-up.
1. Week 2. If no panel of AI judges can tell admired scenes from forgettable ones at least three times in four (the research note's floor), no AI judge counts. Only the owner's picks and outside players do.
2. Week 5. The team writes its own versions of 20 admired scenes, each from a disguised outline of the original, and the two are compared blind. If, after four rounds, the originals still win three times for each team win, with no recent gain, and the owner's blind picks agree, scene writing is below the bar.
3. Week 9. The owner plays the first hour. If after two full rebuilds he still would not choose to play on, the bar is out of reach for this team for now.

If check 2 or 3 fails, the story stops being the selling point: a shorter, plainer story under a game led by its town and its play, with the 20-scene test rerun on each new model.

**What the owner decides.** Recommended choice marked.

1. The approach. A: measure first, slice first (recommended). B: whole story first. C: many versions.
2. Judges that are not Claude models, so the writer's family does not mark its own work. A: yes, a free open model on the PC if one is good enough, else a paid one capped at about US$20 a month (estimate) (recommended). B: no, and his own picks carry more weight.
3. Outside players for the first hour at week 9. A: yes, about six people (estimate) who have never seen it, their verdict equal to the owner's at that gate (recommended). B: not yet, the owner alone. C: never.

## What separates great stories from competent ones

On numbers, throughout: a name in brackets is the source, as the research notes name it. Estimate means the method's own judgement or arithmetic, and a list or paragraph marked as estimates is so throughout. A plain count in the method's design, such as three endings, is an estimate of what is enough.

From first principles, players rate a story highly when four things are true: they care what happens to someone, they cannot guess what comes next, they believe the world and, in a game, they feel that they did it. A competent story is clear and consistent and fails all four, and players then call it forgettable, the top complaint in the project's Steam reading (15% of story comments).

Two warnings. Nearly all the evidence is what admired teams say they did. No source compared them with teams that did the same and failed, so these are habits great work shares, not a recipe. And the notes read some papers as abstracts only, and took quotes through a summarising model.

1. **People the player cares about come first, plot second.** In a long game the player spends more hours in company than in plot. In the project's reading of 400 Steam reviews, characters worth caring about led the praise in 8 of 8 games and made up 35% of story comments, against 4% for a surprising turn (project study; small samples, directions only). The only RDR2 review read calls the gang its strongest asset (The Next Web). Thin: one critic, one small sample.
2. **A lead who wants something and pays for it.** Druckmann: pressure reveals character (Arc Studio). Truby builds from a weakness, a desire and an opponent who attacks that weakness (scriptreader.ai summary). The project's blind reading found the opposite in its three AI-written bibles: a lead who wants to avoid something, and the heaviest moments happening to others.
3. **Theme and ending first, the middle left loose.** Druckmann and Gross find the ending and work back from a theme (Arc Studio). RDR2 began with a plan, fencepost scenes and key characters, then was written as it went (Hollywood Reporter citing Edge). Better Call Saul and Succession kept their plots open late (Yahoo UK; Ashore). Common to all: the end and the meaning are known, the route is not.
4. **A fixed spine, with the player heard through people.** Disco Elysium has one critical path, a backbone that let its writers be "quite extravagant" elsewhere (GamesHub). Baldur's Gate 3 makes its paths converge, and its lead writer aims at characters who react to every choice (GamesRadar; Den of Geek). The complaint to avoid is missions on rails (6% of story comments, project study).
5. **Rewriting far beyond the first draft that works.** Disco Elysium's Cuno was rewritten 20 times (GamesHub). Dunne and Didion wrote 27 drafts of one film (Reason). Catmull says every Pixar film starts out as a disaster (Salon). Thin: two data points, and nothing on how much of a first draft survives.
6. **Rebuilding, not polishing.** Toy Story 2 was restarted. Inside Out worked only when Joy's companion was changed (Salon; Den of Geek). The Witcher 3 team scrapped drafts to start over (Shacknews). Competent teams polish a scene whose bones are wrong.
7. **One voice with the last word, and a candid check with no power.** Armstrong reworks every Succession script for tone (Chortle). Simon made the final call yet "wanted to be persuaded" (Looper/Slashfilm). Pixar's Braintrust gives candid notes that the director may ignore (Fast Company).
8. **Detail from real life.** A detail nobody would invent tells the player the world is real. Simon hired reporters and novelists, and his room spent months on subjects like the port (BuzzFeed News; Slate). Kasavin: the unnecessary "dumb stuff" is what makes a game special (Game Developer).
9. **Writers and designers are one team, and play carries the drama.** Baldur's Gate 3's writers were also designers (80.lv). CD Projekt Red ran writers and quest designers through one pipeline (Shacknews). Blacha: a big choice should make the player put the controller away, stand up and walk about (PC Gamer).
10. **Scenes that turn, with structure as a check after drafting.** McKee and Story Grid agree that every scene must turn on a choice (Shortform; Creative Penn). Kaufman, Arriaga and August reject formula as a template (Script Mag; John August). Thin: no data was found on whether formula helps, and its use as a check is the note's inference.
11. **No pure villain, and an ending that costs.** Tuulik: no pure evil (GamesHub). Druckmann and Gross: characters both good and bad, "not sugar-coated" (Arc Studio). The same review calls Arthur's death in RDR2 powerful (The Next Web). Players punish a rushed ending (5% of story comments, project study).
12. **Cutting, and a hard closing edit.** RDR2 lost about 5 hours of a 65-hour story late, and a romance that "didn't work" (GamesRadar). On Better Call Saul the commonest late change was cutting dialogue (Yahoo UK).
13. **Performers and fresh audiences finish the work.** Firewatch cast one lead before writing began, and the actor chosen for the other reshaped the part (Red Bull). RDR2 used about 1,200 actors (GamesRadar). Pixar gets about 90% of its useful feedback from sitting with someone who has never seen the film (Salon).
14. **Years together.** Rockstar's four senior writers had at least 12 years together (Kotaku), Supergiant nearly 10 (Game Developer), Larian's writers six years with their characters (80.lv).

Where this team stands: a team of models can work at points 1 to 12. It cannot copy 13 and 14: it has no actors, no fresh audience unless the owner allows one, and no years together. The method puts substitutes in their place: speech models from the record, fresh readers, character files. None is proven.

## The method for this team

The team can write without limit, cheaply. What it lacks is a trustworthy sense of what is good, which a human room gets from a showrunner's taste, actors and a test audience. So the method spends first on the judge and puts every later step through it.

### Roles and models

Splitting the work between specialised agents was preferred by expert readers (Agents' Room; effect sizes not read). The roles keep to the model ruling: Fable makes, Opus draws together and reviews, Sonnet and Haiku read, extract and screen.

1. **Showrunner and head writer: Fable, the single session at max effort.** Holds theme, ending and tone, and writes every scene that ships. Admired shows are single-voiced however many people write them (Chortle; Looper/Slashfilm), so the strongest model holds the one voice.
2. **Story editor: Opus.** Keeps the whole in view: the arcs, the fact file of who knows what and when, the map of weight. Runs the structure checks and writes the notes.
3. **Character keepers: Opus, one fresh session for each main character,** reading only that character's file. Each argues for its character when the story is laid out and objects when a draft has it act without motive. Keepers review. They do not write shipped lines.
4. **Researcher: Sonnet or Haiku read and extract. Opus draws the world book together.**
5. **The candid panel: fresh Opus sessions that have seen no earlier draft,** reading the whole every two to three weeks (estimate). No authority. The showrunner answers each note in one line, so that ignoring one is a choice and not a drift.
6. **Adversarial critic: a fresh Opus session briefed to prove the scene ordinary:** where it has been seen before, the lines anyone could say, the turn a reader sees coming.
7. **Script doctor: a fresh Opus session, called when a scene fails twice.** It gets the scene and the problem, not the showrunner's theory, and proposes a rebuild. Fable rewrites.
8. **First screens: Sonnet or Haiku.** Checklists, the fact file check, banned-phrase counts, and the sameness, forecast and swap tests below.
9. **Judges: at least three models from at least two families for blind pairs, with Opus for the final review.** Fable never judges its own work.
10. **Play-tester: a Sonnet session walks every branch of each prototype.** It proves that a scene works. It cannot say whether it moves anyone.

There is no separate designer: the showrunner writes what the player does into the scene itself, as the admired teams did (80.lv; Shacknews).

One part of the model ruling is measured, not assumed: whether Opus could draft ordinary scenes for Fable to rework for tone. The cover test includes Opus versions. If neither panel nor owner can tell them from Fable's, he is asked whether to change the ruling for ordinary scenes, which would roughly halve the Fable days of the long phase (estimate).

### The order of work

1. **The judge** (weeks 1 and 2, estimate): the reference shelf, the checked panel, the scene player.
2. **Theme and ending.** One page: the theme as a question with two strong sides, the lead's want as something he goes after, and three endings, each written as a full scene and each naming what the lead loses for good. The owner picks.
3. **People and relationships.** For each main character: a want, a lie they tell themselves, what they do under pressure, how they talk, how they are funny, what they would never say. For each pair: a shared past, a debt and a disagreement. The arcs are laid over each other to find clashes and echoes (Weiner, Slashfilm). Then proof in scenes, not sheets: two audition scenes each, one under pressure and one at rest, heard by the owner as a table read, the scene spoken in temporary voices.
4. **Structure across the hours.** Ten to fourteen fencepost scenes fixed (estimate), the route between them left loose. One spine that every path returns to. A table, chapter by chapter, of what changes: the lead's standing, his means, what he can now lose. A map of weight: the heavy moments, and the rest and humour between.
5. **The slice.** The first hour, and one heavy moment with a lead-in of 20 to 30 minutes (estimate), finished and played. The main gate.
6. **Missions and scenes,** on CD Projekt Red's pipeline (Shacknews): short pitches seeded from real incidents, narrowed to the strongest, then a full scenario, several drafts, a prototype and mood tuning. Each scene turns on a choice, and the turn is played, not watched.
7. **Dialogue, last and in layers:** to the cast voices, then what is left unsaid, then cutting, then the table read.
8. **The closing edit,** once the story plays end to end: one hard pass by the showrunner, expecting to cut about a tenth (estimate).

Systems and play meet the writing at three places. When the story is laid out, every fencepost names what the player's hands do, and that action is the dramatic act. At the scene prototype, choices, timing and a stand-in for the action are playable before any line is polished. In the game, the lines that make the player feel heard are keyed to what the simulation knows the player did. A simulated town can remove anyone, so every fencepost needs a second way in, a lesson Larian learnt in its playtests (GamesRadar).

### Real material in place of invention

A model's unprompted idea of a time and place is the average of everything written about it. The cure is material nobody would invent.

1. **Sources.** Oral histories, local newspapers, court reports, memoirs, council and union records, photographs, price lists and television listings from the story's years. Each is logged with its date and where it was reached. An unreached source is never evidence.
2. **Cards.** Cheap models extract four kinds. Fact cards: a price, a procedure, a job's routine. Incident cards: something that happened to someone, what they wanted, what it cost them. Voice cards: short turns of phrase by region, trade and age. Texture cards: the odd detail with no use.
3. **The borrowed specific.** Every scene's central incident starts from an incident card, or from two colliding, never from the model's first idea. Every scene carries at least three card details (estimate), counted by a cheap model.
4. **Casting from the record.** Each main character is given two or three real speakers from the oral histories as a model for rhythm and habit of speech, never their words or their lives. This stands in for the actor who reshapes a part (Red Bull).
5. **Depth before breadth.** The team picks the few trades and institutions the story lives in and builds a day-in-the-job file for each.
6. **Composite, never copied.** Incidents are merged and changed, no real private person is recognisable, no passage is reused, and the licence allowlist governs what is kept. Cards pass canon's content rule before a writer sees them.
7. **A period check** on every scene against the cards: words, prices, laws, technology.

### Typical AI failures: the breaker and the detector for each

Four detectors recur below. The forecast test: a cheap model reads a scene up to its turn and lists its five likeliest continuations. If the real turn is among them, it is the average turn. The swap test: speaker names are stripped and a cheap model must say who speaks each line. The loss ledger: every heavy moment, with who loses what, for how long, and who caused it. The sameness check: a judge is asked only whether two pieces are the same story, which AI judges answer nearly as well as people (81% agreement with humans, Hamburg study). The forecast and swap tests are the method's own design and untested. Each is kept only if admired works on the reference shelf pass it more often than forgettable ones.

1. **A passive lead.** Evidence: the project's blind reading; the two best models tested solved a motive task only 14 and 15 times in 30 (Wang and Kreminski). Breaker: before a scene is written, one line says what the lead is trying to get and what he will give up for it. Detector: strike the lead's decisions from the beat list. If the plot still happens, he is a passenger.
2. **Heavy moments happen to others.** Evidence: the project's blind reading. Breaker: every fencepost costs the lead something he cannot get back, and the two heaviest follow from his own choice. Detector: the loss ledger fails any story whose three largest losses are not his.
3. **Repeating structure.** Evidence: the project's blind reading; models reuse plot elements from one generation to the next (Echoes in AI). Breaker: each mission has a shape card (opening, turn, ending, action, place, company, mood). Neighbours differ on at least three (estimate), and each chapter changes the lead's standing. Detector: the sameness check on every pair of mission summaries.
4. **Sameness with everything else.** Evidence: human stories were about 30% alike, model stories mostly 80 to 90%, and negative prompting and temperature did little (Hamburg study). Breaker: vary the input, not the sampling, which is the research note's reading and not a tested result. Real incident cards are the seeds, and the first idea for any turn is written down and banned. Detector: the forecast test; and five default versions from a cheap model given the scene's one-line brief, with a rebuild if the sameness check calls the team's scene the same story.
5. **Tidy arcs.** Evidence: thin, not measured in any source reached. Breaker: the plan names in advance one thread left open, one main character who does not change, and one who learns the wrong thing. Detector: a table of every arc's end, failed if all end in growth.
6. **Moralising.** Evidence: thin. The nearest is that writers found safe output made dark or rule-breaking writing hard (Wordcraft, an old model). Breaker: the theme is a question, and its other side is argued best by someone the player likes. No character says what the story is about. The lead is allowed to do wrong, and the story does not excuse it. Detector: a count of lines that state a lesson or name a feeling outright; and whether a fresh reader can make the antagonist's case from the script alone.
7. **Safe endings.** Evidence: thin for AI. Players praise an ending that lands and punish a rushed one (4% and 5% of story comments, project study). Breaker: the ending is chosen first, for its cost, from three written in full, and the last hour gets as many passes as the first. Detector: does the lead get what he wanted at no lasting cost?
8. **Flat humour.** Evidence: thin, not measured. Humour was 5% of story praise (project study). Breaker: humour belongs to characters, each funny in their own way and one not at all, drawn from the voice cards, never added in a joke pass. Detector: the forecast test screens stock punchlines. Past that only a person can tell, so the owner's table reads decide.
9. **Generic dialogue.** Evidence: some model phrases appear over 1,000 times more often than in human text (Antislop), and a model could not hold distinct voices (Wordcraft). Breaker: lines written to the cast voices; in key scenes nobody says what they feel outright; a cutting pass that aims to remove a third (estimate). Detector: the swap test; banned phrases in each thousand words; a blanked line that a cheap model fills in almost word for word.
10. **Drift over many hours.** Evidence: long model stories contradict their own facts, most often in the middle (Lost in Stories). Breaker: the fact file is data, not prose. Detector: every scene checked against it by script and cheap model. Such a checker lifted one model from 13 to 29 correct in 30 (Wang and Kreminski).
11. **Polishing in circles.** Evidence: thin for fiction. In essays, help from a feedback-tuned model made different writers' texts more alike (Padmakumar and He). Breaker: after two failed rewrites a scene is rebuilt from a different seed, never polished a third time. Detector: judges are not told which draft is newer, and a better mark on a small change is flagged.

### Judging without human readers

An AI judge that compares the team's written plan with famous finished games is not fair by default. Five reasons.

1. **It remembers the fame.** It knows what players said about the finished game and marks the name. No source reached has measured this, so the team measures it once, with the label test: the same excerpts judged with their famous names and without.
2. **The two are at different stages.** A finished game has performance, music and play. A plan has none. The error runs both ways: fluent, tidy plans read well, while the faults of AI stories lie in execution and surprise (the research note's inference; no source reached tests it).
3. **It favours its own kind.** One AI judge rated AI stories above human ones on all 11 measures, with a perfect 5.00 and no spread on three (Tutone et al.). Judges of one family agree with each other more (Shi, Ma and Vosoughi).
4. **Marks out of ten or thirty say almost nothing.** Judges giving such marks picked the better script 53.8 to 55.8% of the time, while direct pairs reached 85.1% (DramaGrow). The 23.7, 19.3 and 18.7 out of 30 given to this project's bibles were of the weakest kind: absolute marks, on plans, from AI readers of AI writing. The faults those readers named can be checked, and are worth more than the marks.
5. **At its best the tool is modest.** On short stories the best judges match human preference about three times in four: 73% off the shelf and 78% when trained (LitBench), 0.785 at best (LongJudgeBench). One strong model given a rubric fell to 0.20 (LongJudgeBench).

The design around it:

1. **Like with like: a reference shelf.** 40 to 60 excerpts in each of two tiers (estimate): scenes that players and critics single out for praise, and scenes from works of the same kinds whose stories players call forgettable. Forgettable, not bad: competent work is the line that matters. The rankings come from people: player reviews as in the project's Steam reading, critics, awards. Each excerpt is translated to the stage of the team's work: a beat outline in the team's template, or a scene in its script format. Plans meet only plans, finished scenes only finished scenes.
2. **Disguise, the same for both sides.** Names, places, trades and period are changed by one fixed recipe, and the team's text goes through the same recipe so that both carry the same marks. Each judge is asked to name the source, and what it recognises is dropped. Works released after the judges' training cut-off are the cleanest, since no judge can remember how they were received. Writers never see the shelf, because examples leak into output (Wordcraft). It is research, held under the licence allowlist and never shipped.
3. **Blind pairs, both orders, repeated, by mixed families.** A verdict counts only if it survives the swap, a split is a tie, each pair is run three to five times (research note), and at least one judge is not a Claude model.
4. **Checked against human rankings.** This applies the owner's standing ruling. On about 100 shelf pairs (estimate), a panel must put the admired scene above the forgettable one in at least 75% of the pairs it decides (the research note's floor), and must decide at least two pairs in three (estimate). It is checked again when any judge changes, and against 20 to 30 of the owner's own blind picks in the first month (estimate).
5. **Checklists that have earned their place.** An item is kept only if admired works pass it more often than forgettable ones. A structured rubric raised false alarms on clean scripts from 5.7% to 13.3% (DramaGrow).
6. **The cover test.** For 20 admired scenes (estimate), a cheap model writes a disguised brief: the situation, what each person wants, what must happen. Fable writes the scene from the brief. So does Opus, and so does a cheap model at its defaults. A brief the writer can trace to its source is dropped. The panel and the owner compare the versions blind with the disguised original. Content, length and format match, so craft is the only difference. A panel that prefers the cheap default to the admired original is unfit. Cover versions are test pieces and never go into the game. The test covers scene craft only. Whether the team can make people a player cares about over hours is for the slice to show.
7. **The owner as the reader who counts.** Blind picks on his daily page: two short scenes, as text or audio, and which he would rather play on from. Table reads of two to five minutes (estimate). In play, the prototype records what he skips, where he stops and how long he waits at a choice. The next day, one question: who does he remember, and one thing they said or did. His yes is the only pass at a gate.
8. **Outside players, if he allows them.** He is one person and he knows the project. About six at each play gate (estimate). With so few, only large effects count: where five expert raters split three to two, dropping one flipped the verdict 60% of the time (DramaGrow).
9. **What an AI judge may decide.** It may screen out the worse of two drafts, flag checklist faults, and measure sameness, voices and continuity. It may not call a story great. A plan is only ever screened for faults, never ranked for greatness.

### Testing in play

A scene player is built once (two to three Fable days, estimate). After that each scene is a script file with no new code. A table read on the phone. A playable scene: choices, timing and a simple stand-in for the action, so that the player acts. Two slices: the first hour, which tests whether he cares about anyone and wants to go on, and one heavy moment with its lead-in, which tests whether it lands on someone who has only just met the people. A browser cannot show whether faces and voices carry a heavy scene, so the first hour must pass again in the real game before more is built on it. That test is outside this method and inside the bar.

### Rounds

All figures here are estimates. Key scenes (the fenceposts, the first hour, the ending): 10 to 20 passes, with at least two full rebuilds. Ordinary scenes: 3 to 5. Background lines: 1 or 2, plus the screens. A pass counts only when a new signal caused it: a failed test, an accepted note, the owner's reaction, a play record. A scene stops when blind pairs cannot separate the new draft from the last, twice running. Then it passes its gate or is rebuilt, and after a second failed rebuild it is cut. The whole story gets three rounds: the low-detail whole, the full script, the closing edit.

### Cost and time

All figures here are estimates at API list prices. A heavy Fable day is taken as US$115 (project records), and support work by the other models as US$300 to 500 a week while the judge is built and US$150 to 350 a week after.

1. Weeks 1 and 2, the judge and the scene player: US$1,000 to 1,700.
2. Weeks 3 to 6, theme, ending, people and structure, with the cover test beside them: US$1,600 to 2,700.
3. Weeks 7 to 9, the slice and its play tests: US$1,900 to 2,600.
4. To the gate: nine weeks, 25 to 32 Fable days, about US$4,500 to 7,000.
5. The full script, 70 to 110 scenes and missions: 14 to 20 weeks, 65 to 100 Fable days, US$9,500 to 18,500.
6. In all: 23 to 29 weeks and US$14,000 to 25,500.

## Approaches, ranked

### 1. Measure first, slice first (recommended)

**What it is.** The method above, in its order.

**Expected quality.** The best odds of the three, and still long: one in ten to one in four for the full bar, better than even for a story clearly above competent (judgement, not measurement).

**Cost and time (estimates).** Nine weeks and US$4,500 to 7,000 to the gate, then 14 to 20 weeks and US$9,500 to 18,500 for the full script.

**Risks.**

1. The judge cannot be checked: no panel reaches the floor. Then only people can judge, and rounds are slower.
2. Page tests may not separate admired game scenes from forgettable ones at all, since game scripts lean on performance. If the shelf shows this, the page gate is dropped and the play gate carries the weight.
3. The slice trap: a first hour polished beyond what can be afforded for 15 to 20 hours. The guard: passes and cost for each slice scene are recorded and the whole is budgeted from them. If the sum is too high, length is cut, never quality. And a slice cannot prove the whole: the long arc is tested only when the story plays end to end.
4. Delivery. Temporary voices in a browser are kinder than the real test. Synthetic voices and faces may cap what any script can do.
5. The model ruling allows one Fable session, so on 25 to 32 days (estimate) the Fable seat builds nothing else. That, more than the money, is the price.

**First three steps.**

1. Build the shelf, check the panel against it, run the label test.
2. Build the scene player and run the first round of the cover test, with Fable and Opus versions.
3. Give the owner one page: three theme-and-ending options, each ending written in full.

**Decision points for the owner.** The weeks are estimates. Now: the three in the summary. Week 2: accept the judge, or fall back on his own picks. Week 3 or 4: theme and ending. Week 5: the cover result, and whether Opus may draft ordinary scenes. Week 9: go on, change scope, or stop.

**Stop rule.** The three checks in the summary. The thresholds below are estimates, and are fixed at week 2, before any of the team's scenes are judged.

1. Week 5, the cover test, 20 scenes after four rounds. Pairs the panel cannot decide are left out. Pass: the team's versions win at least two for every three the originals win, and beat the cheap default about as often as the originals do. Stop: the originals win three or more for each win by the team, with no gain over the last two rounds, and the owner prefers the original in at least 4 of his 5 blind pairs. Between the two: one more round, then decide. On the method's arithmetic, with about 14 decided pairs and a panel right three times in four, a team truly level with the originals would be wrongly stopped about 1 time in 35, and a team clearly below would slip through about 1 time in 9, to be caught at week 9.
2. Week 9, the slice, after up to two rebuilds. Pass: the owner finishes the first hour unasked, says he would play on, and the next day names two people and the heavy moment. With outside players, at least 4 of 6 do the same. Stop: he still would not choose to play on. If he would play on but recalls nobody, the people are rebuilt once more.

**Fallback.** After a stop at week 5 (scene craft) or week 9 (people who do not land), the story stops being the selling point, the game leads with its town and its play, and the cover test is run again on each new model, about a week's work (estimate), since the shelf and the checked panel are kept. A pass at both, at a cost too high to sustain for 15 to 20 hours, means a shorter spine of 6 to 8 hours (estimate) at the same quality. One more fallback breaks the premise and is the owner's alone to choose: a human editor for the closing edit, since experts still preferred expert-edited text to machine-edited (Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu).

### 2. Whole story first

**What it is.** The classic order to the end: theme, full bible, an outline of every hour, the full script, and only then prototypes and play.

**Expected quality.** The same ceiling in principle. Lower odds in practice, one in ten at best for the full bar (judgement), because for four to five months (estimate) the only judges are AI readers of plans, the weakest evidence there is, and faults in the bones surface after the script exists. The project has run this once in miniature: three bibles, marks out of 30, and a shared list of faults.

**In its favour.** Designing the whole before the parts is real craft: Simon and Burns settled on five seasons early (Slate), and Ellroy outlines for 300 to 400 pages (Granneman). Approach 1 keeps this, at low detail.

**Cost and time (estimates).** 16 to 22 weeks and US$11,000 to 21,000 before the first play test.

**Risks.** Plans flattered by judges. Sunk cost that makes stopping hard. Nothing for the owner to play for months. A full script to rebuild if the first hour fails.

**First three steps.** The theme-and-ending page. The full people-and-relationships bible. A beat outline of every hour.

**Decision points for the owner.** Theme and ending, the bible, the outline, the script, then the first play.

**Stop rule and fallback.** The same first-hour test, reached after 16 to 22 weeks and not nine, at two to three times the spend (estimates). The fallbacks are those of approach 1.

### 3. Many versions, machine selection

**What it is.** Cheap models write many premises, plans and scenes, the judge panel runs a knock-out, and the winners are refined.

**Expected quality.** Most likely competent and dull, which the brief counts as failure: under one in twenty for the full bar (judgement). The candidates will be 80 to 90% alike, and sampling tricks barely help (Hamburg study). The judges' sense of creativity tracks elaboration, a surface feature (Tutone et al.). So a knock-out picks the most polished version of the average story.

**Cost and time (estimates).** Three to four weeks and US$1,500 to 3,000.

**Risks.** False confidence from high marks. Reading those marks as proof would break the owner's ruling on judges.

**First three steps.** 40 premises, each from a different real incident card (estimate). A knock-out by the checked panel. The top three written as first hours and played.

**Decision points for the owner.** Which of the three goes forward, or none.

**Stop rule and fallback.** Stop if the sameness check calls more than half the pairs among the top ten the same story (estimate), or if the owner's blind picks do not favour the winners over the losers. The fallback is approach 1, which keeps the one sound part: short pitches from different real incidents, narrowed to the strongest.

## What would change this recommendation

1. A panel that scores well above 80% against human rankings at the scene stage (estimate). Machine selection could carry more weight, and parts of approach 3 would move up.
2. No panel reaching the floor, or a shelf on which nobody, judge or owner, can tell admired game scenes from forgettable ones on the page. The page gate would go, outside players would stop being optional, and the first hour would be played by week 6 (estimate).
3. The owner allowing a human editor, or actors for the leads. The evidence favours both (Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu; Red Bull), and the odds would rise.
4. A stronger model. The cover test is run again, in about a week (estimate).
5. Faces and voices in the real game failing to carry the first hour. The story would be staged to lean on action, distance and voice heard over play.
6. The sources the notes could not reach, among them the 2018 account of RDR2's writing and the full list of faults experts found in AI prose. Any could shift a point above.
7. A shorter story. It gets more passes for each scene at the same cost, and is the first lever if the week 9 result is close.

## Reviewer's challenge

A fresh Opus reviewer, given the draft and the three research notes but not the lead's view, challenged it. Its text in full:

### Verdict

The recommendation stands with changes. Measuring before writing the whole is right, and the draft handles its sources mostly honestly. But its gates test whether the team avoids the competent-and-dull failure, not whether it reaches the RDR2 bar. The cheapest test that could kill the bar early, performance in the real game, sits outside the method. And the odds and the bar are pitched higher than the evidence allows.

### Disagreements

1. No gate measures greatness, so nothing at week 9 can update the odds toward the bar. (Must-fix.) Every pass condition is a floor. At week 5 the team needs two wins for every three by the originals. At week 9 the owner finishes an hour unasked, wants to play on, and recalls two people the next day. A well-made competent story passes both. Yet the summary says "by week 9 results replace the guess". Change: say plainly that the nine weeks test for failure, not for greatness. Add one upper test: outside players compare the slice blind with the opening of an admired game of similar scope, in the same browser form, and say which they would rather continue.

2. The performance ceiling is tested last, though it is the cheapest early stop. (Must-fix.) RDR2's story rests on 1,200 actors and full-scene capture (games note). The draft says synthetic faces and voices "may cap what any script can do", then places that test "outside this method". If they cannot carry one heavy scene, the bar falls whatever the script, and that can be known in week 2 or 3. Change: the builder stages one two-minute heavy scene in the real game with the planned voice and face tools, and the owner judges it against reference frames. Then write for the ceiling found: more through action, distance, letters and voices heard in play, fewer faces in close-up.

3. Play and mission design get one paragraph, though in an open-world crime game they carry the story. (Must-fix.) The draft folds the designer into Fable and tests the first hour in a browser with "a simple stand-in for the action". That tests a visual novel. It cannot test the free roam between missions, the missions themselves, or whether the town simulation makes the player feel he did it, which is the draft's own fourth principle. Missions on rails draw more complaints than rushed endings in the project's Steam reading (6% against 5%). Change: name a mission design role (Opus, with the town session for simulation hooks); put one mission in the slice as a grey-box in the real game; build one fencepost on what the simulation knows the player did.

4. The cover test favours the AI's vices and can be gamed through the disguise. (Must-fix.) The "fixed recipe" will in practice be run by a model, which pulls the original towards model prose and narrows the gap being measured. Admired game scenes lean on performance and on hours of context; cut out alone they are often sparse, while an explicit, self-contained AI scene reads better on the page, rewarding the over-explaining the method elsewhere fights. And the brief hands over the situation and the turn, which are the hard part. Change: disguise names and places by script, not by model; have the owner and one outside reader confirm the disguised original still reads as itself; include check pairs where the panel must prefer the original over a careful Opus pastiche; put "scene craft only" in the summary.

5. The stop-rule arithmetic is optimistic, and the panel is checked on the wrong pairs. (Must-fix.) The "1 in 9" slip-through holds only for a team that the panel sees lose three pairs in four, which is a team that truly loses every pair. A team merely below, winning 35% of verdicts, passes the two-for-three line about one time in three (the reviewer's binomial check on 14 pairs). The panel is checked on admired against forgettable, a wide gap, then used on close pairs: team against original, and new draft against old. Position bias is worst when texts are close (Shi, Ma and Vosoughi). So the rule "stop when blind pairs cannot separate the new draft from the last" will mostly stop on noise. Change: also calibrate on close pairs, such as the owner's blind picks between drafts; report the odds for a moderately weak team; use 30 or more scenes if the shelf allows.

6. The week-2 floor will probably fail, with no plan behind it. (Must-fix.) The best off-the-shelf judge in the notes reached 73% on short Reddit stories (LitBench), under the 75% floor, and disguised game scenes out of context are harder. If the panel fails, every rule built on blind pairs loses its signal, and the owner's one page a day is far slower than the rounds assume. Change: plan the rounds for failure now: a small paid pool of outside readers, the checkable screens kept (continuity, swap test, loss ledger), taste verdicts dropped.

7. The odds are not honest enough. (Judgement, leaning must-fix.) "One in ten to one in four" has no base rate. Most professional human teams never make a story ranked with RDR2; nothing in the notes shows an AI-only long story near it; model stories passed 3 to 10 times fewer tests than professional ones (TTCW). "Better than even" for clearly above competent is equally unsupported. Change: call the full bar low and unknown, well under one in ten on priors.

8. RDR2 is the wrong yardstick, and a smaller story should be the plan, not the fallback. (Judgement.) RDR2 is a 60-hour story by four writers with 12 years together, and over budget by its creator's account. The small-team stories players rank highly (Firewatch, Hades, Disco Elysium) are focused and character-led. Change: plan a tight spine of about 6 to 10 story hours inside the 15 to 20 hour game from the start, with more passes per scene, and let the town fill the rest.

9. One taste, worn down by daily exposure, holds every gate. (Judgement.) By week 9 the owner has seen theme pages, auditions, table reads and dozens of blind picks: the least fresh reader there is, against Pixar's finding that 90% of useful notes come from people who have never seen the work. Change: make the six outside players at week 9 compulsory and equal to the owner at that gate.

10. The judges and screens may make the story average. (Judgement.) The forecast test bans the five likeliest turns and so pushes towards the sixth, surprising but unearned. Banned phrases, checklists, sameness checks and panels all push towards what models rate well; feedback-tuned help made writers' texts more alike (Padmakumar and He). Change: screens flag, never decide, and the owner's pass overrules any screen.

11. Fable as sole writer: a strength for voice, a weak point for variety and for the build. (Judgement.) One model voicing every character risks the blurred voices Wordcraft found and the within-model sameness Hamburg measured. And 90 to 130 Fable days keep the builder's seat off the game for months. Change: run the Opus-drafting test in week 3, not 5, and put the builder time in the summary as plainly as the dollars.

12. The real cost is larger than shown. (Judgement.) Putting the story into the game (voices, animation, mission scripting) is excluded, and is probably most of the cost. 70 to 110 scenes looks thin for 15 to 20 hours of reactive open world, and US$115 a heavy day is untested at this volume. Change: give a rough range for integration, or say it is unknown and likely larger than the writing.

### Factual checks

- Summary and principle 1: the 35%, 4%, "8 of 8 games", 15%, 6% and 5% figures come from a Steam study not in the three notes, so they cannot be checked here. Line 116 gives 4% to "an ending that lands" while line 9 gives 4% to "a surprising turn"; confirm they are not one number used twice.
- Summary: "frontier models still tell stories that are 80 to 90% alike". The Hamburg study measured repeated samples of one short prompt across 10 models; it does not cover researched, seeded long stories. Needs a qualifier.
- Typical failures, item 10: "13 to 29 correct in 30" was causal soundness on small planning tasks (GPT-4o), not continuity over long stories.
- Principle 5: Dunne and Didion's 27 drafts were driven by executives and stars over eight years on a film not ranked as great. That is churn, not admired rewriting.
- The floor: the note says "about 75%", with 70 to 80% a good result; the draft omits that the best measured judge falls short.
- Stop rule: "1 in 35" checks (2.9%); "1 in 9" holds only as in disagreement 5.
- Cost sums add up. "A little over a third of the project's weekly rate" is unclear and not in the notes.
- Other citations match the notes, including DramaGrow, LitBench, LongJudgeBench, Tutone, Wang and Kreminski, the RDR2 cut and Cuno's 20 rewrites.

### Where the reviewer agrees

- Pairwise, swapped, repeated, mixed-family judging; absolute marks thrown out.
- Real incident cards as seeds; varying the input rather than the sampling.
- Theme and ending first, fenceposts fixed, the route loose.
- Rebuild after two failed rewrites rather than polish.
- Approach 3 rightly last, approach 2 rightly below approach 1.
- The judge takes two weeks, not nine; writing starts in week 3, so the fear of a nine-week judge build is unfounded.

### What the lead takes from it

The lead (Opus, who ran the research and checks) accepts the verdict: the recommendation stands with changes. Taken now, in the summary: the week-3 staged heavy scene in the real game (point 2), the lower odds (point 7), outside players equal to the owner at week 9 (point 9), and the note that the early checks can show failure but not greatness (point 1). Taken for the first steps, not yet written into the body above: an upper test at week 9, the slice set blind against the opening of an admired game of similar scope (point 1); a mission design role on Opus with one grey-box mission in the real game inside the slice (point 3); disguise by script, with check pairs against a careful Opus pastiche (point 4); calibration on close pairs and 30 or more cover scenes, with the stop odds restated for a moderately weak team, about one in three slipping through on 14 pairs (point 5); a plan for a failed week-2 floor, since the best judge measured reached 73% against a 75% floor (point 6); screens that flag and never decide (point 10); the Opus-drafting test moved to week 3 (point 11). Left to the owner: whether the story spine is planned from the start at 6 to 10 hours inside the 15 to 20 hour game (point 8). The lead leans to yes. Not answered here: the cost of putting the story into the game (point 12), which is unknown and likely larger than the writing. On the factual checks: the two 4% figures are two different findings of the project's Steam reading (a surprising turn, and an ending that lands), both genuinely 4%; the 80 to 90% figure is now qualified as short prompts in the summary; the 13-to-29 result and the 27 drafts are weaker support than the body implies.
