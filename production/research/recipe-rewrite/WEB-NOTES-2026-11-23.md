## Claims

From 1 (Florencio)
- Pre-production should yield validated gameplay by prototyping, a defined pipeline, and current documentation, and should be timeboxed.
- Implied order: pillars first, then prototype each core system until fun, then a vertical slice, then pipelines, with documentation throughout.
- The slice is a playable piece of everything core (art, gameplay, systems), strong enough to justify full production; the pillars decide what goes in it.
- Handoff points are each pipeline stage's outputs and acceptance criteria; the article names no formal gate.
- Failures listed: pre-production treated as concept art and early code; prototypes without clear goals; a rudimentary design document read by one person; undefined pipelines causing a character passed back and forth between rigger, animator and modeller; stale documentation; no timebox.
- Changing pipelines mid-production costs much more than in pre-production.

From 2 (Roye)
- The team started with a technology proof of concept, which later gave "a false sense of security".
- The gameplay milestone failed because the game was "too damn big and too ambitious".
- The content from the early milestone was not polished, and several designed features were cut after being built.
- The team then cut to a linear, five-room vertical slice sprint.

From 3 (Krembs)
- Little was written about overall design or asset requirements until about halfway through the slice milestone; art suffered most.
- Team members saw their work in a build only at a weekly meeting, so bugs lingered for days.
- Data split across Dropbox and Perforce made handoffs harder.
- The article does not discuss pre-production order.

From 4 (Yao)
- The slice level could not be built because the engine crashed on saving; the team demoed with blank tiles and art shown separately.
- Yao writes this would have been eased had the engine's capabilities been stress-tested at the start.
- An early proof-of-concept milestone tested two mechanics; testers preferred the air dash, and the team focused on it.
- The main mechanic was settled early, but the HUD and level objectives were not; design meetings "ended without a definitive decision" until each had a stated goal and pillars to refer to.

From 5 (Donovan, abstract)
- Volition uses the slice (also called proof of concept or minimum viable product) as the checkpoint for moving from pre-production into production.
- The checkpoint must meet internal and external needs and show the team understands what it builds and how.
- The talk reviews where Volition's past slices succeeded and failed, and the process changes made. The talk's content itself was not read.

From 6 (Warhorse, secondhand)
- Warhorse had "a working vertical slice when it was shopping the game to publishers"; no publisher backed it, so it went to Kickstarter.
- After the campaign succeeded the studio "had to start nearly from scratch".
- Staff nearly tripled, a motion capture studio was built, stretch goals were added; Rýdl named this as a large part of the slip from December 2015 to mid-2016.
- A technology alpha went to backers in October 2014 and was updated twice; no order of pre-production is given.

From 7 (Rockstar, secondhand)
- An NPC interaction prototype by animation VP Mark Tennant dates from about 2014 and became RDR2's "Greet or Antagonize".
- Rockstar's Rob Nelson reportedly said it would revolutionise how their games work; a colleague was hesitant to show it because Nelson tended to push ambitious ideas in regardless of workload.
- The article gives no Rockstar pre-production order or gates.

From 8 (Foertsch write-up)
- Says many postmortems share "regret about not handling pre-production well".
- Nothing on order, gates or references; the talk itself was not read.
