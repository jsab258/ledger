# Holding information a player cannot write down

Research topic 13. Three games, three different problems, and an answer to a
question I asked you a few deliveries ago.

## First, the good news: we already have one of these for free

Obra Dinn's famous trick is that it will not tell you whether a guess is right
until you have got three complete answers correct at once. The designer's reason
is plain: verify immediately and the player just clicks through every name until
the game says yes.

We do not need that rule, because we already solved the same problem from a
better angle. When the player accuses somebody in our game, the accusation is not
checked against the truth at all. It is weighed by whether anyone will back it
up, and our own file says so: "truth is not an input. A true accusation nobody
will corroborate is ignored."

So a player cannot guess their way to an answer here, because naming a name is a
public act with a cost rather than a query with a result. Obra Dinn needed a rule
to protect its right answer. We have a town to convince instead, which does the
same job and is more interesting.

## Second: Her Story is proof that our information design carries a game

Her Story is a police database you search by typing words. It gives you five
clips per search, out of 271. To find more you have to think of a better word,
and the only way to get a better word is to have heard it in a clip you already
found.

So the thing the player holds in their head IS the interface. Nothing is written
down for them and nothing needs to be.

That is a very close description of what your information ruling already asks
for: learning what people know happens through asking around, buying gossip, pub
talk, eavesdropping, and there is no free look. What you can ask about is bounded
by what you have heard. Her Story shipped on that idea alone and it worked.

## Third, and this answers my own question back to you

In the Shadows of Doubt write-up I asked you to choose between a notebook and a
corkboard with string. A notebook holds any amount and never gets confusing. A
board shows the connections the player drew, because the player drew them.

**Outer Wilds has a third thing and I think it beats both.** Its ship log records
everything you learn and then shows you how the pieces link to each other,
automatically. It is a notebook that draws its own string.

It holds everything, like a list. It shows connections, which is the board's whole
advantage. The player does no pinning, so it is not busywork. And here is the part
I thought only a corkboard could give: the layout is different for every player,
because it reflects the order you found things in. It is still personal.

Better still, we would not have to record anything new to build it. Your own
ruling already tags each Ledger entry as witnessed, heard, or deduced, and a
"heard" entry names who told you. That is already a link from a fact to a person.
The web is sitting in the data you already specified.

I am not withdrawing the question, because which of the three Meridian gets is
still a feel call and still yours. I am adding a third option and telling you I
think it wins.

## And the part nobody can help us with

All three of these games have a fixed, finite, hand-written body of truth. Obra
Dinn has sixty deaths. Her Story has 271 clips. Outer Wilds has a solar system
that does not change.

Ours never stops arriving. Our information is produced by a simulation that keeps
running, about people who keep doing things, and there is no last piece.

A finite mystery can be laid out completely. An infinite one has to be filtered
and ranked, and that is a different interface. The closest thing we already have
is a "lead", which our code defines as who is carrying talk about you, how sure
they are, where it came from, and whether it touches the hidden life. That is a
ranked current view rather than a complete map, and a complete map is not
available to us.

**I could not find a single investigation game with an ongoing generated
information supply that has solved this.** The nearest is Shadows of Doubt, and
its reviews say the cases become interchangeable. So this may genuinely be
unsolved, and if it is, it is the most interesting unsolved problem in the design.

I should be honest that from where I sit I cannot tell the difference between "the
field has not solved this" and "I did not find the game that did".
