# One date in 1989 explains most of Meridian

Research topic 22, on crime fiction in declining industrial Britain. I went
looking for tone and found the town's economics instead. Both are below, the
useful one first.

## The date

**On 3 July 1989 the National Dock Labour Scheme was abolished.**

It was the thing that guaranteed registered dockers their jobs, their wages and
their conditions. There were 9,221 registered dockers in April 1989. By October
1990 there were fewer than 4,000, and in some ports there were none at all.

More than half the dock labour in Britain disappeared in eighteen months, and
it happened right in the middle of our window.

Look at what canon already says about Meridian:

- Tom inherits **a half-dead criminal outfit**
- and **a book of uncollectable debts**
- and one of the three rivals is **the dockside syndicate**, old muscle and
  smuggling, run by the Widow

That one real event explains all three at once. The debts are uncollectable
because the men who owed them lost their jobs. The outfit is half-dead because
the trade it taxed is half-dead. The new flashy crew can rise because the old
order's power came from a closed shop that no longer exists.

I am not suggesting the game mention the scheme or explain any of this to the
player. The value is that Meridian's economics stop being arbitrary, which is
the whole difference between a period setting and a period backdrop.

## The rest of the weather, 1989 to 1992

- **The poll tax.** The riot on 31 March 1990 was the biggest in central London
  since 1886: over 100,000 people, 113 injuries, 340 arrests. Mass
  non-compliance nationally, meaning ordinary people were already slightly
  outside the law.
- **The recession.** House prices down about 10% in 1990, repossessions rising,
  negative equity arriving. Unemployment over 1.7 million at the end of 1990,
  forecast over 2 million.

That last one is quietly the most useful for us. **Negative equity is what
makes somebody stay in a town they want to leave.** For a game with a
deliberately small footprint, a historical reason nobody leaves is worth more
than a fence.

## The tone half: what "British noir" actually means

Canon says "never GTA-style American satire", which says what to avoid. Here is
the positive version, from the people who founded the tradition.

**American noir runs on greed. British noir runs on class.**

In an American crime story a man wants money and fate punishes him. In a
British one a man wants to be treated as somebody, and the system is built to
stop him. That is not a style difference, it changes what a scene is about: who
is allowed to speak to whom, and in what tone.

Ted Lewis, who wrote Get Carter and effectively invented British noir, was
writing about "the willingness to be one's own man in both the criminal and the
British class system", in "a small, industrial English city with its tawdry
shops, dingy rooming houses, and suffocating air of decline".

**And we have already built this and not noticed.** Canon says: "What the town
calls you reads out your standing: the new owner, then Novak, then Tom, then
Toma. The gate is knowing, not liking."

That is the class engine. It is currently written down as a progression
readout. It is actually the spine of the register, and calling it that would
give the writing something to aim at.

## Two more things worth stealing

**From David Peace** (the Red Riding novels, Yorkshire, police corruption): the
crime is not an intrusion into a healthy town, it is what the town *produces*
under those conditions. That is a simulation premise, not a plot premise, and
it is something we can actually do better than a scripted game can.

One caution: Peace's police are irredeemable and his world has no light in it
at all. We have a detective in the cast that the player can deal with. Peace is
the reference for the world, not necessarily for the law, and how corrupt
Meridian's police are is a real decision nobody has made yet.

**From Prime Suspect** (1991, our exact year, the gold standard): it is "a
police drama with little on camera violence", and its tension comes from
procedure and institutional friction. Its authority came from being based on a
real serving DCI rather than from research.

That is the argument for the police-procedure research I did earlier being
worth something. In this tradition the procedure is not background detail to
get right. The procedure IS the drama.
