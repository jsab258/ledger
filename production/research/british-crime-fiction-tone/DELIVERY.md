# Crime fiction in declining industrial Britain

Research topic 22. Delivered to the studio. Nothing here is an instruction.

The brief's framing, taken literally: not "British crime fiction" in general,
which is enormous and mostly cosy, but the specific tradition in which the
DECLINE is the engine of the crime. That tradition is small, it has a founder,
and its concerns map onto Meridian's almost exactly.

Two halves, and the second surprised me more than the first: what the fiction
does, and what was actually happening in a British port town between 1988 and
1992. The second half turned out to contain a single dated event that explains
most of Meridian's premise.

Labels follow `production/art/atlas-02/research/`: CITED, DERIVED, ASSUMED,
HOLE.

## Part 1. The tradition, and what it is actually about

### 1.1 Ted Lewis and the founding: class is the engine, not greed

CITED: "Among crime-novel aficionados, it's generally accepted that Ted Lewis
established the noir school of writing in Britain, and one novel in particular
got it going: Get Carter." Lewis "had a shrewd eye for the shifting class
politics of late-'60s England, the point at which the austerity of the postwar
years had melted away and prosperity was slowly creeping into the regions,
creating a new middle class", and the crucial distinction he wrote about was
"the willingness to be one's own man in both the criminal and British class
system".
[SleuthSayers, Get Carter (Brit noir)](https://www.sleuthsayers.org/2024/04/get-carter-brit-noir.html),
[Crime Fiction Lover, Getting Carter: Ted Lewis and the birth of Brit noir](https://crimefictionlover.com/2017/10/getting-carter-ted-lewis-and-the-birth-of-brit-noir/)

CITED, on the setting: Get Carter is "one of the best-ever fictional portraits
of a small, industrial English city with its tawdry shops, dingy rooming
houses, and suffocating air of decline, giving readers a brutal look at
hitherto hidden English sleaze and seediness".
[Soho Press, Get Carter](https://sohopress.com/books/get-carter/)

CITED, on why the tradition is small: "If Ted Lewis had been born in France or
America, he would occupy a place similar to Jim Thompson, Raymond Chandler and
the rest of the hard boiled school of writers. However, Britain, being the land
of the diamond geezer, decent bloke and jolly good chap, is perhaps
particularly full of those unwilling to even admit that life has a dark side,
let alone examine it."
[Martin Cloake, review of Getting Carter](https://martincloake.medium.com/review-getting-carter-40e39922bb27)

DERIVED, and it is the most useful distinction this topic can offer. American
noir runs on GREED and FATE: a man wants money, the universe punishes him.
British noir runs on CLASS: a man wants to be treated as somebody, and the
system is built to stop him. That is not a stylistic difference, it is a
different engine, and it changes what a scene is ABOUT.

It also gives positive content to a rule `canon.md` currently states only as a
prohibition. "Never GTA-style American satire" says what not to do. The
positive version is: the joke and the menace both come from STANDING, from who
is allowed to speak to whom and in what tone.

Worth noting how well this already fits the premise: `canon.md` says "What the
town calls you reads out your standing: the new owner, then Novak, then Tom,
then Toma. The gate is knowing, not liking." That is a class mechanic. It is
the British noir engine already built into the moat, and nobody appears to have
written down that that is what it is.

### 1.2 David Peace and the post-industrial Gothic

CITED: the Red Riding Quartet (published 1999 to 2002, set 1974 to 1983) is
described as establishing "a new post-industrial 'mythology of the North'",
re-inscribing an area historically defined by the Brontes and heavy industry.
Its style is "cinematic", leaning "towards a clipped, poetic noir style",
"sourced in Gothic traditions" and "concerned with shadowy desire, real crime
and social disintegration".
[University of Brighton, (Dis)locations: Post-Industrial Gothic in David Peace's Red Riding Quartet](https://research.brighton.ac.uk/en/publications/dislocations-post-industrial-gothic-in-david-peaces-red-riding-qu/),
[Vertigo, "No Future": the dystopian world of David Peace's Red Riding Quartet](https://sebald.wordpress.com/2024/10/08/no-future-the-dystopian-world-of-david-peaces-red-rider-quartet/)

CITED, and this is the sentence to keep: "The 'inevitability' of crime created
by the social, political and economic circumstances of the period manifests
itself in Peace's novels through a thick, dark 'Yorkshire noir'."
[University of Brighton, as above]

CITED: "The real story is one of police corruption, police brutality, and
police cover-ups", running across four books rooted in specific years of "a
period that included industrial unrest throughout the United Kingdom, the
looming threat of neo-fascist thuggery, punk rock, recession, the Falklands
War, and the gradual emergence of New Right conservatism".
[Vertigo, as above],
[Springer, No Future and Spectrality in David Peace's Red Riding Quartet](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-21979-5_7)

DERIVED: Peace's structural move is that crime is not an intrusion into a
healthy town, it is what the town PRODUCES under those conditions. That is a
simulation premise rather than a plot premise, and it is closer to what LEDGER
is technically capable of than anything in the cosy tradition. A game whose
people have wants, schedules and memory can produce inevitability in a way a
scripted story has to assert.

The caution that comes with it: Peace's quartet is unrelentingly bleak and its
police are irredeemable. `canon.md` has a detective, Mara Ellis, in the cast.
Peace is the reference for the WORLD, not necessarily for what the law is
allowed to be.

### 1.3 Prime Suspect (1991): the register of the exact year

CITED: the 1991 series "is often considered the gold standard for modern crime
procedurals due to its groundbreaking portrayal of both police work and the
internal politics that shape it", and is "a police drama with little on camera
violence and true human emotion". Its protagonist "confront[s] the
institutionalised sexism that exists within the police force", and her rise "is
not presented as inspirational gloss, but as a complex, realistic struggle
against sexism, bureaucracy, and the personal sacrifices demanded by the job".
Lynda La Plante based the character on a real serving officer, DCI Jackie
Malton.
[Nostalgia Central, Prime Suspect](https://nostalgiacentral.com/television/tv-by-decade/tv-shows-1990s/prime-suspect/),
[IMDb, Prime Suspect (1991)](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0098898/),
[Wikipedia, Prime Suspect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Suspect)

DERIVED, and this is the most directly transferable finding in Part 1: the
defining British police drama of our exact year gets its tension from
PROCEDURE and INSTITUTIONAL FRICTION rather than from violence, and its
authority came from a real officer rather than from research. An earlier topic
in this queue covered PACE 1984 and the pre-1994 caution; this is the dramatic
argument for why that research is worth something. The procedure is not
background detail to be got right. In this tradition the procedure IS the
drama.

## Part 2. What was actually happening, and one date that explains the premise

### 2.1 The docks, and this is the finding of the topic

CITED, with figures: registered dock workers struck unofficially on 7 April
1989 against the plan to abolish the National Dock Labour Scheme. The scheme
was formally abolished on 3 July 1989 and the Abolition Bill received Royal
Assent on 6 July. The TGWU balloted three to one for a strike, which began on
10 July and collapsed.
[Bristol Radical History Group, State Intervention and the Abolition of the National Dock Labour Scheme](https://www.brh.org.uk/site/articles/abolition-of-the-national-dock-labour-scheme/),
[Hansard, Dissolution of National Dock Labour Board, 24 May 1989](https://hansard.parliament.uk/commons/1989-05-24/debates/7bf6a34d-e2b0-4c3e-9d8a-fa287d358abb/DissolutionOfNationalDockLabourBoard),
[legislation.gov.uk, Dock Work Act 1989](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1989/13/data.html)

CITED, the numbers: "There were 9,221 dockers on April 5th 1989; by October
1990, there were less than 4,000 dockers left and many ports where there were
no ex-registered dockers at all." The scheme had protected employment terms,
conditions, wages and benefits; afterwards "dockers experienced mass
disciplining, falling safety standards and constant threats of dismissal, with
shop stewards being derecognised and union rights undermined."
[Bristol Radical History Group, as above]

DERIVED: more than half the registered dock labour in Britain disappeared in
eighteen months, inside LEDGER's stated working window of 1988 to 1992, and in
some ports all of it did.

This is worth saying plainly because of what `canon.md` already contains. The
premise is a man inheriting "a half-dead criminal outfit and a book of
uncollectable debts" in a port town, with "the dockside syndicate (muscle and
smuggling, head Sera Kest, called the Widow)" as one of three rivals. A
dockside syndicate in 1990 is an organisation whose entire labour base, its
protection racket's subscribers, its smuggling accomplices and its recruiting
pool, evaporated in the previous eighteen months.

The debts are uncollectable because the men who owed them lost their jobs. The
outfit is half-dead because the trade it taxed is half-dead. The new crew can
rise because the old order's power came from a closed shop that no longer
exists. One real dated event supplies the WHY for three separate things the
canon already asserts.

I am not proposing that the game name the scheme or lecture anyone about it.
The value is that the town's economics stop being arbitrary, which is the
difference between a period setting and a period backdrop.

### 2.2 The rest of the weather, 1989 to 1992

CITED: the Community Charge provoked "widespread non-compliance, evasion
estimated in millions, and violent protests, including the largest riot in
central London since 1886 on March 31 [1990], when over 100,000 demonstrators
clashed with police, resulting in 113 injuries and 340 arrests".
[Wikipedia, Poll tax riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poll_tax_riots),
[libcom, 1989-1990: Opposition to the Poll Tax](https://libcom.org/article/1989-1990-opposition-to-the-poll-tax)

CITED: the poll tax "arrived during a period of high interest rates, rising
mortgage costs, growing personal debt and the emergence of negative equity. In
1990, house prices were falling by 10% as home repossession rates rose." At the
end of December 1990 economists warned the recession would deepen through 1991
and unemployment rise "to well over 2,000,000 from the current total of over
1,700,000".
[Economics Help, UK recession of 1991-92](https://www.economicshelp.org/macroeconomics/economic-growth/uk-recession-1991/),
[Wikipedia, 1990 in the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990_in_the_UK)

DERIVED, as texture rather than as plot: between 1989 and 1992 an ordinary
household in a British port town could be simultaneously losing work, paying a
tax it considered illegitimate and non-compliance with which was a mass
movement, and owing more on a house than the house was worth. Those three
pressures are, respectively, a reason to take a job from somebody like Tom
Novak, a reason to be already outside the law in a small way, and a reason to
be unable to leave.

That third one is worth dwelling on for a game about a small footprint.
Negative equity is what makes a person STAY in a town they want to leave.

## Part 3. What follows for LEDGER

### 3.1 The register, stated positively

`canon.md` gives the tone as "Grounded noir. Comedy: dry British wit plus
seaside-postcard smut, sparingly, so the noir holds. Tabloid and Viz-adjacent
flavour. Never GTA-style American satire." D3 adds "pun shopfronts in the
British tradition" and "Never GTA-style American satire, never joke density
that breaks tone."

From Part 1, the positive formulation those rules are reaching for:

- The engine is STANDING, not money. A scene is about who is allowed to speak
  to whom in what tone. The existing "what the town calls you" ladder is this
  mechanic and should probably be named as the register's spine rather than
  treated as a progression readout.
- Respectability is the mask, and the subject is what is underneath it. Lewis's
  achievement is named as showing "hitherto hidden English sleaze and
  seediness", and the reason the tradition is small is a national reluctance to
  look. A game whose moat is that the town KNOWS things is structurally about
  exactly that.
- Procedure is the drama, not the paperwork around it. Prime Suspect is the
  proof, in our own year.
- The crime is produced by the conditions, not imported into them. That is
  Peace, and it is the one of the three a simulation can do better than a
  script.

## Part 5. Findings and interpretation

### Findings

F1. British noir's engine is class and standing; American noir's is greed and
fate. The distinction is structural, not stylistic.

F2. Ted Lewis founded the tradition, and its defining setting is "a small,
industrial English city with its tawdry shops, dingy rooming houses, and
suffocating air of decline".

F3. David Peace's contribution is post-industrial Gothic, in which crime is
produced by economic circumstances rather than intruding upon them, with police
corruption as the spine.

F4. Prime Suspect (1991), the defining British police drama of LEDGER's exact
year, runs on procedure and institutional friction with little on-camera
violence, and drew its authority from a serving DCI.

F5. The National Dock Labour Scheme was abolished on 3 July 1989. Registered
dockers fell from 9,221 in April 1989 to under 4,000 by October 1990, with some
ports left with none.

F6. The poll tax riot of 31 March 1990 was the largest in central London since
1886: over 100,000 demonstrators, 113 injuries, 340 arrests, with mass
non-compliance nationally.

F7. House prices fell about 10% in 1990 with rising repossessions and the
emergence of negative equity; unemployment was over 1.7 million at the end of
1990 and forecast above 2 million for 1991.

F8. `canon.md`'s OPEN list still carries the engine question that D16 closed on
2026-09-10.

### Interpretation

I1. The dock labour abolition is the single most valuable thing in this topic.
It supplies, from one dated real event inside the working window, the reason
the inherited outfit is half-dead, the reason the debts are uncollectable, and
the reason a new crew can rise against an old dockside syndicate. The canon
already asserts all three; this explains them.

I2. LEDGER's "what the town calls you" ladder is not a progression readout, it
is the British noir engine. Naming it as such would give the writing a spine
that a prohibition against American satire cannot.

I3. Peace is the reference for the world and not necessarily for the law.
A cast containing a detective the player can deal with is not the Red Riding
world, and choosing how corrupt Meridian's police are is a real decision that
nobody has recorded.

I4. Negative equity is the quiet mechanic of the period: the thing that makes
people stay in a town they would rather leave. For a game whose footprint is
deliberately small, a historical reason nobody leaves is worth more than a
fence.
