# How people look at him: gaze and faint knowledge

Research note, 28 September 2026, by a separate research helper given the problem (about thirty minutes). The problem: a passer-by who has heard a little about him behaves exactly like one who has heard nothing (every head turns to him within about 5 m). What do real people do, in numbers?

## 1. How strangers normally look at a passer-by

Goffman (1963) described the polite pattern: look at the other person up to about eight feet (2.4 m), then lower the eyes as they pass, "a kind of dimming of lights". The look says "I have seen you"; dropping it says "you are of no special interest".

The best measured numbers are British. Fotios and colleagues (2015) eye-tracked 40 walkers around Sheffield, by day and after dark. Of 1,538 pedestrians in view, 73% were looked at, usually twice (median two looks). The looks came early and from far off: median distance 10.3 m (13.0 m by day, 8.9 m after dark), mostly between 4 and 18 m. Each look lasted about half a second (median 0.48 s; most between 0.16 and 0.72 s; a rare few up to 4 s). Foulsham and colleagues (2011) found the same shape on a university campus: 26% of looks at an oncoming person came in the first 3 s after they appeared, and only 4% in the last 3 s before passing.

At close range people look away. People hardly look at a live stranger who could look back, though they will watch the same person on video (Laidlaw et al. 2011). When they want to avoid eye contact, they point the head and eyes at the ground (Hessels et al. 2022). A filmed street study of 300 passers-by (Arminen and Heino 2023) gives the close-range baseline for an ordinarily dressed person: 49% gave no look at all and 46% a brief polite glance, begun at 2.5 to 3 m, median 0.06 s and never longer than 0.56 s. Only about 3% did more. Patterson and colleagues (2002) put the passing zone at 10 to 12 feet (3.0 to 3.7 m), crossed in 1 to 2 s.

So today's behaviour is wrong in two ways. Real strangers look from about 10 m, briefly and not all of them; by 5 m and closer they are looking away.

## 2. What changes when they know something

- **Longer and repeated looks.** When the same researchers walked past dressed in a way that marked them out, long looks rose about tenfold. Arminen and Heino grade the steps: two short glances (median 0.56 s in total); an "intensified" glance still held at close range (0.39 to 1.52 s, median 0.71 s); an extended look of a second or more, either eyes only or with the head turned (median about 1.9 s, longest 3.4 s). Some direct looks carried on after passing. Their line between polite and impolite is about one second.
- **Gossip changes seeing.** Faces paired with negative gossip stayed in awareness longer than faces paired with neutral or positive gossip (Anderson et al. 2011). Bad news about someone makes that person's face stand out.
- **What counts as a stare.** Face to face, the eye contact people find comfortable averages 3.3 s (most prefer 2 to 5 s; nobody wanted more than 9 s), and they want less of it from someone who seems threatening (Binetti et al. 2016). A passer-by holding eye contact for that long has gone well past polite. People who are stared at leave faster (Ellsworth et al. 1972).
- **Looking spreads.** When one person stood in a street looking up, 42% of passers-by looked up too; with fifteen, 86% did (Milgram et al. 1969). A study of 3,325 pedestrians found the same effect, levelling off well short of everyone (Gallup et al. 2012). One knower's look can pull in a companion's.
- **Not found:** no measured study of nudging a companion or lowering the voice. Those rest on the ethnography below and on common observation.

## 3. How a small community shows it knows

- Lofland (1989, 1998): villages and small towns barely have a "public realm" of strangers. Life there is private or parochial: people greet those they know, so an unknown face is news in itself. In a small port, even "heard nothing" should draw a little more attention than a city street does.
- Elias and Scotson (1965), a Leicester estate studied in the 1950s: the settled families kept newcomers in their place through gossip (blame for outsiders, praise for their own) and by avoiding contact, not by confronting them. Knowing shows as coolness and distance, not accusation.
- Milgram (1972), "familiar strangers", and Blokland and Nast (2014), "public familiarity": between stranger and acquaintance sits recognition by sight, at most a nod and seldom words. That is the natural register for faint knowledge.

## 4. Games

- *Splinter Cell: Blacklist* (Walsh 2015): keep distances and timings consistent but vary the animations and lines. Players must be able to "read" what an NPC knows, and that took spoken lines as well as animation: specific lines the first few times, generic ones after. Lines that NPCs say to each other read as natural, where callouts aimed at the player do not.
- *Hitman: Absolution* (Fauerby, GDC 2012): the crowd's response scales from a few heads turning to the whole crowd scattering. *Hitman 2* (2018): the player can shake off a suspicious look by turning his face away (Thompson 2019).
- *Fable* (2004): villagers cheer or cower according to renown. This is the theatrical version to avoid.
- *Red Dead Redemption 2* (2018): townsfolk come to recognise the character by outfit and horse (fan wiki).
- *Talk of the Town* (Ryan and Mateas 2017): belief strength decays over time, hearsay counts for less than seeing, and retelling strengthens a belief. It is a model for the strong-to-faint scale itself.
- **Readable in third person?** A viewer can judge head direction anywhere in their field of view, but eye direction only within about 4 degrees of where they are looking (Loomis et al. 2008). From a camera behind him, a look made with the eyes alone is invisible, so it must use the head. No measured figure was found for how long a look must last before a player notices it; the one-second line in section 2 is the best stand-in.

## A suggested ladder (the helper's reading, not a finding)

- **Heard nothing:** about half glance, once or twice, 0.3 to 0.6 s, from 8 to 13 m; eyes down by 3 m.
- **Faint:** more people look, a second glance, one look held into close range (0.7 to 1.5 s, head slightly turned), sometimes a glance back after passing. Nothing said to him; perhaps a word to a companion once past.
- **Strong:** 1.5 to 3 s with the head turned, a look back after passing, the companion drawn in, lowered talk. May speak to him.
- **If he looks back:** faint knowers look away at once; strong ones may hold the look.

## Numbers worth using

- 73% of passing strangers looked at, median two looks each (Fotios et al. 2015)
- Look distance median 10.3 m, interquartile 8.3 to 12.3 m; 13.0 m by day, 8.9 m after dark (Fotios et al. 2015)
- Look length median 0.48 s, interquartile 0.40 to 0.64 s (Fotios et al. 2015)
- 26% of looks in the first 3 s after someone appears; 4% in the last 3 s before passing (Foulsham et al. 2011, via Fotios et al. 2015)
- Polite look ends by about 2.4 m (Goffman 1963); polite glance starts at 2.5 to 3 m, median 0.06 s, at most 0.56 s (Arminen and Heino 2023)
- Baseline: 49% no look, 46% polite glance, about 3% more (Arminen and Heino 2023)
- Intensified glance 0.39 to 1.52 s (median 0.71 s); extended look 1 s or more (median about 1.9 s, longest 3.4 s); extended looks about ten times commoner at a marked person (Arminen and Heino 2023)
- Passing zone 3.0 to 3.7 m, crossed in 1 to 2 s (Patterson et al. 2002)
- Comfortable eye contact: 3.3 s on average, most prefer 2 to 5 s, nobody over 9 s (Binetti et al. 2016)
- Looking spreads: 42% copy one looker, 86% copy fifteen (Milgram et al. 1969)
- Eye direction readable only within about 4 degrees of where the viewer is looking; head direction readable anywhere in view (Loomis et al. 2008)

## What the game took from it (28 September)

StreetVoice's look timings (LookHoldSeconds, LookAwayMetres, LooksBack): a stranger's civil glance 0.5 s, eyes away inside 2.4 m; somebody who knows a little holds one look 1.5 s (the top of the intensified glance, past the one-second polite line) and keeps it into close range; somebody watching keeps him in view while in range and looks back after he passes. Every look turns the head, never the eyes alone.
