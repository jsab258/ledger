## 1. NPC life and schedules

**1.1 "Using Behavior Objects to Manage Complexity in Virtual Worlds" (a paper, not a talk).** Authors Martin Černý, Tomáš Plch, Matěj Marko, Jakub Gemrot, Petr Ondráček and Cyril Brom; Plch, Marko and Ondráček work at Warhorse (Marko gave the GDC 2025 talk in L1). Posted 3 August 2015, about the first game before release. Link https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.00377v1.pdf (read in full, 15 pages, 12 Oct 2026; kind: developer and university paper). It shows how a town of daily routines is built from places and things that carry their own behaviour, so a new pub or chair needs no new code. Three facts, verified from the paper: (1) a schedule names only a kind of activity such as work or relax; the town, as a parent area, picks the place, and the place says what to do there; quests change a day by taking over one slot of it, and small scenes such as two people chatting have low priority and give way to anything real; (2) the budget was up to 5 ms a frame on one processor core for all NPCs together, the system measured under 1 ms with 30 complex NPCs and under 2 ms with 300 simple ones, and one scripter filled a new village with about 60 areas and 200 objects in two days; (3) planned in 2015 and not shown as built: a short-lived area made on the spot after a murder, telling witnesses to run (unverified that it shipped). Ties: RULINGS 23 Sep (after a deed, near people look, gather and drift back); PLAN phase 3 (every visible person has a simulation identity). Content rule flag: its examples use a pub and drink. Where the lab should look in the game files later: the `ai/` tables named brain, subbrain and situation and the `LEVEL/scheduler` table (KIT section 2) for the paper's brains, scenes and day plans, then one level's `level.pak` tables for placed areas and objects.

**1.2 "Kingdom Come: Deliverance, Interview-Special Teil 5" (German; the title goes on "Künstlich intelligenter Ehebruch und denkende Türen").** Tomáš Plch, lead AI developer at Warhorse, interviewed by PC Games during a visit to a milestone meeting; published 18 April 2015, about the first game in development. **2.3 "Open World Enemy AI in Mafia III" (a book chapter posted online).** Written by Jiri Holba for Game AI Pro 360; his employer is not stated on the page, but the text speaks as the Mafia III AI team (unverified: Hangar 13). Posted on Game Developer 18 May 2020. Link https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/open-world-enemy-ai-in-mafia-iii (read in full, about 5,800 words; kind: developer article). It is a plain account of how a crowd, police and gangsters notice a player. Three facts, verified from the text: (1) every NPC has a sight detector on its head; the player gives off a "Player" event; five detection points sit on the body (head, elbows and knees by default, set by designers per pose); a thing seen is first recognised, at a speed that depends on factors such as the angle to where the NPC looks, and the same sight system sees fights, dead bodies and a drawn weapon; (2) hearing is separate from the audio engine: each sound has its own reach, a gunshot switches an NPC to battle at once, and footsteps count only after several in a short span; (3) the shipped game had 18 base archetypes and 120 variants holding senses, voice, reactions to the player and animation, and a rule that sneaking slowed recognition was dropped because "a big guy crouching in the middle of street" is not harder to notice. Ties: RULINGS D25 and D29 (half-recognition shows in posture first); the coverage audit's recognition ladder. Where the lab should look in the game files later: for Mafia: The Old Country, FModel Search for "perception", "sight", "hearing" and "detect" (KIT section 3), expecting only combat perception since 4.3 and 5.1 say it is linear; for KCD2, the `ai/AIConceptSignalDatabase` table (KIT section 2) for the signals NPCs react to.

## 3. Dialogue and quests

**3.1 "Everything Is Connected: Warhorse Studios' Quest System for Kingdom Come: Deliverance II".** Petr Nohejl, Lead DevOps Programmer at Warhorse, with host Jase Lindgren, on Perforce's podcast In Development; 14 May 2026. Link https://www.perforce.com/resources/podcasts/p4/in-development/everything-is-connected (the full transcript read, 12 Oct 2026; kind: developer interview, podcast). It also belongs to group 6. Three facts, verified from the transcript: (1) the quest tool began as a dialogue editor for the first game and now holds quest logic, random road encounters, the chatter between NPCs you overhear and cutscene scripts; it stands outside the engine so sound directors, translators and outside studios can use it; NPC navigation and behaviour trees are not in it, and the two talk by signals; (2) its "switchboard" view shows one map's quests as boxes joined by wires, with graphs inside graphs, saved as XML text; deleting one wire can block the game, so a server check loads the graphs before a designer may save, and a build farm plays quests in the real levels day and night, by priority, because separate test rooms did not work well in the first game; (3) people and time: two C# programmers full time on the tool, one to four on the C++ side and one person on the engine's quest graph; the studio had about 100 people when he joined and about 250 at the end; the first game came out in 2018, seven years after the 2011 founding, and the sequel began studio-wide in summer 2019. The page spells the tool Scout in its summary and Scald in the transcript; KIT section 2 names it Skald (unverified which spelling Warhorse uses). Ties: TOWN.md N3 and the grounded-replies brief; RULINGS 24 Sep (the claim check is a net); RULINGS 3 Oct (the AI tester's nightly walk mirrors the farm that plays quests all night). Where the lab should look in the game files later: Skald from the modding tools (KIT section 2, step 4) and the quest graph XML under `data\Quests\Final`, for how a random encounter is enabled and how the dialogue file and the logic file are kept apart.

**3.2 "Designing System Driven Dialogue in 'Mafia III'" (slides).** Rémy Boicherot, Systems Designer at Hangar 13, GDC 2017. Link https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2017/Presentations/Boicherot_DesigningSystemDriven.pdf (123 pages with speaker notes read in full; kind: speaker's slides); the video is at https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4J0KVdzx52w (title read, video unread). The talk is about three bosses whose mood about a district shows in how they speak. Three facts, verified from the slides: (1) lines are assets, not branches: about 850 lines in all (250 per boss, 100 for the player), about 20 used in one scene (2 to 3 percent) and about 120 in a whole playthrough (15 percent); each line has an ID, a play count, a last-played time, a play-once flag and conditions on game state, and the choice runs: drop played-once, test conditions, highest priority, least played, least recently played, then random; (2) there is no loyalty bar: moods (content, neutral, angry, plus ultimatum, war and dead) show only in speech and movement, players still read them, and a late extra mood could not be voiced after the recording deadline; (3) a fairness fix: a boss loses points for being ignored only after two missed districts, because plain penalties felt unfair, and characters know only what they were exposed to. Ties: D11, D33 and D34 (no reputation numbers, he sees his own position only); RULINGS 24 and 25 Sep (prepared lines, a character says only what the simulation knows); the 45-second limit on neighbours' talk could use the same played-count and last-played tests. Where the lab should look in the game files later: for Mafia: The Old Country, FModel Search for "subtitle", "dialog" and "conversation" and whether a line carries condition fields (KIT checklist D), expecting plain scripted scenes since system dialogue was Mafia III's.
