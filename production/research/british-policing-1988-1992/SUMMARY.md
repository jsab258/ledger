# British policing 1988 to 1992: what a person actually experiences

Research topic 6. One thing before the procedure, because it is better than the
procedure.

## The years we chose are the years the police stopped being believed

Three things happen inside our working window, and they are one story.

**The tape arrives.** Recording police interviews became operational in 1988,
became compulsory for serious offences in some police areas from 1 January 1992,
and only reached the rest of England and Wales on 1 January 1993. Before that,
the record of what you said in an interview room is a police officer's
handwritten note. What you said is what he wrote down.

**A whole squad collapses.** The West Midlands Serious Crime Squad was disbanded
in 1989 after allegations of evidence tampering. Over sixty convictions from its
cases have since been quashed. It was raised in Parliament in January 1989 and
shut down by the Chief Constable.

**The Birmingham Six walk out in 1991**, convicted partly on confessions taken in
that force's custody.

So our game is set precisely in the years when the country was reading about
what happens when there is no tape, while the tape was being installed. For a
game whose whole subject is what people believe, the calendar has handed us the
theme.

## The thing that most changes our design

**In 1988 to 1992, saying nothing to the police costs you nothing in court.**

The rule that lets a court hold your silence against you arrived with the 1994
Criminal Justice Act, two years after our window closes. The caution everyone
knows from television, "it may harm your defence if you do not mention...", is
not our caution.

That matters because our own measurements say honesty currently pays nothing:
truth and silence end at the same suspicion number. In period, that is right at
the police station and wrong on the street. Silence is legally free and socially
expensive, because the town is watching even when the court is not. That
asymmetry is the period's, not an invention.

I could not verify the exact pre-1994 wording from any page I could open, so I
have not put a line in anyone's mouth. Somebody should read the original before
it is spoken.

## The clock is real and it can be played

If Tom is arrested, this is the structure, and none of it is a fade to black:

- Up to 24 hours from arriving at the station.
- A review at six hours, by an inspector who is not on the investigation.
- Further reviews at least every nine hours.
- A superintendent can add 12 hours, to 36.
- Beyond that it takes magistrates, up to 96 hours.
- The custody sergeant is at least a sergeant, is independent of the
  investigation, and opens a custody record which the detainee can later inspect.

Our own notes say the arrest tile is empty and that "an arrest that leads
nowhere is a consequence that stops". This is what stops it stopping, and it is a
real schedule with named hours rather than something we would have to invent.

## The courtroom scene the period gives us, and the modern one it does not

I have to correct something here, because my first searches returned modern
procedure and two pieces of it did not exist yet.

"Plea before venue", the thing where you indicate a plea and the magistrates
decide where you are tried, is from 1997. Not ours.

What we have instead is committal. In our window, a case heading for the Crown
Court went through a hearing at which **the defence could require prosecution
witnesses to attend and be cross-examined**. Old-style committals, effectively a
mini-trial, and they were abolished in 1996.

Think about what that is for us. A witness who told a rumour to the whole street
can be made to say it on oath, in front of magistrates, months later, and be
taken apart. For a game about who saw what and who will repeat it, that is the
scene, and it vanishes from English law four years after our game is set.

## One smaller thing that lined up neatly

The rules on identification parades require that the suspect and their solicitor
be given "the description of the suspect as first given by any eye-witness".

That is our identification ladder in legal language. Our rungs are literally "a
man, big, long coat" and "the one with the limp". The law says the defence gets
to see exactly that first description. We already store it.

## One tension with canon, noted and not pressed

Canon says the police are "corruptible as individuals, never as a thesis". The
period's actual texture is the opposite: the defining story of these years is an
entire squad, disbanded, with sixty convictions quashed.

I am not recommending a change and canon outranks my file. The individual framing
is probably the better game, because a police force that is rotten as a body
leaves the player nowhere to stand and would make Mara Ellis impossible. But it
is a deliberate departure from the period rather than an accident, and anyone
researching this will hit it and assume canon is wrong.

## How much to trust this

Less than the other topics, and I want to be straight about it. The government's
legislation website is blocked from where I work, so **I did not read a single
piece of primary law.** Every date and duration above is a summary of a page
about the law rather than the law.

Worse, the police codes of practice have been rewritten many times since 1986,
and every version my searches found was from 2019 or 2023. So where I describe
what a detained person is entitled to, that is the modern rulebook. The 1984 Act
itself is stable; the detail on top of it is not.

The one thing I most want and could not get: the 1992 tape-recording order
applied to "specified police areas" and I could not find out which. Whether a
northern port town would have been in the first group is a real question with a
real answer, sitting in a document I cannot open.

I also found nothing at all on what a provincial police station physically looked
like in 1990: the front counter, the charge room, the cells. That is the
equivalent of the measured pub plan the atlas did, and it does not exist yet.
