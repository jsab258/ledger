# Kerbside rules and street furniture, England 1990

Read 17 Nov 2026, by a lane helper

Scope: yellow lines and their plates, bus stop flags, pedestrian guard railing (BS 3049), street name plates, as they stood in a northern English town in 1990. Every page below was opened on 17 Nov 2026. Search snippets were not used as evidence. Anything not confirmed from an opened primary or reputable page is marked UNVERIFIED.

## 1. Yellow lines, time plates and kerb marks

### What 1990 looked like (TSRGD 1981 regime)

- Double yellow lines in 1990 did not mean "no waiting at any time". A Commons written answer of March 1991 says they marked a restriction of at least eight hours between 7 am and 7 pm on at least four days a week, with the actual times shown on plates along the street.
  - Source: HC Deb 11 March 1991 vol 187 c400W, https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1991/mar/11/parking (read 17 Nov 2026).
  - Quote (as summarised from the opened page, wording close to the original): double yellow lines restrict waiting "for at least eight hours between 7 am and 7 pm on at least four days a week"; the times "are shown on plates placed at regular intervals" along the road.
- So yes: in 1990 double yellow lines needed upright time plates beside them, at intervals. This is confirmed for double lines by the Hansard answer.
- Single yellow lines: plates were needed. The 1994 rules (below) carried that requirement forward for the single line (1994 diagram 1017). That single lines also needed plates under the 1981 rules is very likely but was not read in the 1981 text itself: UNVERIFIED for 1990 specifically.
- Broken yellow lines also existed under the 1981 rules. The 1994 Regulations name 1981 diagrams 1016.1, 1018, 1020 and 1021 as old markings that stayed lawful only until 1 January 1999. The exact look of each 1981 diagram was not read: UNVERIFIED.
  - Source: SI 1994/1519, regulation 3(2)(b), https://legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1994/1519/body/made/data.pdf (read 17 Nov 2026). Quote: "diagrams 1016.1, 1018, 1020 and 1021 in Schedule 2 to the 1981 Regulations".
- Exception: inside a controlled parking zone, the hours are on the zone entry signs and the lines need no plates. That is in the 1965 Commercial Motor piece and in 1994 Direction 23(2).

### When the plate rule changed

- TSRGD 1994 (in force 12 August 1994) brought in "a rationalised system of yellow lines for waiting restrictions". From then on, double yellow (diagram 1018.1) was only for restrictions that apply at all times, or every day for at least four consecutive months. But Direction 23(1) still listed 1018.1 among the markings that need an upright sign (signs 637, 637.2, 637.3 or 642.3). So from 1994 to 2002 double yellows still needed a plate.
  - Source: SI 1994/1519 explanatory note, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1994/1519/note/made/data.pdf (read 17 Nov 2026). Quote: "a rationalised system of yellow lines for waiting restrictions".
  - Source: SI 1994/1519 Schedule 19 (General Directions), https://legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1994/1519/schedule/19/made and https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1994/1519/schedule/19/made/data.pdf (read 17 Nov 2026). Direction 20 quote: 1018.1 is for a restriction "which applies on that side of the road at all times of day on every day of the year". Direction 23 table, item 2: 1018.1 with signs 637, 637.2, 637.3 or 642.3.
  - Date in force: London Tribunals key case Cooper v Richmond, https://www.londontribunals.gov.uk/sites/default/files/keycases/Cooper-v-Richmond.pdf (read 17 Nov 2026), notes the 1994 Regulations came into force on 12 August 1994.
- TSRGD 2002 (SI 2002/3113): 1018.1 no longer appears in the Direction 25 table of markings that need a sign. The table lists only 1017, 1019 and 1020.1. So the plate requirement for "at any time" double yellows ended with TSRGD 2002 (in force early 2003; that exact date was not read: UNVERIFIED). Under 2002, a 637.3 plate goes with 1018.1 only where that sign is used, for example for a seasonal restriction (the seasonal reading is UNVERIFIED).
  - Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/3113/schedule/19/part/IIn2/made/data.pdf (read 17 Nov 2026). Quote: a marking "may be placed on a road only in conjunction with, and on the same side of the road as," a sign in the table. 1017: "637.2, 639, 639.1B, 640 or 650.3"; 1018.1 is not listed.
- Summary for the game (1990): every yellow line, double or single, has upright time plates at intervals, unless the street is in a controlled parking zone. No "at any time" double yellows without plates.

### Plate diagram number and look in 1990

- The 1981 plate diagram numbers and colours were not confirmed. The 1981 text was blocked (see Unreached). In the 1994 and 2002 rules the time plates are diagrams 637.2, 638, 638.1, 639, 639.1A/B and 640. ### Kerb loading marks (yellow kerb blips)

- Yes, in use long before 1990. Commercial Motor, 8 January 1965, "Markings for loading bans", describes yellow kerb marks for loading prohibitions, with the times on plates nearby, usually on lamp posts.
  - Source: https://archive.commercialmotor.com/article/8th-january-1965/28/markings-for-loading-bans (read 17 Nov 2026). Quotes: "yellow kerb marks"; "The kerb marks will run up the face of the kerb and extend some 10 to 12 in, across the pavement"; "about 10 ft. apart"; "The times during which these prohibitions apply will be shown on plates erected alongside (normally on lamp posts)."
- The 1994 rules keep them as 1019 (loading restriction at set times) and 1020.1 (at all times / four months or more), each needing a plate (Schedule 19, Direction 21 and 23). Under the 1981 rules a single mark versus a double mark meant different times. That mapping was not read: UNVERIFIED.

## 2. Bus stop flags after deregulation (1986)

- The regulation sign outside London is TSRGD diagram 970. It shows Margaret Calvert's bus pictogram and the words "Bus stop" in the Transport typeface. Operators could add their name, route numbers or stop name, drop the symbol, and pick their own colours as long as they contrasted. Posts and the backs of 970/971 signs may be any colour (1994 Directions 36(4) and 37(3)).
  - Source: The Beauty of Transport, "Flying the Flag", 9 Mar 2016, https://thebeautyoftransport.com/2016/03/09/flying-the-flag-beautiful-and-not-so-beautiful-british-bus-stop-flags/ (read 17 Nov 2026). Quotes: "The non-London sign features Calvert's standard bus vehicle pictogram, and uses the Transport typeface"; under the National Bus Company, flags were "pretty much exactly diagram 970 reproduced in metal"; after 1986 "each local bus operator wanted to affix their own bus stop flag to the bus stop pole", a "totem-pole effect". Reputable enthusiast blog, not primary: treat the design notes as well sourced but secondary.
  - Source: SI 1994/1519 Schedule 19, https://legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1994/1519/schedule/19/made (read 17 Nov 2026), for the post colour freedom. The 1981 wording for 970 was not read: UNVERIFIED for 1990.
- So a 1990 northern stop plausibly shows an old NBC-style or council 970 flag (white, black bus outline, "Bus stop"), with one or more operator flags or name strips added after 1986. Several flags on one pole is period-correct.
- Flag size: a 1971 Commercial Motor piece on Teesside's switch to the DoE sign describes a white flag with a black bus outline, a band in the operator's colour, black lettering and 1 in route numerals, measuring (scan reads) "1ft x 1ft 6in" (about 300 x 450 mm).
  - Source: https://archive.commercialmotor.com/article/30th-april-1971/23/teesside-goes-for-doe-signs (read 17 Nov 2026). Quotes: "white in colour", "black .outline of a bus", "Measuring Ift x Ift 6in" (OCR; read as 1 ft x 1 ft 6 in). One town, 1971: the size for 1990 is UNVERIFIED as a general rule.
- Pole height, pole diameter, and pole band colours: not found on any opened page. UNVERIFIED. (A common modern pole is a galvanised or painted tube about 76 mm across. That is from memory, not from a source: UNVERIFIED.)
- Timetable cases: the same 2016 article shows timetable cases on poles, sometimes above the flag, but gives no date for when they became common. That they were common on rural and suburban poles in 1990: UNVERIFIED.

## 3. Pedestrian guard railing, BS 3049:1976

- BS 3049:1976, "Specification. Pedestrian guard rails (metal)", published 31 March 1976, withdrawn 15 November 1995. BS 7818:1995 is its successor. The BSI page does not say "replaced by", so the succession comes from the dates and from industry practice: partly UNVERIFIED. So BS 3049 was the standard in force in 1990.
  - Source: BSI Knowledge, https://knowledge.bsigroup.com/products/specification-pedestrian-guard-rails-metal (read 17 Nov 2026). Quotes: "Specification. Pedestrian guard rails (metal)"; withdrawn 15 November 1995.
- The standard's own text is paywalled and was not read. No figure below comes from BS 3049 itself. All dimensions are UNVERIFIED for 1990 and come from later BS 7818 era drawings and makers, which carried the classic design forward:
  - Panel length 2000 mm (Bracknell Forest drawing SD/400/01A: "2000 Length to suit"), https://www.bracknell-forest.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2021-08/pedestrian-and-cycle-restraint-systems.pdf (read 17 Nov 2026).
  - Infill: "12 dia. mild steel infill bars" at "a maximum pitch of 110" mm (same Bracknell drawing). First Fence also gives "12mm in diameter set at 110mm centres", https://firstfence.co.uk/pedestrian-guard-rail-kit-with-visibility-gap-pg2-bs-7818-1-0m-wide (read 17 Nov 2026).
  - Height: First Fence "Installed height of 1.0m with 50mm ground clearance". IAE spec sheet "900mm actual panel (leg 1350mm o/all)", https://iae.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/PEDESTRIAN-GUARDRAIL-PDF-SPEC.pdf (read 17 Nov 2026). So about 1.0 m to the top rail.
  - Frame and posts: First Fence frame 50 x 30 mm RHS, legs buried 400 mm. Bracknell posts "25 x 50 x 2.5 rectangular hollow section". The Nottinghamshire high-visibility drawing SD/3/36A states "Design and construction to BS 3049" and lists a "70 x 8" member (probably the flat top rail; label garbled), https://www.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/media/3764461/sd336a-high-visibility-pedestrian-guardrail.pdf (read 17 Nov 2026).
  - Distance from kerb face: not found. The Bracknell drawing shows "450 minimum" but its label is unclear. UNVERIFIED.
- Visibility variant: yes, at least by the time of the Nottinghamshire drawing, which is built to BS 3049 and is titled high visibility guardrail. Its infill is staggered in 4 or 8 rows depending on the sight angle. A modern "visibility gap" is a 200 mm open strip across the top of the panel (First Fence). Whether the high-visibility (staggered infill) type was common on 1990 streets, and its date: UNVERIFIED. The plain vertical-bar panel is the safe default for 1990.

## 4. Street name plates c.1990

- Street name plates are not traffic signs in law. They rest on the Public Health Act 1925 and similar acts, so no Transport-typeface rule applied to them.
  - Source: The Beauty of Transport, "How Serifs Lost the Road War but Won the Streets", 3 Nov 2021, https://thebeautyoftransport.com/2021/11/03/how-serifs-lost-the-road-war-but-won-the-streets/ (read 17 Nov 2026). Quotes: "Unlike all other road signs, street name signs are not legally mandated road signs"; "a Ministry of Transport circular of 1952 made Kindersley's serif typeface one of several suggested"; "the typeface that eventually won out in vast swathes of the country was Kindersley MOT"; for 1970s to 90s estates "Kindersley MOT was the visual identity of your neighbourhood". Secondary source.
  - Source: roads.org.uk, "From War to Worboys: Anderson and Kindersley", https://www.roads.org.uk/articles/war-worboys/anderson-and-kindersley (read 17 Nov 2026). Quote: "In 1950, the MOT bought his streetnameplate alphabet, calling it 'Kindersley'".
- So no: the Worboys / Kinneir-Calvert Transport style was not the recommended street name style from 1965. The recommended faces from the 1950s were the Ministry alphabets including Kindersley's serif capitals. Transport did appear on some council plates (Camden is cited), but a 1990 northern town more likely had black capitals on white in Kindersley MOT or an older local style. That a "Circular Roads 1965" recommended Transport for name plates: not found, treat as UNVERIFIED and probably wrong.
- Later guidance, three years after the game's year: DfT Circular Roads 3/93, Appendix B, recommends sans serif capitals, black on white with a black border, and a 100 mm capital height. It shows a "Pre-1965 Revised Standard" alphabet and "Transport Heavy" as examples. This is 1993, so it describes the trend just after 1990, not the rule in 1990.
  - Source: GeoPlace, https://www.snn.geoplace.co.uk/department-for-transport-circular-roads-393-appendix-b (read 17 Nov 2026). Quotes: "Authorities are recommended to employ "sans serif" lettering on reflectorised name plates"; "Capital lettering should be used"; "A 100mm actual capital letter height of lettering is the recommended standard"; "90mm may be used" with broader alphabets; "75mm" where space is tight; "150mm" on fast main roads; "black lettering on a white background with a black border"; posts "Aircraft grey No. 693 to BS 381c".
  - Mounting (Appendix A): West Oxfordshire copy, https://www.westoxon.gov.uk/media/dtrfuuua/dft-recommendations-on-installation-of-street-name-plates.pdf (read 17 Nov 2026). Quotes: "the lower edge of the plate is approximately 1 metre above the ground"; "approximately 2.5 metres where obscuration is a problem"; "never be lower than 0.6 metres or higher than 3.6 metres".
- Plate size and material: no opened source gave a typical cast aluminium plate size. A plate about 200 to 250 mm tall for 75 to 100 mm capitals follows from the letter heights, but that is an inference: UNVERIFIED. Cast aluminium (raised letters) and pressed or flat enamelled steel were both period options: UNVERIFIED.
