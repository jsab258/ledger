## (a) Disco Elysium (2019; Final Cut 2021)

How talk works as a verb
- Skills speak. 4 stats, 6 skills each, 24 voices. Skills interject as inner voices and the player may say a line aloud or keep it (1). "Your skills talk to you" is the stated core idea (1).
- Passive checks: a skill fires on its own if the number is high enough, and the player only gets a line. Active checks: dice-roll options locked behind a skill (6). Kotaku (6) says many options are locked behind checks; a failed check can be retried after putting a point into the stat. The "white" and "red" labels are not in any source I reached, so not confirmed here.
- A failed check can be the funniest result, and a light bulb bursts on screen (5). Retry is allowed on some checks, so trying at bad odds is often worth it (5).
- What a choice changes: thoughts (the skills comment, and some thoughts carry forward) (1, 5); small later lines (shaving a beard changes a later talk with Kim about aftershave) (3); insulting someone can close that person off or be remembered (6). Writers estimate "probably thousands" of such small reactions (3). Trials are resolved by talk, not combat (6).
- Choices per exchange: no source gives a count. Kotaku (5) describes short, Twitter-length replies, one click each, with the Continue button changing colour when a skill is about to speak.
- Length: no per-conversation figure found. One Steam reviewer reports over 1 million words (7); a reviewer-summary site says players estimate about 90 per cent of play is reading and listening (8, a player estimate, not measured).

Numbers
- Over 1 million words (7, repeating the developer figure; the Dualshockers piece with the developer quote appeared in search but was not opened).
- 428 new dialogue cards for one four-way call-sign choice (3).
- Tool strain: Articy froze under the volume; "we were writing too much" (Hindpere, 4). Some characters took a month or two each to write (4).

Developer statements
- Non-combat skill use is underdeveloped and should carry combat's tension (1).
- Kurvitz wanted readers to forget they were reading, and no long blocks of exposition (2).
- Keenan: the dialogue is for texture, not only for plot; minor characters get histories that may never pay off (3).

Critics
- Kotaku (6): "less traditional RPG, more visual novel"; no combat; physical acts also run through checks. Kotaku (5): the click loop is addictive, no real complaints.

## (b) L.A. Noire (2011; remaster 2017)

- Verb: question a suspect; pick Truth, Doubt or Lie (9). Lie often needs evidence shown; Doubt is a middle that is hard to predict (9). Player reads face animation against what is already known (9). Developers avoided extra HUD meters (9).
- Remaster renamed options Good Cop, Bad Cop, Accuse so the player can judge Cole's tone (10). AV Club (11) says the original working names were Coax, Force, Accuse.
- Complaints: labels too vague; players pick wrongly with a correct read of the case (9). Doubt often led Cole to angry accusations and threats, not mild doubt (10). Evidence-to-proof link is weak and players collect "just in case" and use trial and error (11). Reloading to retry is tedious (11). The reviewer in 11 still failed every option after the relabel; the writer in 10 preferred the old labels.
- Praise: interrogations felt more intense than the action; script, acting and face tech carry it (9).
- No counts of exchange length found. No player table: reviews on GameSpot and Giant Bomb appeared in search but were not opened. Evidence is critics and blogs only, 3 sources.

## (c) Red Dead Redemption 2 (2018; PC 2019)

- Verb: holster, focus on a person, then greet or antagonise; a defuse button press and surrender exist (13, 14). Greeting may get a hello; antagonising a sheriff made him draw (13). Repeated antagonising leads to a fight or a duel (14). Hitting first is a dishonourable crime with a wanted level (14).
- Honour: good acts raise it and bad lower it; rank unlocks discounts and outfits (13).
- It runs on anyone, anywhere, one button, no menu. Conversation is short and ends in a reaction, not a branch (12, 13).
- Praise: "probably the most finely-tuned piece of world building" (12, a critic). Players use it for duels with anyone (15: three Reddit posters positive; one called it a top open-world game).
- Punishment: none found in my sources. The system is shallow in content by design. Lack of complaints reflects my sources, not proof.
- No developer quotes reached.

## (d) Kingdom Come: Deliverance II (2025)

- Six dialogue stats: Persuasion, Coercion, Impression, Domination, Presence, Intimidation (16). Guide (17) lists Speech, Charisma, Might, Coercion, Domination, Dread; usually one or two options show per talk (17). The two sources name them differently, so the exact set is unconfirmed.
- Charisma is tied to clothes and cleanliness, and matters most with nobles and townspeople (17). Noble clothes do not help with bandits (16). Excess intimidation hurts reputation (17). KCD 1 wiki (18): your numbers are compared with the other person's; you see theirs only if you know them or read people well.
- Resolution: past help can skip a check entirely (a tavern owner agrees to play dice because Henry helped earlier; this is a game-of-chance quest, so noted only as the source's example, not as a reference for our design) (16). Quests branch into different ends, and a theft request can be reported to the rival's leader (16).
- Critic (16): talk is "far more engaging and impactful" than in KCD 1; occasional mismatch between Henry's words or tone and the option chosen.

## (e) Numbers and typed-AI talk

- Choices per minute and share of play in dialogue: no published figure found for any of the four. Closest: ~90 per cent reading and listening (player estimate, 8).
- Facade (19): typed free text appears as subtitles; real-time, can interrupt. About 40 discourse acts; template rules turn text into an act. Session about 20 minutes; about 200 beats in the pool, about 18 per story, one beat roughly a minute; authors expect 6 or 7 replays. Never an error message: a character deflects ("hold on") or changes subject. Known failure: false positives, and players can say millions of things but characters answer in hundreds (19).
- Modern AI NPCs (20, Apr 2024, demos): praise: voice talk felt more immersive than menus, and "say anything" is a neat trick. Punish: seconds of lag; NPCs dodged questions and felt empty; conversation funnelled to a few preset outcomes (the NPC refused ideas until he said the wanted one); freedom to speak is not freedom to act. 1 critic, 1 piece. Spector: open, if trained on game content (20).

## What the sources say makes talk feel like play

- The line costs something and the result is shown at once: failed-check light bulb and retry (5, 6).
- A second voice that argues, not only the NPC: skills as inner voices (1, 5).
- Short replies, one click, a cue before a thought arrives (5).
- Small memory of what the player did: thousands of tiny reactions (3); past help skips a check (16).
- Who you are visibly changes the odds: clothes, cleanliness, reputation (16, 17, 18).
- Anyone can be addressed, with a clear tone choice and a visible reaction (12, 13, 14).
- Labels must tell the player what the character will do: the L.A. Noire complaint (9, 10, 11); KCD2 has a smaller version (16).
- Evidence should visibly connect to the claim it breaks; guessing "just in case" kills it (11).
- Free text: never show an error; deflect in character (19). The risk is funnelling and empty answers (20). Lag hurts (20).
- Volume is a cost: text-heavy design is the main barrier to buying (8) and broke the writers' tool (4).
