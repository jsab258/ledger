## (a) Nemesis: how the orc shows it remembers

Mechanisms (concrete):
- Dialogue tied to history. De Plater: the orcs' dialogue relates to their memory of what the player did (1). An orc captain who killed the player taunts him next time, and escaped foes "remember and reassure you" he will not be so lucky (6, PC Gamer, 2017). A 2014 Kotaku writer, after two failed kills, was taunted by the orc and said he hoped for revenge later (5).
- Return after "death": killed orcs may "cheat death and come back" with a scar (1).
- Betrayal: a defeated orc may come back angry and betray the player; an abandoned captured orc is betrayed (1).
- Revenge: defeating an orc you had a grudge with can give a revenge mission, and the reward gear reflects the relationship (1). In the 2014 design a player's death creates a vendetta mission in a friend's game (2).
- Rank and power struggles: orcs compete for rank, and the system keeps producing Showdown missions against war chiefs (2).
- Loyalty: rescued orcs "become even more loyal" (1).
- Frequency: no source I reached gives a number for how often a named orc recurs. Source 1 says the team focused on "five or six or seven" memorable orcs (a design focus, not a rate). Source 6 gives none either. Not reached: any measured recurrence rate.

Developer statements:
- Goal stated at GDC 2018 (Chris Hoge, Monolith): "orcs that the player loved to hate" (3). The talk covered making orcs memorable, "exceptional moments", and keeping the relationships going (3, blurb only).
- De Plater says the most memorable bosses are those shaped by the player, "totally unique to your game", not written into the story (2). The living world runs separately from the story and meets it at a few beats (2).
- A faction morale and discipline layer was built and scrapped in development; features like dominate and warchief ambush grew from it (4). Lesson: a simpler per-person relationship won over a faction-wide stat.
- After launch the studio admitted that buying orc followers "risked undermining the heart" of the system and that knowing the purchases existed "reduces the immersion" (8).

Critics:
- PC Gamer 2017: the feuds are "easily the best thing" in the game, because orcs have varied traits; the orcs kept the critic playing as he lost interest in busywork (6).
- Tom's Guide 2017: "technically impressive and mechanically novel" but "not enough to carry an entire game"; the main campaign "rarely leverages" the player's own Nemesis history, so the game feels like two games (7). Same review: loot boxes were "the most efficient way" to get items for postgame quests and "would simply be better without them"; the late game is a steep grind (7). Confirmed by the studio's own removal of them, with the Shadow Wars endgame called too grindy without spending and too easy to bypass with it (8, 3 Apr 2018).

## (b) KCD2: how the world shows it knows

Mechanisms:
- Reputation is kept per place and per group (soldiers, villagers, monks, traders); a person's opinion feeds the group's, which feeds the village's (9). A village reaches 100 when a majority of each group is favourable (9). A map icon per settlement shows how content it is (10).
- Witnesses: they report crimes to a guard or confront you; undiscovered crimes can still be found out and reported later (10). Heavy crime in one area in a short time causes "Unrest": residents arm, guards call reinforcement (10).
- Low soldier reputation makes guards far likelier to stop and search you; being seen committing a crime in one town drops that group's standing sharply (9).
- Branding: a PC Gamer writer, after a failed speech check on a 10,646 groschen fine, took a brand instead of jail. Townsfolk shunned him, a woman he had just saved shouted "You're that criminal!", merchants refused trade, reputation gains were halved, a second serious crime could mean execution, and it ran on a timer (11).
- Remedy: indulgence or a priest's pilgrimage; quests for locals (10).
- Warhorse (Klima, 2024): NPC lines react to specific acts (theft, trespass, finding a body, an emptied chest), "even depending on what was stolen" reactions differ; reputation is "more fine-grained"; about half the recorded lines are not story (13).
- PC Gamer review: a shopkeeper notices you wearing the armour you stole; the reviewer was chased from a fortress after passing the man he stole a ring from (12).
- Not reached: sources for being recognised in the same clothes as a general rule, washing blood off, and stocks. The review above (12) is the only recognition-by-item case.
- Design limit: Warhorse said the true historical punishment would be too harsh, so the game is softened (14, about game 1).

Critics: the review says the world "happens independent of you", but it "pulls its punches" on some choices and lets you get away with a lot (12). The branding piece: "It sucks, but it also kinda rules" (11, quote of the writer).

## What the sources say makes remembering felt, not just present

- It is said to your face by a named person, with the history in the words (orc taunts, 1, 6, 5; "You're that criminal!", 11).
- The consequence changes what you can do: refused trade, halved gains, a timer, a worse penalty next time (11); guards searching more often (9).
- It is a person-by-person memory that rolls up to a place, not a single global number (9, 10).
- It comes back later and unannounced: a crime found out after the fact (10), a guard recalling last week's trick (17, B1), an orc returning with a scar (1).
- It is tied to the exact act or item: different reaction by what was stolen (13); recognised wearing the stolen goods (12).
- The memory must be used by the main story as well; when the campaign ignores it, critics call it two games (7), and a player was bothered when KCD2 dialogue forgot game 1 (17, B5).
- Hard to believe once money buys it away (8).
- It should not be too harsh to be borne: Warhorse softened crime punishment (14); the branded player still wondered if it was "too realistic" (11).
- Players love it enough to ask for it in other games (16, patent complaints).
