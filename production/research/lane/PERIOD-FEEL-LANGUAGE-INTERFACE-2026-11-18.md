# Period feel through language and interface (18 November 2026)

Summary: yes, period can come through words and interface. The cheapest wins are a 1990 style sheet for every written line, the Ledger written as a period account book, 1990 money in prices and talk, evening-paper contents bills on the loading screen, and typed letters and forms found in the world. A Teletext look is possible but needs a font decision first.

Written by a research helper for Jafar's open question, about 25 minutes, 18 November 2026. Games and shows studied from the outside only: reviews, interviews and talks, never their files. Tags: (S) source read; (SN) search snippet only; (P) the project's own earlier research; (J) judgement, not a source; (E) estimate. Pages that could not be reached are listed at the end and are not used as evidence.

## 1. How games and films carry a period in words and interface

| Work | What carries the period, besides pictures | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Mafia III (2016, set 1968) | Nothing in the playlist is from after 1968: the team kept a hard cut-off so the songs feel current, not like oldies. Store signs and radio hosts set the mood. The story is framed by documentary footage shot in the present day, looking back. Its pedestrians use period slurs; LEDGER cannot (canon: no slurs of any kind). | Game Informer, Ben Reeves, 19 Oct 2015 (S); GameSpot review, 2016 (S) |
| Her Story (2015, set 1994) | The whole game is a police computer desktop. Barlow mixed Apple II, Windows 3.1 and Windows 98 looks. He ran the footage through two VHS players to age it, because filters failed. The keyboard sounds were recorded from an old keyboard, panned by key position; he called the sound "all about authenticity". The opening screen is kept slightly too long to set the pace. | Wikipedia summary citing Pocket Gamer, 13 Oct 2015, and Barlow's talk of 1 Oct 2015 (S, second hand) |
| Papers, Please (2013) | The period and regime are carried by paperwork the player handles. Pope: "The bureaucratic structure is something that fits naturally"; he kept the setting "fairly generic" and credits technical simplicity. | Game Developer (Gamasutra), Leigh Alexander, 3 Sep 2013 (S) |
| Disco Elysium (2019) | Dialogue is "primarily aesthetic or textural, as opposed to instrumental": people get odd pasts and opinions that make the world feel lived in, and the game remembers small choices ("micro-reactivity"). The talk column was modelled on how people skim a feed. | Game Developer on Justin Keenan's GDC talk, 21 Jul 2021 (S); 80.lv, 14 Jan 2020 (S) |
| Kentucky Route Zero (2013 to 2020) | Conversations that always move forward rather than a hub of questions. Nothing reached on its typography or old-computer screens. | Funambulism interview with Cardboard Computer, 23 Jul 2013 (S) |
| Life on Mars (BBC, 2006, set 1973) | Period carried by talk and attitudes, set against a modern outsider. Possessions were older than the year, because people could not afford new things. The director avoided a nostalgia trip. LEDGER keeps the "older than the year" idea and drops the slurs. | The York Historian, 17 Oct 2016 (S) |
| Alan Wake 2 (2023) | The menu-like space is a detective's room with a corkboard; save points are thermoses in the world. No period detail found. Useful as the model for in-world saving and case boards. | Fangoria, 15 Jun 2023, updated 16 Mar 2024 (S) |
| GTA Vice City (2002, set 1986) | Nine stations plus a tape slot, two of them talk stations, with DJs and adverts; music mostly from the 1980s. The NME retrospective could not be reached. | GTABase radio page (S) |

What these share (J):
- The interface is a prop from the world (Her Story's desktop, Papers, Please's documents, Alan Wake 2's room).
- A hard date rule on content (Mafia III's music cut-off) is a cheap, checkable discipline.
- Things older than the year feel more true than things exactly of the year (Life on Mars).
- Texture in talk beats information in talk (Disco Elysium).

## 2. 1990 British cues, with sources

### Money in 1990
- Notes (Bank of England, withdrawn notes page) (S):
  - £5: the new smaller Series E note was first issued 7 June 1990 (135 x 70 mm). The old larger Series D fiver (145 x 78 mm) stayed legal tender until 29 November 1991. So in late 1990 two sizes of fiver were in pockets at once. That is a good small cue.
  - £10: Series D (151 x 85 mm) all through 1990; the new £10 came 29 April 1992.
  - £20: Series D (160 x 90 mm) in 1990; the new £20 came 5 June 1991.
  - £50: Series D, from 1981.
  - £1 note: ceased to be legal tender in England on 11 March 1988. Scottish £1 notes were not researched.
- Coins (predecimal.com) (S): 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p (since 1982), 50p (the large one until 1997), £1 coin (since 1983).
  - 5p was made smaller in 1990 (24 mm to 18 mm), so both sizes appear that year. When the old one stopped being legal tender was not found.
  - 10p stayed large until 1992.
  - No £2 coin in circulation (from 1997). No halfpenny (demonetised December 1984).
- Stamps (edinphoto rate table) (S): first class 20p and second class 15p from 2 October 1989; 22p and 17p from 17 November 1990; 24p and 18p from 16 September 1991.
- VAT: 15% in 1990. Norman Lamont raised it to 17.5% in his budget of 19 March 1991 (Wikipedia, 1991 budget) (S). The date it took effect was not confirmed (E: spring 1991).
- Real banknote designs: whether a game may reproduce them was not researched. Canon bars real logos. Recommend generic notes in the right colours and sizes, never a copy (J).

### Teletext and Ceefax
- Ceefax broadcast from 23 September 1974 with 30 pages; ORACLE on ITV from 1975 (Wikipedia, Teletext) (S).
- The screen is a grid of 24 rows of 40 characters (S). Eight colours by control code: black, red, green, yellow, blue, magenta, cyan, white; flashing text; double height and double width; block mosaic graphics, joined or separated (Wikipedia, Teletext character set) (S).
- The UK character set puts £ where # sits elsewhere, with ½ and arrows in place of some signs (S).
- Pages are numbered from 100 upward (S). About two million teletext sets by 1982 (S); the 1990 figure was not found.
- Ceefax and ORACLE are real brand names. Canon says the regional TV channel is still owed by the brand bible, so any teletext service would carry that minted name (P, canon).

### Typed and carbon forms; police paperwork
- A 1990 office letter is most likely a crisp daisy-wheel impression in Courier or Prestige Elite, not a worn manual machine; a back office might still have an old manual (P, PERIOD-PRINT-AND-FONTS, 1 Oct 2026).
- Carbon copies, rubber stamps: not sourced (P marks them a HOLE). Blue-violet carbon blur is a judgement.
- Police pocket notebooks: serial-numbered, pages never removed, errors struck through once, ends ruled off and signed (P, same note, citing its sources).
- Witness statements under section 9 of the Criminal Justice Act 1967 carry a signed declaration that the account is true to the best of the maker's knowledge and belief, knowing that falsehood can be prosecuted (legislation.gov.uk) (S). The page read shows the current text, amended since; the exact 1990 printed form was not seen.
- Interviews were noted by hand before tape; taping became routine across England and Wales only from 1 January 1993 (P, british-policing-1988-1992). The pre-1994 caution's wording is unverified (P): no one speaks it until the original is read.

### Phones and pagers
- The KX100 phone box arrived in 1985; about 3,000 a month went in from 1987 to 1989; BT had 81,000 housed payphones in 1989 (Wikipedia, KX telephone boxes) (S). Cardphones began in July 1981 (S).
- BT's Landis & Gyr phonecard payphone (brochure dated "4/91" in its code): the display counts down the units left, flashes as they run out, a tone warns up to 30 seconds before cut-off, and a button lets the caller change card (britishtelephones.com) (S).
- Green optical cards and unit sizes: search snippets only, not confirmed.
- Pagers: the Science Museum's BT Motorola tone pager is dated 1980 to 1990. Pagers peaked in the 1980s; early models made the caller ring back from a phone; the UK commercial pager network closed in 2001 (Science Museum Group) (S). Tone versus numeric pagers in 1990: not confirmed.
- BT's logo is real. Its 1981 yellow and purple identity gave way to the "piper" on 2 April 1991 (P, snippet). Use the brand bible's owed telephone operator, never BT's mark (canon).

### Newspapers and headlines
- British headlines pile nouns ("Nude pic row vicar resigns") and lean on short words: probe, slam, row, quiz, shame, brave, pal. The style goes back at least to 1970 (Language Log, 5 Mar 2009, with commenters) (S). Leave out "tot" and the like: no children anywhere.
- Contents bills, four or five words, stood outside newsagents to sell the evening paper (P, snippet).
- Local evening papers were black and white with spot colour at most in 1990 (P, Grimsby paper, by inference).

### Clock and dates
- Railways went to the 24-hour clock in timetables in 1964 to 1965 (SN: Commercial Motor headline, 11 Sep 1964; Railnews, 2009).
- Speech used the 12-hour clock ("half six", "quarter to ten") (J; the Wikipedia page on UK time notation could not be reached).
- Dates were written day, month, year: "14th August 1990", "14/8/90" (J, low risk; no page reached).
- Proposed rule (J): 12-hour in talk, handwriting and shop signs; 24-hour on police records, timetables and the fare book's times.

### Shop signs and price tickets
- A 1990 street mixes hand-painted fascias, plastic light boxes and new cut vinyl; Letraset was giving way to desktop publishing (P).
- Prices in pence under a pound ("45p", "99p"), pounds and pence above ("£1.20") (J). Hand-written price cards and stuck-on gun labels (J; no 1990 photograph was reached).
- Ruled trades on Quay Street give the shop tickets: fishmonger, launderette, grocer, newsagent and tobacconist, ironmonger, tea room, chandler (RULINGS, 3 Oct).

### Regional speech and how to use dialect lightly
- Eye dialect (misspelling to show accent) tends to "denigrate the speaker", reinforces stereotypes, and is "more distracting than helpful"; critics suggest word choice, grammar, rhythm and idiom instead, with only a few spellings as a cue (Wikipedia, Eye dialect, citing Preston, Nuessel, Dufresne, Walpole) (S).
- Features of Hull, Grimsby or other English port-town speech of 1990: not reached this session (the Yorkshire dialect page and the theses found were not opened). Nothing is claimed here. A source pass is owed before any word list is written.
- The accent itself lives in the voices and is judged by ear (RULINGS, 30 Sep). Text should not fight it with phonetic spelling (J).

Nothing in this section touches drink or betting.

## 3. Where each cue could go in LEDGER

| Place | Cues | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| The Ledger (player's notebook) | An account book: £ and p columns, date column, narrative column. Pocket-notebook rules: entries struck through once, never torn out, ends ruled off. | Talk box and suggested lines | 1990 vocabulary and money ("a fiver", "two quid", "20p for the phone"); no anachronisms (mobile, email, £2 coin, 17.5% before spring 1991); short, texture-first lines; dialect by word choice only. | A style sheet plus a word list the content gate can read. |
| Menus | The fare book's index; or a teletext-style page for settings (page numbers, coloured headings). | Teletext needs a font decision (section 4). |
| Saving screen | A line written into the fare book, or the radio's "base to all cars"; never a spinning icon. | Alan Wake 2's in-world save is the model. |
| Maps | A street-atlas feel: condensed capital street names, coloured road order, grid index; never the A to Z brand (P). | The current rule is no map beyond what Tom could know (study 10). |

## 4. Fonts and assets against the allowlist

The allowlist admits fonts under the SIL Open Font Licence 1.1 only (item 7, ruled 30 September). Nothing else may be recommended.

On the allowlist (OFL 1.1):
- Courier Prime: typed letters and forms; already in the designs (P, FONTS.md).
- Cutive Mono, Lekton: other typewriter looks (P, verified 1 Oct).
- Tinos, Libre Franklin, Old Standard TT, Work Sans, Kalam, PT Sans, Marcellus SC: newspaper body, tabloid headlines, datelines, signs, handwriting (P).
- VT323 (Peter Hull and Jacques Le Bailly): OFL 1.1 by its GitHub README (S). It copies the DEC VT320 terminal, so it reads as an office computer screen, not Teletext.

Not on the allowlist:
- Bedstead (Ben Harris), the closest Teletext face, built on the SAA5050 teletext chip. Its maker released it into the public domain, and believes the original chip font is public domain in the UK, adding he is not a lawyer (bjh21.me.uk) (S). Public domain is not OFL, so it is off the list. Using it needs Jafar's ruling first.
- Other "Mode 7" or Teletext fonts found: licence not confirmed. Off.
- Transport, Rail Alphabet, Gill Sans, Times as sold: commercial faces. Off.

A third way: the builder draws our own Teletext-style pixel font from the published grid (40 by 24, 2 by 3 mosaic blocks, eight colours). It would be our own work. Copying the SAA5050's letter shapes pixel for pixel carries the same question as Bedstead (J).

Music and radio stay under item 5 (self-hosted MusicGen or Stable Audio Open), with no real lyrics (Never ship 5).

## 6. Decisions, with recommendations

1. Adopt the 1990 style sheet for every written line? A) yes (recommended); B) no.
2. Teletext look? A) leave it for now (recommended); B) the builder draws our own font; C) ask Jafar to add public-domain fonts such as Bedstead to the allowlist.
3. The evening paper's name (canon, owed by the brand bible): the builder offers three names on his page, one recommended. Needed for proposal 4.
4. Dialect in text? A) word choice and grammar only, never phonetic spelling (recommended); B) a few spellings as a cue.
5. Banknotes on screen? A) generic notes in the right sizes and colours, never a copy of a real design (recommended); B) research the Bank of England's reproduction rules first.
6. Clock rule? A) 12-hour in talk, handwriting and shops; 24-hour on police records and the fare book (recommended, research owed); B) 12-hour everywhere.
