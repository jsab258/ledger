# The parade shopfront, 1900 to 1935, as it stands in 1990

Research for the shopfront kit (production/art/shopfront-kit/README.md), first piece Rita's
pawn shop, the east parade's bay 2 (x 15.0 to 21.0). Written 6 October 2026. Every source below
was read on 6 October 2026, fetched in full (page or PDF), not from a search snippet. Quotes are
short; the rest is paraphrase. Source keys are listed at the end with their addresses.

## In brief

- A parade shopfront is a timber frame between two pilasters: plinth, shaft, capital; a console
  on each capital ends the fascia; a cornice caps the fascia. Under the window a stallriser with
  a projecting sill; a transom with a row of shallow toplights above; a glazed shop door, often
  in a recessed lobby with a tiled or terrazzo floor; a separate solid panelled door to the flat.
- The spec's sizes mostly agree with the guides: stallriser 0.60 (guides 0.40 to 0.70), fascia
  0.55 (guides: at most 0.60, at most a fifth of the front's height), mullions 48 mm in front of
  the glass (guide: 40 to 70). Four points differ; they are listed under "Where the research
  differs from the spec". The kit models the spec's values.
- No source gives a pilaster's width or projection, and no measured drawing of a surviving
  1900 to 1935 front was found. Those sizes stay the spec's.
- By 1990 such a front has been repainted many times and is failing where water sits: sills,
  the pilasters' feet, the stallriser's bottom (pavement splash), the door's bottom rail.

## 1. Anatomy

**Pilasters.** They stand on a "foot" or plinth and carry a capital, on which the console sits;
usually timber, panelled or fluted, sometimes plain [CV]. They come plain, half-round, panelled,
beaded or fluted, and the base may vary in height to take a sloping pavement; consoles are often
scrolled or carved [CW]. Guides want a plinth on new pilasters; pilasters may be painted render,
brick or painted timber [BH]. The stallriser should not rise above the pilaster's base, about
450 mm [KC], so the plinth is at least the stallriser's height. Period examples: fluted pilasters
with consoles carrying a box fascia, Skipton, c.1908 [HE1]; granite pilasters on granite plinths
with stone consoles, Muswell Hill, a 1930s front in an 1890s parade [HE2]; capitals with upright
foliage on 1930s parades [HE3]. No source gives a width or a projection. London's building rules
of about 1902 [EL] limited a shopfront's projection from the wall in streets up to 30 ft wide (the
figure is garbled in the scan, probably 5 in) and allowed 10 in in wider streets; a cornice 13 in
and 18 in respectively.

**Stallriser and sill.** Traditional heights 450 to 700 mm under a moulded projecting cill; some
late-Victorian and Edwardian fronts lower [BH]; 400 to 700 mm [CV]. Ellis (1902) gives 2 ft 6 in
to 3 ft for butchers, fishmongers, grocers and confectioners, and only inches for tailors and
bootmakers [EL]. Materials: render, panelled timber or facing brick, and glazed brick-shaped
tiles "usually in green but sometimes in brown or red" [BH]; glazed brick and tile, which chip at
the corners [CV]; mostly render or panelling on a rendered or stone plinth [KC]; panelling
usually raised and fielded [BC1]. Examples: rich brown glazed tile on a moulded plinth, 1908
[HE1]; low black Vitrolite, 1930s [HE2]; polished black marble [DV]. The frame "included both a
bottom rail and a deep sill" [BC1]; the window needs a substantial cill overhanging the
stallriser [BH]; Ellis calls the stallboard the stout sill the sash sits on, the glass fixed from
inside [EL]; hardwood for the cill [CW]. No source gives a drip or the sill's section.

**Window, mullions, transom, toplights.** Mullions were rounded or oval, "projecting about
40-70mm from the glass"; tall windows sometimes had opening transom lights with leaded glass
[CW]. The transom should divide the window on the line between the door and its fanlight [KC];
transoms often ran near the top, above the door, giving a row of shallow transom lights [CV].
Edwardian fronts had very slim mullions beside the lobby, and often a ventilation grille under the
fascia [DV]. By the late 1920s, traditional fronts had curved glass lobbies, leaded clerestories
and marble or tiled lobby floors; the 1930s used etched sunrise patterns, bronze, chrome and
Vitrolite [BC2] [HE2]. One modest example: "very slender bronze sills, mullions and transoms"
[DV]. No source gives a glazing bar's width.

**Shop door and lobby.** The door is glazed, its bottom panel (the kick plate) the stallriser's
height, with a fanlight over it; the lobby often has a decorated tiled floor [CV]. Painted
softwood, two-thirds glazed [BH]. Recessed lobbies "often incorporate valuable terrazzo or tiled
floors" [CW]. Examples: a marble threshold then mosaic lettered with the name [HE1]; a white
mosaic floor with the shop's name [HE2]. Ellis: good entrance doors 7 ft to 8 ft 6 in high by
3 ft to 4 ft 6 in wide; lock rail 2 ft 8 in to its centre; bottom rails 9 in; shop doors usually
mahogany on floor springs [EL]. Today's access minimum, 800 to 900 mm, is modern [CV] [RI].

**Side door to the flat.** "Solid unglazed panelled doors are appropriate for separate access
to upper floors", raised and fielded or with bolection mouldings [BH]. It should match the
parade's other doors and may share the lobby [RI]. Upper floors were often let separately by
1914 [HE4], and inter-war parades planned their own access [HE3]. No source gives four panels or a
size; the standard British imperial leaf is 1981 x 838 mm (6 ft 6 in x 2 ft 9 in), with 1981 x
762 and 2032 x 813 [FA].

**Fascia.** No deeper than cornice and architrave together, and at most a fifth of the front's
height [RI]; traditionally not more than 600 mm, sloped slightly forward, between the consoles,
the cornice lead-flashed or it rots [CV]; not below the pilaster heads, its height set by the
corbels [KC]. Old fascias often survive under later boarding [BH] [BC1].

**Letter plate.** Today's standard, BS EN 13724:2013, wants an aperture 230 to 280 mm wide and
30 to 35 mm high, between 0.7 and 1.7 m up [WP] [DG]; the height range is confirmed by Approved
Document Q's guidance, "700mm up to 1700mm from the finished floor level" [GOV]. It postdates 1990.

## 2. Materials and colour

"Sombre colours were often used on traditional shopfronts": maroon, dark green, black, dark blue,
brown; inter-war Vitrolite and chrome [WM]. Joinery dark blue, dark green, red, brown, black or
white, gloss or semi-gloss, on painted softwood; rendered pilasters and stallrisers in a pale
contrast [BH]; rich dark colours [CV]. Vitrolite in sixteen colours; faience, terrazzo, marble,
chrome, bronze [BC2]. Brass handles and catches [HE1]; brass- and bronze-clad timber [BC1].
Oak facing south "soon bleaches in the sun to a pale grey" [EL].

## 3. Wear by 1990

No source describes 1990 itself; these describe how such fronts decay.

- Paintwork needs touching up yearly, "especially around the cill and the joints"; stallrisers
  are damaged and left dirty and stained by pavement splash; blocked gutters and cracked
  flashing spill water down the front [CW].
- Timber stallrisers decay from splash; pilaster feet rot; tacked-on mouldings "quickly look
  tatty" [CV].
- Door bottoms decay where they meet damp stone or splash, worse where paint is neglected;
  built-up paint makes doors bind; shrinkage splits panels; gloss needs renewing about every four
  years [HES].
- Fascias rot from their unpainted backs and edges; rails' top edges were bevelled to shed
  rain; bars were scribed, not mitred, as gaps hold water [EL].
- Vitrolite cracks low down and round doors, patched with painted ply or Perspex [BC2]; later
  decades added secondary fascias and crude planted mouldings [BC1]; superstores hit parade shops
  in the 1980s and 1990s [HE3].

For the kit: chipped and failed paint on arrises, sills, the transom's top and the pilaster's
foot; grime in the panels' angles and the lobby; darker, dirtier paint low down on the
stallriser and door (splash); sun-faded colour on south-facing fronts; brass worn bright where
hands touch it. The first two come from the baked masks; the splash band and the fade are the
master material's (a height gradient and a per-shop tint), not the meshes'.

## 4. Where the research differs from the spec

The kit models the spec's value in every case; the session decides.

1. **Shop door glazed from 1.0 m.** The guides glaze it from about the stallriser's height: "two-
   thirds glazed" [BH]; its bottom panel the stallriser's height [CV]. That is about
   0.6 to 0.7 m, not 1.0.
2. **Shop door 2.04 m high.** 2040 mm is a metric leaf (Scotland's usual height [FA]); Ellis gives
   period entrance doors 7 ft (2.13 m) or more [EL]. A 7 ft leaf would put the door head at about
   2.16 and leave a 0.24 m fanlight under the 2.40 transom.
3. **Side door's letter plate, 250 x 40 mm with its centre at 1.0 m.** Ellis's lock rail centre is
   2 ft 8 in (0.81 m) [EL]; today's standard aperture is 30 to 35 mm high [WP]. A period plate
   would sit lower, on a lock rail near 0.8 m. Neither number is wrong for 1990 (the standard is
   later); the spec's is kept.
4. **The bay's widths.** The spec counts the doors as leaves (0.838 and 0.90) and gives the
   glazing 3.562 m. Doors stand in frames: with 50 mm jambs and 3 mm gaps the side door is 0.944
   wide and the shop door 1.006, which leaves the window 3.35 m. (Not a research source: a
   consequence of building the frames.)

Not a disagreement but worth the session's eye: the street's emitter today places the shop glass
0.12 to 0.16 m in FRONT of the wall face, ahead of the 0.10 m pilasters
(StreetVignette.cs, Shopfront(), `Z = frontZ - sgn * (gr + 0.02)`), where every guide recesses it.
The kit puts the glass plane 0.03 m in front of the wall face, 0.12 behind the stallriser's sill
nose, behind the pilasters and fascia. The street's fascia box runs the bay's full width, across
the pilaster heads, 0.12 proud; the consoles' feet are only 0.06 deep, so the box buries the
lower half of each console, where the guides put the fascia between the consoles [CV] [KC]. And
the D5 downpipes at the party walls (x = 9, 15, 21, 27, 33; 0.094 m from the wall, 68 mm across)
run through the paired pilasters' plinths and capitals, which butt at the party line and project
0.15 and 0.13.

## 5. The professional method for such a kit

- **A kit on a grid.** Pieces share a footprint, sub-kits divide into each other, pivots sit at
  the ground plane, and moving pivots later means fixing every placed copy [GD]. Pivots at a
  corner or the bottom centre for snapping; power-of-two snaps; matching UVs and reused
  materials; material instances for variation [UE1]. Duplicate standard pieces rather than
  resize them [UE2]. Stay in the footprint; test loops, stacking and gaps [LDB]. City Sample's
  buildings are hundreds of instances of modular Nanite pieces [UE3]. A Western street's facade
  kit on a 1.5 m / 3 m metric, two trim sheets, a master paint shader with procedural edge damage
  [80A]. For this kit: every piece on one datum (the wall face, the pavement, the middle of the
  piece's width), sized to the parade's 6 m bay, with length arguments for other bays.
- **Mid-poly.** One mesh per asset with planned bevels and face-weighted normals, no separate high
  and low poly [80B]; bevels plus weighted normals give clean shading [80C] [GA]. Blender's Bevel
  modifier's Harden Normals points bevel faces' normals with the faces around them [BLB]; the
  Weighted Normal modifier sets custom normals by face area [BLW]. Unreal must import the
  normals, not compute them, or the weighting is lost [UE4].
- **One material per surface, colour per instance.** Material instances change a parent's
  parameters without recompiling [UE5]; Custom Primitive Data sets a colour per placed mesh and lets
  similar meshes batch (Epic's example, 94 to 46 draw calls) [UE6], or per instance in an
  instanced group [UEF]. For this kit: one slot each for paint, metal and tile or stone; each
  shop's paint colour set per placed piece. No source states "one slot per paint" in those words.
- **Wear from baked masks.** A material can blend layers by vertex colour, a channel per Lerp
  [UE7]. Cycles bakes straight to a colour attribute [BLK]; Pointiness is "an approximation of the
  curvature of the mesh per vertex", which the manual suggests for dirt and wear [BLG]. Baked
  curvature, AO and edge masks drive edge wear and dirt through material layers [80D]; vertex
  colours can replace textures on Nanite's dense meshes [KK], but need enough vertices to look
  good [80E]: hence the kit's support loops and cuts every 0.3 m.
- **Nanite.** Enable it where supported; it takes opaque materials, several UVs and vertex
  colours; no numeric triangle budget is given [UE8]. Fewer vertices than triangles is healthy; vertices
  to triangles at 2:1 or more signals a fault [UE9].
