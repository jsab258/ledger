# How solo and two-person teams shipped ambitious games

Research topic 18. Delivered to the studio. Nothing here is an instruction.

The question as briefed: not "can a tiny team ship", which is settled, but HOW
the ones that did it actually did it, and what that says about a project whose
premise is one person directing an AI studio.

Scope boundary held: this topic is about games that SHIPPED. The ones that
shipped badly or never shipped are topic 21 and are left there, except where a
shipped game's own near-failure is part of its record.

Labels follow `production/art/atlas-02/research/`: CITED (a source says it),
DERIVED (arithmetic or inference, shown), ASSUMED (a judgement with no source),
HOLE (could not be established).

## Part 1. Seven cases, with what each one actually did

### 1.1 Manor Lords (Greg Styczen, Hooded Horse, 2024)

The closest case to LEDGER on engine and ambition: one person, Unreal, a
historical setting, a visual bar well above what a solo project is expected to
reach.

CITED: roughly seven years of development, mostly single-handed, on Unreal
Engine 4, after earlier work in RPG Maker 2000, Flash and Dark Basic. He
received an Epic MegaGrant. Shortly before the April 2024 release he brought in
additional programmers because platform-version problems threatened the launch.
[Wikipedia, Manor Lords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manor_Lords),
[mein-mmo, Manor Lords has only one developer, but without secret helpers it almost failed](https://mein-mmo.de/en/manor-lords-has-only-one-developer-but-without-secret-helpers-it-almost-failed-just-before-the-end,1122658/)

CITED, and this is the transferable half: he used Quixel Megascans plus
hand-made photogrammetry, which "sped up development greatly while increasing
visual fidelity", and motion capture recorded with two friends in a children's
photo studio. He does all the programming and most of the art, prefers making
his own assets, and "edits, mixes and mashes assets together to make something
new".
[Unreal Engine developer interview (via search summary; page egress-blocked)](https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/developer-interviews/solo-dev-makes-sophisticated-sim-manor-lords-using-unreal-engine)

CITED, his advice to solo developers: "Try not to fight the engine too much",
and "Accept that some things might not be perfect and focus on finding the fun
in your game instead."
[same interview, via search summary]

CITED, the label is contested by his peers: a competing city-builder developer
argued "Don't say Manor Lords was made by a solo developer", on the grounds
that "the team size on it is maybe even larger than ours". Styczen's own answer
is that he has earned the title because "if I quit, it's game over".
[PC Gamer, Don't say Manor Lords was made by a solo developer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/city-builder/manor-lords-solo-developer-perception/),
[GamesRadar, Manor Lords creator kind of agrees that he isn't really a solo dev](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/city-builder/manor-lords-creator-kind-of-agrees-that-he-isnt-really-a-solo-dev-but-says-hes-earned-the-title-because-if-i-quit-its-game-over/)

Interpretation: that argument is worth more to this project than the game is.
The canonical solo success is disputed AS a solo success by people who do the
same work, and the dispute is about where you draw the boundary around the
tools and the bought content. LEDGER will meet the identical argument, with AI
in the slot Megascans occupies here.

### 1.2 Return of the Obra Dinn (Lucas Pope, 2018)

The closest case on GENRE: a first-person deduction game where the player
reconstructs who did what to whom, made by one person.

CITED: four and a half years, solo.
[Wikipedia, Return of the Obra Dinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_of_the_Obra_Dinn),
[Game Developer, Road to the IGF: Lucas Pope's Return of the Obra Dinn](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/road-to-the-igf-lucas-pope-s-i-return-of-the-obra-dinn-i-)

CITED, and it corrected the hypothesis I went in with: the 1-bit art style was
an AESTHETIC starting point, taken from Macintosh games he played as a child
and made legible with modern technology, not a scope decision. What the solo
constraint actually cut was GAMEPLAY: he had ideas that were "too complicated or
difficult for a one-person dev" and simplified around them.
[PlayStation Blog, How Lucas Pope created the unique 1-bit art style](https://blog.playstation.com/archive/2019/10/17/lucas-pope-on-return-of-the-obra-dinns-art-style),
[Game Developer, For Lucas Pope, Return of the Obra Dinn was a bunch of appealing design problems](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/for-lucas-pope-i-return-of-the-obra-dinn-i-was-a-bunch-of-appealing-design-problems)

I went looking for "he picked a cheap art style to save time" and found the
opposite, which is worth recording as a correction rather than quietly dropping:
the visual identity was the thing he kept, and the MECHANICS were what a
one-person budget forced him to cut. That inverts the usual advice and it points
straight at LEDGER's moat, which is mechanics.

### 1.3 Stardew Valley (Eric Barone, 2016)

CITED: four and a half years as sole designer, programmer, animator, artist,
composer and writer. Mostly 10 to 12 hour days, seven days a week; 70-hour
weeks for years. He nearly cancelled it, did not back up his hard drive, and
recovered the project off a failed development PC. He sometimes hated the game
and finished it because stopping would have made him a failure in his own eyes.
[Wikipedia, Eric Barone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Barone),
[GameRant, Stardew Valley Creator Almost Canceled the Game](https://gamerant.com/stardew-valley-eric-barone-development-cancel/),
[NPR, The legacy and future of the farming game Stardew Valley](https://www.npr.org/2025/01/24/g-s1-44510/the-legacy-and-future-of-the-farming-game-stardew-valley)

DERIVED, and it is the number that makes every other case legible: 4.5 years at
70 hours a week is roughly 16,000 hours of one person, for a 2D game with no
photoreal target, no voice, no live conversation and no simulation of the kind
LEDGER's moat requires.

### 1.4 Kenshi (Chris Hunt, Lo-Fi Games, 2018)

CITED: twelve years of development, the first six as a one-man team, funded by
working as a night security guard. 2.3 million copies sold as of April 2024.
[Wikipedia, Kenshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenshi_(video_game)),
[Game World Observer, Kenshi hits 2.3 million copies sold](https://gameworldobserver.com/2024/04/10/kenshi-sales-2-3-million-copies-lo-fi-games),
[PC Games Insider, Surviving Steam Greenlight and a decade of development with Chris Hunt](https://www.pcgamesinsider.biz/indie-interview/68200/surviving-steam-greenlight-and-decade-of-development-with-lo-fi-games-chris-hunt/)

Interpretation: Kenshi is the closest case to LEDGER's simulation ambition in a
3D world, and its visuals were widely called dated at release. It bought its
depth with a decade and spent nothing on the visual axis.

### 1.5 Dwarf Fortress (Tarn and Zach Adams, Bay 12, 2002 to 2022)

CITED: two brothers, twenty years on one idea, a simulation deep enough to be
the acknowledged ancestor of Minecraft, RimWorld and Prison Architect, released
on Steam in December 2022 and making its authors millionaires after two decades
of donations.
[PC Gamer, After spending 20 years simulating reality](https://www.pcgamer.com/after-spending-20-years-simulating-reality-the-dwarf-fortress-devs-have-to-get-used-to-a-new-one-being-millionaires/),
[Rogueliker, Dwarf Fortress is an astonishing 20 years old](https://rogueliker.com/dwarf-fortress-20/)

DERIVED, and this is the load-bearing observation of the whole topic: Dwarf
Fortress is the only game in this set whose simulation depth is in LEDGER's
class, and it rendered in ASCII for twenty of those years. It did not trade the
visuals away reluctantly. It never bought them at all.

### 1.6 Shadows of Doubt (ColePowered Games, 2023)

The closest case on BOTH genre and simulation: a procedural detective game in a
simulated city where every resident has a home, a job and a routine.

CITED: lead developer Cole Jeffries, work begun in 2018, roughly eight years,
with Miles as voxel artist and Stark Holborn as writer. Not a solo project, a
very small team.
[Wikipedia, Shadows of Doubt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadows_of_Doubt),
[ColePowered, DevBlog 45](https://colepowered.com/shadows-of-doubt-devblog-45/)

CITED, DevBlog 21, dated 27 March 2020 and titled "How Voxels Saved the
Project": "realism was off the table due to workload"; the voxel decision "was
the biggest factor in making this ambitious project doable with a small team and
small budget, because the turnaround of most art assets is minuscule in
comparison to anything else"; a low-fidelity low-poly direction "would have been
more effective at conveying the desired atmosphere" but would have increased art
asset turnaround.
[Steam News, DevBlog 21: How Voxels Saved the Project](https://store.steampowered.com/news/app/986130/view/2072159695699766540),
[ColePowered, DevBlog 21 (page egress-blocked)](https://colepowered.com/shadows-of-doubt-devblog-21-how-voxels-saved-the-project/)

That is the single most directly relevant paragraph found in this topic. The
nearest thing to LEDGER that exists ruled out realism EXPLICITLY, as a workload
decision, named ART ASSET TURNAROUND as the binding constraint on an ambitious
small-team simulation, and accepted a look it considered worse in order to keep
the simulation.

### 1.7 Animal Well (Billy Basso, Shared Memory / Bigmode, 2024)

CITED: seven years, solo. Begun in 2017 and developed "during nights and
weekends while he held a day job at a medical startup"; he moved to full time
in early 2021. He built a custom engine. In 2021 he brought in Dan Adelman for
business management and marketing, and later partnered with Bigmode as
publisher rather than self-publishing.
[Wikipedia, Animal Well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_Well),
[Software Engineering Daily, Animal Well with Billy Basso](https://softwareengineeringdaily.com/2024/05/15/animal-well-with-billy-basso/),
[Game Developer, Why Animal Well's home-brewed engine was key to its success](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/why-animal-well-s-home-brewed-engine-was-key-to-its-success)

DERIVED, and it is the most uncomfortable data point in the topic: this is the
ONE case in the set whose working pattern matches the vision statement's
"evenings and weekends", and it ran that way for about three and a half years
and then stopped running that way in order to finish. Nights and weekends got
the project to a state worth going full time for; it did not get it shipped.

Its custom engine does NOT transfer. Animal Well is a 2D game with a
hand-authored pixel look and no third-party content pipeline to lose by leaving
one. LEDGER is photoreal 3D with a fetch-and-import pipeline, and D16 closed the
engine question on its own evidence.

## Part 2. What the seven have in common

### 2.1 The timescales, and what they are timescales OF

| game | people | years | what it gave up |
|---|---|---|---|
| Return of the Obra Dinn | 1 | 4.5 | mechanics too complex for one person |
| Stardew Valley | 1 | 4.5 | nothing, and it cost 70-hour weeks |
| Manor Lords | 1 plus help at the end | ~7 | authored art, replaced by scans |
| Animal Well | 1 | 7 | the nights-and-weekends pattern itself |
| Shadows of Doubt | ~3 | ~8 | realism, explicitly and on the record |
| Kenshi | 1, then a few | 12 | the visual axis entirely |
| Dwarf Fortress | 2 | 20 | the visual axis entirely, from the start |

DERIVED: median about seven years. Every one of them is a full-time or
better-than-full-time effort for most of its span. None of the seven is a
part-time project that finished part-time.

### 2.2 Nobody in this set bought both

The set splits cleanly and there is no case in the middle.

The three with simulation depth in LEDGER's class, Dwarf Fortress, Kenshi and
Shadows of Doubt, all gave the visual axis away, and one of them wrote down the
reason in a devblog. The two with a visual bar in LEDGER's class, Manor Lords
and Obra Dinn, have no persistent per-person memory, no gossip, and nothing that
would pass condition 2 of the Meridian Test.

DERIVED: the vision statement's claim that LEDGER does "the one thing neither
[GTA 6 nor KCD2] can" is, at small-team scale, a claim that it will be the first
project in this set to refuse the trade. That is not an argument against it. It
is the reason the method matters more than any single feature, and it is the
thing the throughput ledger exists to settle.

### 2.3 What they all delegated, and it is the same thing

- Manor Lords: Megascans and photogrammetry for the world's surfaces; two
  friends for motion capture; programmers at the end for platform work.
- Shadows of Doubt: a dedicated voxel artist and a dedicated writer, on a
  three-person team.
- Animal Well: business management to Dan Adelman, publishing to Bigmode.
- Stardew Valley: nothing, and section 1.3 records what that cost.

DERIVED: every one of them kept the identity work and handed away the VOLUME
work, and the only one that refused is the one whose record is 70-hour weeks and
a near-cancellation. LEDGER's structure is that same split with AI in the volume
slot, which is the most encouraging finding in this topic and also the least
proven.

### 2.4 The scope literature, and the quality of its evidence

CITED, with a warning about the sources: the widely repeated advice is that
scope creep is the dominant cause of small-team failure, that it arrives as
small harmless additions rather than a decision, and that the remedy is to "set
a deadline and cut your project to meet the deadline instead of pushing the
deadline back to meet the project".
[Wayline, Scope Creep: The Silent Killer of Solo Indie Game Development](https://www.wayline.io/blog/scope-creep-solo-indie-game-development),
[Wayline, Scope Creep in Indie Games: How to Avoid Development Hell](https://www.wayline.io/blog/scope-creep-indie-games-avoiding-development-hell),
[Game Developer, The Last Humble Bee postmortem: Staying sane in solo development](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-last-humble-bee-postmortem-staying-sane-in-solo-development)

HOLE, and it should be stated rather than dressed up: these are advice blogs.
One cites "a post-mortem analysis of hundreds of failed indie games" with no
method, no sample definition and no link. The individual anecdotes in them (a
nine-month project taking 27 months) are unattributed. I could not find a
dataset behind any of it. The advice is probably right and the evidence offered
for it is weak, and those are two different statements.

HOLE: no source found in two searches names an AMBITIOUS game that shipped
entirely on part-time hours. The forum material confirms that small games ship
part-time; Animal Well is the nearest ambitious case and it went full time to
finish. Absence of evidence here, but the absence is consistent across the
whole set.

## Part 3. What this project has, measured against that

### 3.1 The premise, in its own words

CITED, `ledger-v2/respec/vision-pillars-v2.md` as copied into `CLAUDE.md`:
"Built almost entirely by Claude Code operating as an autonomous studio; Jafar
directs (non-technical decisions, feel checks, one-click generation runs,
evenings and weekends, small budget, no deadline)", and underneath the game sit
"two quieter goals: prove the method (that one person directing AI agents can
produce this class of game at all) and learn game development by doing it."

DERIVED: the method is not a means to the game here, it is a stated goal
alongside it. That reframes every comparison above. The seven cases are
comparisons for the GAME. For the METHOD there is no case, because none of them
had one.

### 3.2 The rate, which this project already measures

CITED, `production/throughput.md`: the planning unit is verified pieces per
week, a piece counts only when it passes station 3 and lands at station 4, and
partial work counts zero. The ledger reads, verbatim from its table: 2026-W36
dialogue bank 1; 2026-W36 signage/brand 0; 2026-W36/W37 prop/asset 22;
2026-W37 prop/asset 0.

CITED, from the same file: cost per verified piece is "UNDEFINED, not zero: the
denominator is zero verified pieces, and a cost divided by no pieces is not a
number." The measurable unit is SESSIONS per verified piece, because "nothing
inside the container can read Jafar's usage page".

CITED, the two figures it does carry. SETUP: 22 engine-specialist and 7
instrument-builder spawns on 8 and 9 September plus 11 director spawns, stated
as an upper bound because the log cannot attribute a spawn to a line. MARGINAL,
after the pipeline exists: "ZERO SESSIONS AND ABOUT 0.35 MINUTES OF RUNNER
TIME", with its own caveat, "it prices the IMPORT of a piece and not the MAKING
of one". Round trip push to landed evidence: 2 min 07 s for a mesh run, 5 min
42 s for a cold build with a cook, a packaged launch, two crimes, a gossip
round, 30 frames and a clip.

DERIVED, and it is the single most important sentence this topic can offer: the
whole question of whether the method works reduces to whether the MAKING cost,
the one the throughput ledger explicitly has not measured, behaves like the
IMPORT cost. If authoring a piece worth importing is near-zero marginal sessions,
LEDGER breaks the seven-year median. If it is not, LEDGER is a small team with a
faster build server, and the seven-year median applies.

### 3.3 The deadline, which was retired on purpose

CITED, `D1-engine-probe.md`, amended 2026-09-02: the two-week timebox is
"RETIRED by Jafar ('forget the deadline, it's not relevant')". The probe is
bounded "by production/budget.md and by the attempt budget on queue item 027
(constitution law 10), never by a date."

CITED, `ledger-v2/respec/roadmap-v2.md:33`: "every phase with a taste gate also
gets a time or attempt budget at kickoff, set while calm."

CITED, `production/budget.md`: "LEDGER stops when the week's LEDGER share is
spent. Not slows down: stops."

DERIVED, and stated as an observation rather than a criticism, because the
vision says "no deadline" deliberately: the project has three bounds and they
bound three different things. The budget bounds the RATE. The attempt budget
bounds how many tries ONE item gets. The phase gates bound when a phase is
DONE. None of them bounds how many items are in scope.

### 3.4 The one count that has a before and an after

CITED, `production/systems-inventory.json`, read on 2026-09-14: 91 tiles, 26
exists, 40 partial, 25 absent. Of those, 69 carry `typedOn` 2026-09-09 and 22
carry 2026-09-10.

DERIVED, with the split: the 69 typed on 9 September were 26 exists, 36 partial,
7 absent. The 22 added the next day by Jafar's ruling were 18 absent and 4
partial, and 11 of the 22 are player-facing. So in one day the census grew 32%
and the count of known-unbuilt systems went from 7 to 25, a factor of 3.6.

Interpretation, and the generous reading is the correct one: that is not scope
creep. It is the CURE for scope creep. Those systems existed as requirements
before they were written down; counting them is what makes them arguable. The
literature's failure mode is the requirement nobody counted, not the one somebody
listed.

What follows from it is narrower and still worth saying: an inventory that grew
by a third in a day, built by the project's own admission from "listing what we
could think of", has no evidence yet that it has stopped growing, and the
coverage audit was commissioned on exactly that reasoning. The historical record
says a growing absent column is how small teams die. The defence the record
would recommend is not a smaller census, it is the standing rule already in
force here: a gap that exists in another game is not a gap in LEDGER until it is
ruled in.

## Part 5. Findings and interpretation

### Findings

F1. Median time to ship across the seven cases is about seven years, and every
one was full time or harder for most of its span.

F2. The set splits cleanly: three deep-simulation games gave the visual axis
away entirely, two visually ambitious games have no simulation depth, and
nothing sits in the middle.

F3. Shadows of Doubt, the nearest analogue on genre and simulation, ruled out
realism explicitly as a workload decision on 27 March 2020 and named art asset
turnaround as the binding constraint.

F4. Obra Dinn, the nearest analogue on genre, cut MECHANICS rather than visuals
under the solo constraint.

F5. Manor Lords reached its visual bar on Megascans, hand photogrammetry and
mocap with friends, and its solo status is disputed by peers on exactly the
boundary question LEDGER will face.

F6. Animal Well is the only case matching "evenings and weekends" and it ran
that way for about three and a half years before going full time to finish.

F7. Every case delegated volume work and kept identity work, except Stardew
Valley, whose record is 70-hour weeks and a near-cancellation.

F8. `production/throughput.md` measures verified pieces per week and reads 1, 0,
22, 0 across two weeks, with cost per verified piece explicitly UNDEFINED.

F9. The marginal cost of a piece after the pipeline exists is recorded as zero
sessions and about 0.35 minutes of runner time, and the file states that this
prices the import and not the making.

F10. The deadline was retired by Jafar on 2026-09-02 and replaced by a rate
bound (the budget), an attempt bound per item, and per-phase gates. None of the
three bounds the number of items in scope.

F11. The systems inventory grew from 69 to 91 tiles in one day, 18 of the 22
additions being absent work, taking the absent column from 7 to 25.

### Interpretation

I1. The comparison that matters is not LEDGER against these seven games. It is
LEDGER's own throughput ledger against itself over time, because the method is
the variable none of the seven had and the only instrument that can price it is
already built.

I2. The most valuable unmeasured number in the project is the MAKING cost of a
piece, not the import cost. The throughput ledger says so in its own words and
names the twelve-package batch as what would measure it.

I3. The trade that every deep-simulation game in the set made, realism for
depth, is the trade LEDGER has decided not to make. That decision is only
survivable if AI generation genuinely collapses art asset turnaround, which is
the exact constraint Shadows of Doubt named. Everything in the asset-packs and
photoreal-on-a-budget topics is therefore load-bearing on the method, not just
on the look.

I4. "No deadline" is defensible and is Jafar's call, and the record shows why
the literature disagrees. The substitute the record would accept is not a date.
It is a bound on the COUNT of things in scope, which is the one bound this
project does not have, and which the standing rule about gaps in other games is
already half of.

I5. The honest summary of the seven cases for a director: none of them tells us
LEDGER is impossible, and none of them tells us it is possible either, because
all seven priced human hours and LEDGER is not spending those. They tell us
exactly one thing with confidence, which is what happens if the method turns out
not to work.
