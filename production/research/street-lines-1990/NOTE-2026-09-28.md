# Street lines, 1990: which are wrong (research note, 28 September 2026)

The problem as given: 350 spoken lines for one street in the Hook, each to be
right for a working-class street in a British port town in 1990 and to obey
canon. Every line was read against canon.md, the premise and the content rule;
the facts that decide a line were checked against dated sources (listed at the
end). About thirty minutes. Line numbers are the line numbers of
street-lines.txt as handed over.

**Result: 25 lines flagged: 7 wrong, 18 doubtful.** The rest pass. Plain lines
were not flagged for being plain.

## Wrong (7)

1. **171** "How's the pub treating you?"
   - Why: Mickey's is a minicab office (canon, D19), not a pub, and the line
     also puts drink-trade talk in a resident's mouth.
   - Replace: "How's Mickey's treating you?"

2. **214** "Doctor wants money I haven't got."
   - Why: in 1990 seeing a GP, and casualty, cost nothing. What cost money was
     the prescription (£3.05 an item from 1 April 1990), a dental check-up
     (£3.15 from April 1989) and an eye test (about £10 from April 1989). A
     doctor asking for money is the American picture. The real barrier was lost
     pay: Statutory Sick Pay did not cover the first three days off.
   - Replace: "Doctor'd sign me off, and who pays the rent then?" (or, using a
     true 1990 cost: "Three pound a prescription. I've not got three pound.")
   - Sources: Hansard 3 April 1990; Hansard 12 April 1989; Gosling 2017.

3. **223** "I'd have it looked at if looking at was free."
   - Why: looking at was free (same facts as 214).
   - Replace: "I'd have it looked at if I could spare the day."
   - Sources: as 214.

4. **227** "There's a woman on Copper Row does it cheap."
   - Why: treatment for a wound was free in 1990, so a cheap back-street
     healer has no reason to exist. "A woman who does it cheap" also carries
     the pre-1967 back-street echo that a British ear hears at once, which is
     both 1950s framing and near the content rule's line.
   - Replace: "Casualty'll see to it. It costs you a wait, that's all."
   - Sources: as 214; "casualty" was the everyday name for A&E (the BBC's
     *Casualty*, from 6 September 1986).

5. **237** "There's no shame in it costing money."
   - Why: the treatment did not cost money (as 214).
   - Replace: "There's no shame in a week on the sick."
   - Sources: as 214.

6. **245** "The little ones are the ones that get you. Penny here, penny there."
   - Why: "the little ones" is the everyday phrase for young children, so it is
     heard as children costing money. Canon: no children, not even mentioned.
   - Replace: "It's the small rises that get you. Penny here, penny there."
   - Source: Wiktionary, "little one": "A young child."

7. **155** "There they are. The busy one."
   - Why: said to Tom. In 1990 speech one known man is "he"; singular "they"
     here reads as a leftover from a neutral template. The idiom is "There he
     is."
   - Replace: "There he is. The busy one."

## Doubtful (18)

### Premise: only right with the right speaker or subject (5)

These are fine between long-standing neighbours and wrong when aimed at, or
about, Tom, who has known the street for days (canon: a stranger, known only as
Mickey's nephew). They need a condition when wired, not new words.

8. **75** "That's not the man I know."
   - Never when the subject is Tom: nobody knows him. For Tom: "That's not how
     he's struck me."
9. **78** "Mickey's family. That still means something to me."
   - Only when the subject is Tom; it makes no sense about anyone else.
10. **83** "That's a hard thing to say about a man who's been decent to me."
    - Only where the speaker has a history with the subject; about Tom, only
      once Tom has done something for that speaker.
11. **200** "Have it your way. You always do."
    - "You always do" claims years of acquaintance; never to Tom. For Tom:
      "Have it your way, then."
12. **207** "You'll come round. You always come round."
    - As 200. For Tom: "You'll come round. Most do."

### Idiom drifting American or later than 1990 (8)

The Google Books figures below are rates per billion words in the British
and the American book corpora, 1986 to 1992 against 2010 to 2019, queried
28 September 2026. Books are not speech, so they are a pointer, not proof. For
comparison, two plainly British phrases rose only 2 to 9 times over the same
span ("mind how you go" 16 to 36; "I'm not having this" 4 to 32).

13. **188** "We're not doing this."
    - The refusal-to-engage sense is later and American-leaning: British books
      0.9, American 3.7 in 1986 to 1992; British 19 by the 2010s.
    - Replace: "I'm not having this."
14. **208** "Then we're done."
    - "We're done" as "we're finished with each other": American books 57 against
      British 27 in 1986 to 1992; British 644 by the 2010s, a 24-fold rise.
    - Replace: "Then that's that."
15. **123** "No. You don't get to nod and keep walking."
    - "You don't get to" meaning "you've no right to" is the American moral
      construction that spread here later: American 50 against British 29 in
      1986 to 1992, British 362 by the 2010s. (The viewer displayed the query as
      "you do not get to".)
    - Replace: "No. You'll not just nod and walk on."
16. **203** "One of us has to be the bigger, and it won't be you."
    - The idiom is broken ("the bigger" what?), and its modern form, "be the
      bigger person", barely existed in 1990: British 0.4 in 1986 to 1992
      against 21 in the 2010s.
    - Replace: "One of us has to be sensible, and it won't be you."
17. **36** "There's a version where {what}. I've heard worse ones."
    - "There's a version where" is modern storytelling talk (judgement; the
      phrase is too rare to measure).
    - Replace: "One way I heard it, {what}. I've heard worse."
18. **62** "Whatever you think you know, unknow it."
    - "Unknow" is an archaic word, flat in both corpora; in a street voice it
      reads as the 2000s "you can't unsee it" joke.
    - Replace: "Whatever you think you know, forget it."
19. **277** "It's gone quiet in a way that doesn't feel temporary."
    - Written, twenty-first-century phrasing (judgement), not a shopkeeper's
      speech in 1990.
    - Replace: "It's gone quiet, and not the kind that lifts."
20. **289** "It's not you. Don't let it be you."
    - The second half has no clear meaning and sounds like counselling talk
      (judgement).
    - Replace: "It's not you. Don't go blaming yourself."

### Slips and period detail (5)

21. **17** "You want to know why I'm quiet lately? {Cap(what)}."
    - A tense slip: "lately" needs "I've been". Reads as translated.
    - Replace: "You want to know why I've been quiet? {Cap(what)}."
22. **33** "Two people told me {what}. Different two people."
    - "Different two people" is garbled; the point seems to be two sources that
      don't know each other.
    - Replace: "Two people told me {what}. And they don't talk."
23. **169** "Mickey's one. Still standing, then."
    - "Mickey's one" is not an idiom anywhere. In the North a man of 32 is
      still "Mickey's lad".
    - Replace: "Mickey's lad. Still standing, then."
24. **229** "How much do you need?"
    - In the injury set it reads as paying for treatment, which was free (see
      214). It is right once 214, 223 and 237 are about lost pay. Keep it, but
      only alongside those fixes.
25. **242** "Have you seen what they want for coal?"
    - Coal was real in 1990 but no longer the usual heating: about 2.2 million
      of 24 million homes (about one in eleven) had solid fuel as their main
      heating, against about 17 million (71%) on gas. As the street's default
      price grumble it leans 1970s.
    - Keep it for an older resident with a coal fire. For everyone else:
      "Have you seen the gas bill?"
    - Source: Housing Energy Fact File 2013, Tables 6a, 6b, 6d.

## Checked and passed (for the next reviewer)

- **Docks (40, 274, 291) and Ron's world.** The dock labour scheme ended in
  July 1989, and Grimsby and Immingham lost 498 dock jobs that year. "No work
  at the docks" is right for 1990.
- **Prices (239 to 266).** Retail prices rose 9.5% in 1990, peaking at 10.9% in
  September and October. Complaints about rising prices are right, and so is
  "penny here, penny there".
- **The slump (267 to 294).** The recession ran from mid-1990 to mid-1991.
  Takings down, laid-off assistants and shutters down all fit 1990 to 1991.
- **Northern speech ("Aye" 322 and 350, "any road" 338, "I'll not", "I'd not",
  "Mind yourself").** These fit: the casting research models the town's
  voices on Hull and Grimsby.
- **Weekdays without "on" (181, 294, 326, 346), "catch you" (346).** Ordinary
  British speech. "Catch you later" was already in British books in 1986 to
  1992.
- **Other passes.** "God. And here?" (101) is an exclamation, not mockery.
  "That's leverage" (89): the figurative sense dates from 1858 and is not an
  Americanism. "The landlord" (325) reads as the house landlord. "That thing
  you asked about" (335) is neutral.

## Outside the brief, noticed in passing

- Lines 60, 76, 83, 92, 94 and 103 assume the subject or the listener is a man.
  They are fine when that holds, wrong in overheard talk about or to a woman.
  Line 47 ("I heard it from Darren") is wrong if Darren speaks it.
