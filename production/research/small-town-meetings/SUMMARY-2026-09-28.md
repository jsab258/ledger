# Where friends met in a Humber fishing town, around 1990

Research note, 28 September 2026, by a separate research helper given the problem (about thirty minutes): the prototype city's routines kept 55 of 80 friendships apart, and the routines must be rewritten so friends meet the way friends, neighbours and workmates met in such a town.

## What was found and what was not

The biggest gap is how often. No measured figures were found online for how often working-class Britons of the 1980s saw their friends, neighbours or relatives. The numbers exist, in the 1986 British Social Attitudes survey's module on social networks and in the studies below, but every copy reached was paywalled or unreadable. Nothing was filled in from memory. To get real numbers someone has to get hold of one of:

- British Social Attitudes 1986, the "Social Networks and Support Systems" module
- Grundy and Shelton (2001), contact between adult children and their parents, 1986 to 1999
- McGlone, Park and Smith (1998), *Families and Kinship*
- Willmott (1987), *Friendship Networks and Social Support*

Two things could be read: a seven-country comparison of the 1986 data found Britons kept closer ties with relatives than Americans or Australians (Höllinger and Haller 1990); contact with mothers did not fall between 1986 and 2001 (Treas and Gubernskaya 2012). Neither gives usable figures. Nothing was found on how contact frequency relates to how close the tie is.

## Where people met (the places the content rule allows)

- **Fish dock.** In Hull, before the fish dock moved in 1975, the bobbers unloaded trawlers from about 2am; the auction was at 7am, then filleters and barrow lads boxed the fish, and processing staff finished around 5pm. Monday was the busiest landing day. By the early 1990s Hull's market was at Albert Dock and "a shadow" of its old self. In Grimsby in 1990 a photographer spent a year on the dock "from 6ish to lunch-time when the last filleting was done". Jobs went through family and nicknames, and people gathered in smoky cafés with all-day breakfasts and tea urns. Grimsby today still runs buyers from 6am and a 7am auction, Monday to Friday (today's market, but the rhythm matches).
- **Chip shops and cafés.** Hessle Road had named chip shops and tea bars by the dock. Women from the fish factories bought their lunch at a fish shop.
- **Chapels.** An Elim church and the City Temple on Hessle Road.
- **Wash house and launderette.** Hessle Road women traded news at the public wash house (older era). For 1980s launderettes, only film and television portrayals.
- **Shopping street.** A Hessle Road oral history: "you didn't get very far, it always took hours... stop-start... you knew everybody." People rehoused on East Hull estates took two buses back to Hessle Road for the weekly shop and to chat with old neighbours.
- **Doorstep and terrace.** Women gossiped in the terraces; on summer evenings they mended nets in the street.
- **Post office.** Pensions and benefits were paid weekly in cash at sub-post offices, on a fixed payday for each benefit to spread the crowds (Hansard 1980). Thursday as "pension day" comes only from an online forum: unconfirmed.
- **Nothing specific found** for dock canteens and tea breaks in 1990, allotments, the library or the bus stop.
- **A working men's club's "non-drinking side"**: no support. The club movement was founded alcohol-free in 1862, but beer came in soon after. Treat clubs as excluded.

## Real but excluded by the content rule

Pubs (Rayner's on Hessle Road; a Grimsby dock pub open from 6am), social clubs with members' hours after 3pm, the trawlermen's all-night house parties, bingo and betting shops.

## How long meetings lasted

No measured durations. Only that a shopping trip on Hessle Road could turn into "hours" of short stops.

## Hessle Road, Grimsby and the docks

- Distant-water trawlermen spent about three weeks at sea and then about 72 hours ashore (the "three-day millionaires"). Most fishing families lived by the dock on Hessle Road until the 1970s clearances moved many to East Hull estates.
- A Grimsby photographer says the fish docks "died" once new European rules arrived around 1990 (his opinion).
- The National Dock Labour Scheme was abolished on 3 July 1989. Grimsby and Immingham had 498 dock redundancies in 1989, including the whole Lindsey Dock workforce, then 24 in 1990 and 10 in 1991. Casual labour became "a key feature" of dock work again. No figures found for Hull.

## Day of the week

- Shop hours were set by the Shops Act 1950 until 1 December 1994: each shop had an early closing day, shut by 1pm, and Sunday trading was restricted.
- Wednesday was the most common early closing day nationally, Thursday next (a 1914 source). No specific day found for Hull or Grimsby.
- Hull's traditional general market days were Tuesday, Friday and Saturday, the market halls open every day (a 1969 source).
- No 1990 market days found for Grimsby's Freeman Street market (today Tuesday to Saturday).

## Numbers and patterns worth using

1. Hull fish dock, older era: bobbers from about 2am, auction at 7am, processing staff finished around 5pm, Monday busiest. (Hull Fishing Heritage, undated)
2. Grimsby 1990: the dock busy from about 6am until early afternoon, with cafés at its heart. (Thornton 2022)
3. Grimsby today: buyers from 6am, auction at 7am, Monday to Friday. (Grimsby Fish Market, undated)
4. Trawlermen: about three weeks at sea, then about 72 hours ashore. (Gill 2025)
5. Rehoused people came back by bus once a week to shop and chat. (Harper 2021)
6. A shopping trip meant repeated short stops with people they knew. (Half Life of the Blitz 2022)
7. Pensions and benefits: weekly, in cash, at the post office, on a fixed payday for each benefit. (Hansard 1980) Thursday unconfirmed.
8. Early closing: one weekday afternoon, most often Wednesday; Sunday trading restricted until 1994. (Shops Act 1950; Hansard 1914)
9. Hull market days: Tuesday, Friday and Saturday. (Victoria County History, 1969)
10. Grimsby and Immingham dock redundancies: 498 in 1989, 24 in 1990, 10 in 1991. (Hansard, 13 December 1991)
