# Threatening a witness: England, 1990

Research note, 29 September 2026, for NOTE-11's threat item (`../checklist-sweep-2026-09-28/`); police routine is in `../police-response-1990/`.

## What this is

What "Say a word and you'll regret it" was in law in 1990, and what such threats did. Surveys first asked about intimidation in 1994, so the figures are from 1993 to 1998.

## What we found

1. **No offence of witness intimidation existed until 1994.** Threatening a witness was the common-law crime of perverting the course of justice (Crown Court only, maximum life), which needed proof of intent to obstruct justice. The 1994 Act (s.51, up to five years) presumed that intent, because the old law left gaps "before and after the period when a trial is in progress" (Home Secretary). [5, 8, 11]
2. **A vague threat was a small offence, if any.** It is not a threat to kill (1861 Act s.16). It fits the Public Order Act 1986 s.4 only if it makes someone expect immediate violence. Otherwise it is s.5, threatening words likely to cause alarm or distress: a fine of at most £400 in 1990, and no offence if both people are inside a home. s.4A (intentional harassment) came only in 1995; the Malicious Communications Act 1988 covered letters, not speech. [3, 4, 6, 7, 10]
3. **Charges were rare.** In 1992, 816 people were convicted of perverting the course of justice and 235 were cautioned: about 17 recorded cases for every 10,000 crimes cleared up. One officer talked an assault victim out of pressing charges, warning of worse reprisals. [1]
4. **Most people who were threatened never told the police.** Only a quarter of intimidated men and a third of women reported it; only 6% if they had not reported the first crime. [2]
5. **Fear silences witnesses more than victims, mostly over serious or organised crime.** On five high-crime estates in 1993, fear lay behind 22% of what witnesses kept quiet about (6% for victims): 30% for assaults, 65% for handling stolen goods. The author warns fear may be an excuse. Nationally, fear of reprisals explained only 2 to 3% of unreported crime. [1, 2]
6. **Vandalism rarely reached the police anyway.** About a third of witnesses called the police: 40% of the women and 29% of the men. Only 38% told nobody, and the person told was often a neighbour. Calling the police nearly doubled the risk of intimidation (11% against 6%). [2]
7. **Threats come from the offender and are mostly words.** 85% came from the offender; his family or friends joined in 27%. Verbal abuse came in about 70% of cases, threats in 41 to 49%, assault in 16%. Half the victims saw plain malice; only 8% saw an attempt to stop their evidence. [2]
8. **Who is at risk.** Those who know the offender; nearly 20% of victims on the poorest council estates; women more than men; age made little difference. It often followed police contact others could see: one witness's window was bricked six hours after the police called. [1, 2]
9. **In close-knit places the fear can be general.** Researchers describe "community-wide" intimidation, where general fear stops reporting without any direct threat. The 1993 Salford survey was abandoned after an interviewer was threatened and residents who took part were abused. There, trust and the politics of "grassing" shaped daily life. [1, 2, 9]

## What it suggests for the game (suggestions only)

- A threat over the window should not buy Sheila's or Ada's silence: the deed is minor, he is a stranger, and few would have gone to the police. The threat becomes the story, told first to a neighbour (option (a) in NOTE-11).
- In law the threat is the bigger wrong, if it reaches DS Ellis; that should be rare.
- Fear should depend on what the witness believes stands behind him, not his words. **Question for Jafar (canon):** does the outfit's name frighten the Hook enough to give Tom's threats weight? (Recommended: not yet; the outfit is half-dead and he is new.)
- Offence and anger are likelier than terror; women are likelier than men to call the police; being seen with the police raises the witness's fear and the street's talk.

## Measured, 30 September: the words against the checking model

Jafar ruled on his page that the checking model reads each line about a deed for
a threat. Before wiring it, the two readings were measured on a labelled set of
forty lines (threat-lines.json): twenty threats, plain and veiled; twenty that
are not (a plea, an offer, a joke, a quotation, a warning for their own good,
friendly talk). The model was Haiku, through Claude Code on the subscription,
never the key (ClaimBench threats; the rows are in threat-read-2026-09-30.jsonl).

- The word shapes (Silence.Threatens) caught 3 of 20 threats, with no false alarm.
- The model (ThreatRead) caught 20 of 20, with 1 false alarm in 20: "Be careful
  who you talk to about it. There's people round here who wouldn't like it.",
  which the set itself calls borderline.
- Either reading: 20 of 20, the same one false alarm.

So a threat is a threat when either reading finds it. The model reads beside the
reply, never before it, so the reply waits no longer.
