## B4. Flemish bond and burnt headers (period reference)
- Course pattern (O): stretchers and headers alternate in the same course; each header sits centred over the stretcher below (our own review.json describes it so). buildingconservation.com Brickwork article: "stretchers and headers alternate in the same course" in the late 18th and 19th century examples (https://buildingconservation.com/articles/brick/brickwork.html, read 16 Nov via fetch extract). Geograph Brick bonds: "one long side face and another to header end face"; variants with a header "at every third or fourth brick"; "protruding headers" (https://www.geograph.org.uk/article/Brick-bonds, read 16 Nov, text 2009).
- Period: Kew Palace 1631 earliest whole Flemish facade in England; Georgian face brickwork "laid mainly Flemish bond"; Victorian: Flemish bond "predominated domestically, while English bond was favoured industrially" (buildingconservation.com above, O). RICS: Flemish bond common in the Georgian period, rural buildings often irregular; decorative brickwork "golden age" 1860-1890; bond and gauge "varied considerably by region" in the 19th century (https://ww3.rics.org/uk/en/journals/built-environment-journal/one-brick-at-a-time.html, read 16 Nov).
- Burnt/vitrified/flared headers, colour: overburnt headers "grey to blue" (RICS, same page; used from medieval to the 17th century, to pick out diaper saltires and lines). Tudor: "flared headers of over burnt or vitrified bricks", "deep purple to slate in colour", stained by potash from wood fuel; set in quarter-brick offsets in English or English cross bond to make diaper (buildingconservation.com). Wikipedia Flemish bond: glazed or burned headers give "a dark, glassy surface"; "shiny black" in colonial America (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flemish_bond, read 16 Nov; encyclopaedic, secondary).
- Frequency and pattern: Historic England listing text found via search, 12-14 Brook Street, Jockey Green, Great Bedwyn, Wiltshire: "Flemish bond brickwork with burnt headers in a chequered diaper pattern" (c.18th century; listed 30 July 1986) (https://www.britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101034043-12-13-and-14-brook-street-wolverhampton-b-b-b, read 16 Nov; the historicengland.org.uk original returned 403). Note: that entry is C18 and in Wiltshire, not a 19th-century northern terrace; a snippet also mentioned an early-C18 Great Bardfield house (S, not opened). So "chequer" (every other header burnt) is attested for earlier, rural/southern work; headers not burnt in a chequer pattern are not described anywhere read.
- 19th-century British terraces: no primary source read confirms burnt headers as a regular feature of 19th-century terrace fronts; the search for Lancashire/Yorkshire/Hull/Liverpool appraisals gave none (S). Sources read place vitrified headers mainly in Tudor to early Georgian work (U for the Victorian northern/port claim). Maybank (BRICK-2026-10-04 source 3, O by earlier helper) says northern 9-inch walls are garden-wall bond. Victorian "blue brick" in the north appears as engineering-brick plinths and bands (Hull/Grimsby appraisals, earlier helper, O) which is a different thing from burnt headers.
- Consequence for the 11 Nov tile (observation, not a decision): its dark headers evenly spread resemble a chequer diaper that sources tie to earlier work; no source read gives a share of burnt headers in Victorian terraces. Gloss: only Wikipedia ("glassy") and Tudor texts ("slate"); no numbers on gloss.
- Snapped (half) headers: Flemish bond may use snapped headers so a 4.5 in outer leaf looks solid (buildingconservation.com, O). Corner treatment (quoins, closers): not covered by sources read (U).

## B5. Mortar, joints, brick size
- Brick: UK modern standard from 1965 215 x 102.5 x 65 mm (8.5 x 4 x 2.5 in); with 10 mm joints unit 225 x 112.5 x 75 mm (9 x 4.5 x 3 in) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brick, read 16 Nov, first 100,000 characters). Historic English common brick length and width fairly constant from 1625 (statute 9 x 4.5 x 3 in); depth about 2 in or less earlier, about 2.5 in more recently (same page; the 9 x 4.5 x 3 in figure there reads as a unit including joint only in the modern case; the 1571 charter in buildingconservation's Maybank article gives "9 x 4.5 x 2.5 in"). RICS: gauge and brick size "increased in the latter part of the 19th century", no figures. Period course 85 to 88 mm: own measure from photographs (TERRACE-KIT-TABLE line 33), not a published source.
- Joint width: Tudor 15-25 mm (irregular bricks); Victorian machine-pressed bricks reduced joints to "0.3 inches (8mm)" (buildingconservation.com Brickwork, O). Designing Buildings: joints "typically 10 mm-wide" (https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/Repointing, read 16 Nov). Thin joints (3-6 mm) for pressed fronts: search said unconfirmed (S, U). Review.json measured our tile at about 10 mm.
- Profile: Victorian "weather-struck" and "cut" joints "particularly popular" (buildingconservation.com). Profiles in use: flush (rag), bucket handle, weather struck, weather struck and cut, recessed (Designing Buildings). Georgian "Tuck" pointing (ribbons cream, black, occasionally red in Victorian times: S only). Reviewer's note: Hook sheet mortar "greyer and recessed".
- Colour: sources read give no numeric mortar colour. Lime mortar before about 1919 (earlier helper, S). Black ash mortar in industrial towns: Sheffield c.1880 "coal ash dust and quicklime" (earlier helper, O: periodproperty.co.uk; not re-read now). Lime mortar mixes: 1:2:5 hydraulic lime mix; Tudor lime-rich 1:1 to 1:1.5 with fuel particles (buildingconservation.com).
- Cement repointing: cement pointing harder than historic lime; cement mismatch erodes surrounding masonry (Designing Buildings); raking out to at least 20 mm; pointing grip limited to about 12.7 mm (buildingconservation.com Maybank article). Cement patches in grey (1990, I, BRICK-2026-10-04).
- Soot on mortar versus brick: no source read gives it. Earlier helper (BRICK-METHODS item 4): black crust where sheltered from rain, washed faces clean, sills shed lime run-off (Designing Buildings "Pattern staining", Bilbao black-crust study 2017; O/S, not re-read now). Mortar vs brick sooting in a port town: U.

## B6. Variation in a wall
- Brick-to-brick: colour from raw material chemistry, firing temperature and kiln atmosphere; iron gives pink, lime gives white or yellow; most clay bricks fire red, shifting through purple to brown or grey at about 1,300 C (Wikipedia Brick, read 16 Nov). Within a Flemish wall, headers' firing difference arises from end exposure to the flame (excessive vitrification closest to the heat, buildingconservation.com Maybank article).
- Spalling: under-fired or impure bricks decay faster, "especially with frost action" (buildingconservation.com Brickwork). Spalling itself not described on pages read.
- Repointing: lime pointing "old lime pointing often looks worse than it is and is frequently replaced unnecessarily"; repoint where lime has weathered back more than a bed joint height (Maybank article). Patch variety (cement grey patches, recessed new pointing against flush old): described only in own notes (I) and Richmond guide in search result (S).
- No numbers found for patch share, spall share or brick-to-brick colour spread; own earlier rule: in-house spread under half the step between neighbours (BRICK-METHODS, I).
