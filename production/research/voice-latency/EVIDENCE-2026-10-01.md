# Would moving the voice into the game shorten the delay? The evidence (1 October)

Jafar's condition (Thursday's page, 07:51): before the two weeks of moving the voice into the game start, evidence that it actually shortens the delay, not only frees the graphics card. This runs the research's cheap steps (GPU-QUEUE-PRIORITY-2026-10-01.md) on the real path: the voice's compiled one-token step (tools/voice-live/nano_step_graph.py, 406 MB, ONNX Runtime 1.24.4 with DirectML) timed with nothing else running and beside the finished game standing in the street (the AI tester's packaged copy, its saved settings: 3440 by 1440, every quality group at 3), on this PC's AMD Radeon RX 6700.

## Step 1: is it the round trip to the card each token?

| | Nothing else running | Beside the game |
|---|---|---|
| Today's loop, a read-back each token (`--time`) | 76 to 84 a second | 19 a second |
| Nothing read back until the end (`--pipeline`, 256 steps) | 92 to 113 | 17.5 (one run crashed) |
| The same, DirectML spinning while it waits (`--pipeline --spin`) | 97 to 103 | 17 and 31 (one run crashed) |

Skipping the read-back gives nothing beside the game, and each call blocks until the card has done the step. **Not the round trip.** (Against: the research's cheapest cure, sampling on the card with batched read-back, would not help.)

## Step 0: where the voice's work sits on the card

Windows' counters every 2 to 8 s (tools: scratchpad voice_counters.ps1, typeperf):

- The game: the 3D engine at 90 to 94% and a compute queue at 64 to 72%, 4.8 to 6.1 GB of the card's own memory; the card's memory 7.7 to 9.3 GB in use.
- The voice runs on the card's **3D engine**, the one the game holds at over 90% (DirectML's default queue).
- **Video memory.** With nothing else running, the voice's process holds 2.7 to 2.8 GB of the card's own memory (and about 1 GB shared) and runs 94 steps a second. Beside the game it was given 1.40 GB of the card's own memory and 2.03 GB of shared system memory, which the card reads far more slowly, and the run crashed in DirectML soon after. **Half of what the voice works on is pushed out of the card's memory beside the game.**

Moving the voice into the game does not give it more video memory: the card's memory is shared by all of it either way. The in-game high-priority queue (the research's only remaining lead for the 3D engine's time) would help only the second cause, and is unproven.

## Still running

- The memory cause, tested directly: the game with its texture budget cut (`sg.TextureQuality 0`, `r.Streaming.PoolSize 600`), the voice timed beside it.
- The processor half (step 2): the voice's loop on the processor beside the game as it is, then with the game held to processors 0 to 7 and the voice to 8 to 11, then with the game limited to 4 cores.

## 2 October, 10:00: the processor half, measured

The same compiled step (nano_step_graph.py --time, three runs of 92 tokens each), idle and beside the finished game standing in the street (the AI tester's start, 1280 by 720 windowed, quality at 3):

| | Idle | Beside the game |
|---|---|---|
| On the card (DirectML) | 80 to 93 a second | 12.2 to 12.4 a second |
| On the processor (ONNX Runtime, 3 threads) | 40 to 43 | 16.4 to 20.8 |
| On the processor, the game held to cores 0 to 7 and the voice to 8 to 11 | | 26.0 to 27.3 |

- Beside the game the card is the slowest place for the voice: the game holds its 3D engine at 84 to 88% and 4.9 GB of its memory; even the small step graph (0.7 GB) had 0.45 GB pushed into shared memory.
- The processor, with the game kept off four cores, runs the voice's loop 2.2 times faster than the card does beside the game, at about real time (Nano makes 25 tokens for each second of speech), before any 8-bit step.
- **So the evidence his condition asks for points one way:** the voice on the processor, on cores the game does not use, shortens the voice's share of the delay beside the game, not only frees the card. Still to measure before the two weeks start: the decoder (the second half of the voice's work) on the processor beside the game, and the 8-bit step (voice-in-the-game step 2).
- Not run: the game with its texture memory cut. The tester would not start a second game while someone was using the PC (input 22 s earlier), as it should; it runs when the PC is free.
- The whole voice on the processor (the voice server with LEDGER_VOICE_CPU=1, its token loop and its decoder in PyTorch), idle, six lines through tools/voice-live/latency.py with instant stand-in words: first sound made 2.49 s median, 3.22 s slowest (Sheila's first line, her p267 voice learned afresh), 1.93 s fastest. Beside the game, on cores the game does not use, is the run that decides it; it waits for the PC to be free.

## 2 October, 10:36: the memory cause, measured

- The game with its texture memory cut (`-ini:Engine:[SystemSettings]:r.Streaming.PoolSize=600` and texture quality 0; its log confirms the pool at 600), the voice's step on the card beside it: 35.3 to 36.6 tokens a second, against 12.2 to 12.4 with the game's textures as they are. Three times faster: half the voice's memory pushed out of the card's own was the main cause, as step 0 suspected.
- So two routes now have numbers beside the game: the voice on the card with the game's texture budget capped (36 a second, at a cost to how the street looks, unmeasured), and the voice on the processor on cores the game does not use (27 a second for the step; the whole voice on four cores alone, no game, first sound 3.59 s median, 5.3 s slowest over eight lines). The whole voice beside the game on its own cores is still to time: the tester's guard read its own key presses from the run before as someone at the PC and would not start the game; next time it waits two minutes between runs.

## 2 October, 14:00: the whole voice beside the finished game, the deciding run

Eight lines each through tools/voice-live/latency.py (instant stand-in words, so this is the voice's share alone), beside the finished game standing in the street (the AI tester's start, 1280 by 720 windowed), first sound made after the words:

| | Median | Slowest | Fastest |
|---|---|---|---|
| On the card, as the game runs it today | 3.66 s | 4.52 | 2.56 |
| On the card, the game's texture pool capped at 600 MB | 3.44 s | 3.93 | 2.50 |
| On the processor, four cores the game does not use (the game held to the other eight) | 4.40 s | 5.10 | 3.29 |

- **Moving the voice onto the processor as it is does not shorten the delay**: its token loop is faster there beside the game (27 a second against 12, above), but the second half of its work, the decoder that turns tokens into sound, is slower on four cores than the whole voice on the busy card.
- Capping the game's textures helps a little (0.2 s on the median, 0.6 s on the slowest), at a cost to the look not yet measured.
- So his condition for the two weeks (evidence that moving the voice shortens the delay) is **not met** by the voice as it is. The one route left that could meet it is the voice's decoder exported to a compiled graph at 8 bits and run on the processor (voice-in-the-game step 2): to test before any two weeks, about two days.

## 2 October, 20:20: the step in 8 bits, idle

The voice's token step (F:/LedgerTools/voice-graphs/nano/nano-step.onnx) quantized to 8-bit weights with ONNX Runtime's dynamic quantization (QInt8, 10 seconds, 406 MB to 102 MB: nano-step-int8.onnx), timed as on 1 October (nano_step_graph.py --time --cpu, now with --graph; three processor threads, the PC otherwise idle but for an independent checker compiling tests):

| | Tokens a second (three runs) | Largest score difference from the library's |
|---|---|---|
| Full precision | 38.1, 41.9, 42.2 | 0.00001 |
| 8-bit | 59.5, 70.5, 69.8 | 1.9 |

- The 8-bit step is about 1.7 times faster on the processor, idle. Not yet measured beside the game.
- Its scores move by up to 1.9, enough to change which token is drawn: the 8-bit run stopped at 90 tokens where the full one stopped at 92. The research warned of exactly this ("8-bit shifted pauses"). So it counts only once its speech is heard and judged by ear against the full step's, line for line; until then it is a speed, not a result.
