# Street clutter, 1990: ten pieces for the high street (research note, 29 September 2026)

**The problem.** The street needs ten pieces of everyday street clutter. It is a terraced high street in a small North East or Yorkshire port town, with small shops, a minicab office, a fish shop, a pawn shop, a café and a yard behind a parade. Each piece is to be modelled in 3D and checked against real photographs of the thing as it looked in Britain around 1990. This note picks the ten. For each it gives:
- the model or type in use
- dimensions
- colours and materials
- photographs (links only)
- a breakdown into simple shapes for modelling

Content rule applied: no pub signs, beer or milk crates, betting shops, toys or children's things. About thirty minutes of research by a separate helper given the problem, saved here by the builder (the helper could not write files). Sources are dated at the end. **Uncertain** marks anything not confirmed.

**Limit, read first.** Photographs taken 1985–1995 in northern England were not found in the time. Most links show surviving examples photographed after 2000. For the kiosk, the lamp and the pillar box the shape is unchanged, but details such as logos and plates are not always 1990's. The last section lists where period photographs are likely to be.

## 1. Pillar box

**Type in 1990.** Mostly cast-iron cylinders of the older standard designs, each carrying the cipher of its reign (VR, EVIIR, GR, GVIR, EIIR). The design came in two sizes from 1879: Type A, wider, and Type B, narrower (Wikipedia; PostboxMap).

The capless Type K was designed by Tony Gibbs and first cast by Lion Foundry, Kirkintilloch. It was introduced on 31 July 1980 and made until about 2000–01. It also fits 1990 as a newer replacement box (1980s Actual; Wikipedia).

EIIR is reportedly the commonest cipher, over 60% of boxes still standing (**uncertain**, secondary source). Recommendation: an EIIR Type A of the 1950s–60s; Carron made many.

Penfold hexagonal replicas appeared in conservation areas from 1990 (one was dedicated at Tenby on 5 May 1990). They are wrong for an ordinary working street.

**Lettering.** "POST OFFICE" in raised letters; "ROYAL MAIL" came on new boxes in the early 1990s (Wikipedia). Exactly where the words sit on an EIIR Type A needs checking against a photo (**uncertain**).

**Dimensions (uncertain; no drawing found).**
- UK Architectural Antiques listings: the whole casting is 73 in (185 cm), of which 15–20 in is buried. That leaves about 135–147 cm above the pavement.
- Body width in those listings: 19 in (48 cm) on a c.1955 box, and 15 in (38 cm) on a Carron "PB42".
- Wikipedia: Type B is 64 in (1.63 m) tall with a 48 in circumference (about 39 cm across). It does not say whether the height includes the buried part.
- An untraced source gives a Type A as 5 ft 4 in tall and 1 ft 7¼ in (49 cm) wide. **Unverified.**
- Working figures: about 150 cm above ground and 49 cm across. Scale from a photo to confirm (a KX100 is 89 cm wide).

**Colours and materials.**
- Grey cast iron. Type K was the first box in impact-resistant iron.
- Red body, black base. The paint standard is **uncertain**; BS 381C 538 "Cherry" is often cited but not confirmed.
- A collection-times plate on the door. Many boxes also had a small "next collection" number tablet (**uncertain**).

**Photos (survivors only).**
- Geograph 4378457: Tenby, 10 May 2008, CC BY-SA. A Penfold replica put up in 1990, useful as what not to use. https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/4378457
- Geograph 24094: Kirkby in Cleveland, 5 July 2005, Mick Garratt, CC BY-SA. The box type is not stated. https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/24094
- Identification guide: https://postboxmap.co.uk/identification-guide

**Modelling.**
- Base: black, about 20 cm tall.
- Body: a cylinder 49 cm across and about 115 cm tall.
- Cap: domed, with an overhanging moulded rim a few centimetres proud of the body. The top is at about 150 cm.
- Aperture: a horizontal slot with a small hood, just under the cap.
- Door: a flush panel with the cast EIIR cipher and crown, a plate frame and a keyhole.
- Wrong for 1990: "ROYAL MAIL" lettering; modern large "Last collection" plates with barcodes or QR codes; purple "priority" plates (2020); gold boxes (2012); a Penfold replica; a plain "ER" cipher without the II.

## 2. Telephone kiosk: BT KX100 (1985 livery)

**Type in 1990.**
- Designed by David Carter Associates (DCA Design) and engineered by GKN, Telford.
- Introduced in 1985 under British Telecom's £160 million modernisation; made until 1996.
- About 3,000 a month were made in 1987–89 (Wikipedia; The Telephone Box).
- A red K6 could still stand alongside. The KX100 is recommended because its livery dates the street.

**Dimensions.**
- 7 ft 1 in (216 cm) tall by 2 ft 11 in (89 cm) wide (The Telephone Box).
- 2192 × 890 × 890 mm and 266 kg (Grokipedia; its citations were not checked).
- Use 2170–2190 mm tall, 890 mm square.

**Materials and livery, 1985–91.**
- Frame: stainless-steel struts, rear panel and roof, with anodised aluminium frames around glass or acrylic panes.
- Flat roof, and a gap at the base so litter blows through.
- Two large panes per side, separated by a slim black plastic modesty band.
- Smoked glass early, clear glass later.
- Door handle panel: moulded plastic, yellow on coin kiosks, green on Phonecard kiosks.
- Logo: the yellow dotted British Telecom "T" on the upper glass.
- From 1991 came the Piper "BT" logo and a red handle, both **wrong for 1990** (The Telephone Box; Wikipedia; Country Life).
- A lit "TELEPHONE" header in 1985–91 is **uncertain**; check the Maaraig photo.

**Photos.**
- The Maaraig kiosk on the Isle of Harris is believed to be the only one still in the full 1985 livery. Photo by John Maher, in the Twentieth Century Society article of 6 June 2024: https://c20society.org.uk/news/final-call-phonebox-campaign-revived-for-threatened-kx100-kiosks
- Geograph 5558709: Paythorne, Lancashire, 19 July 2017, David Hillas, CC BY-SA. The shape is right; the door has the post-1991 Piper logo. https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/5558709
- Geograph tag page, 124 images, mostly from 2005 on: https://www.geograph.org.uk/tagged/KX100+Telephone+Kiosk
- The Telephone Box: http://www.the-telephone-box.co.uk/kiosks/kx100/

**Modelling.**
- Box 89 × 89 × about 218 cm, with a flat stainless roof slab slightly proud of the frame.
- Each side: an upper and a lower pane either side of a black band 10–15 cm high at chest height. The rear panel is solid steel, with the phone on it.
- A glazed door with a vertical yellow or green handle strip.
- A small gap at the base.
- The yellow dotted T on the upper glass.
- Wrong for 1990: Piper logo, red handle, "BT" lettering, a domed red roof (that is the 1996 KX100 Plus).

**Alternative: the K6.** Giles Gilbert Scott, 1935. The following is from memory and was **not checked**: 2.51 m tall, 0.91 m square, BS 381C 539 "currant red", "TELEPHONE" fascias, crowns. Verify before use.

## 3. Street lamp: concrete column with a Thorn Beta 5 lantern

**Type in 1990.**
- The Beta 5 is described as the commonest low-pressure sodium (SOX) lantern in the UK.
- It was installed in huge numbers from the 1960s, especially during the 1970s energy crisis.
- The familiar version appeared in the early 1980s, replacing mercury lanterns; one example is dated 1983 (Simon Cornwell; streetlightonline; Technojunk Files).
- Lamp: usually 35 W SOX (earlier 40 W SOX or 60 W SOI).
- Columns were precast concrete, from Stanton or Concrete Utilities (the "big two"), who made them into the 1980s.
- Example column: the Stanton 10F of 1958, 15 ft (4.6 m), top or side entry, bracket reaching out 1 ft 4 in to 1 ft 6 in.

**The light.** Two close lines in the yellow, about 589 nm, give a monochrome orange in which colours vanish. The lamp takes about 10 minutes to reach full brightness.

**Dimensions.**
- Column: 4.6–6 m (**uncertain** for any given street).
- Lantern: 7¾ in (20 cm) tall on the early version. Its length was not found; estimate 60–70 cm (**uncertain**).

**Materials.**
- Column: grey precast concrete, square or tapered in section, with a door near the foot (**uncertain** detail).
- Lantern: aluminium canopy, and a clear or opal acrylic ("Diakon") bowl with refractor grooves.

**Photos.**
- Geograph 1485521: a 1950s Stanton and Staveley concrete column (Robert Lamb; date not checked).
- streetlightonline Beta 5 pages: https://www.streetlightonline.co.uk/beta_5_photographs.htm and https://www.streetlightonline.co.uk/older_beta_5_photographs.htm
- Stanton catalogue scans on Flickr: https://www.flickr.com/photos/36844288@N00/albums/72157626567190154/

**Modelling.**
- Column: a tapered shaft 4.6–6 m tall, 20–25 cm at the foot, with a door plate about 50 cm up.
- Bracket: an arm reaching out 40–45 cm, or a spigot for a post-top lantern.
- Lantern: a boat-shaped canopy 60–70 cm long over a deep clear trough-shaped bowl, with a glowing U-shaped tube inside.
- Light: a single-colour orange source, with no white light on the street.
- Wrong for 1990: LED heads, white light, flat-glass lanterns, modern galvanised columns.

## 4. Belisha beacons and the zebra crossing

**Type in 1990.**
- Amber globes on black-and-white posts, one each side of the crossing (two required). National from 1935.
- In 1990 the rules were the "Zebra" Pedestrian Crossings Regulations 1971, as amended in 1990. They were replaced in 1997.
- Zigzag markings date from 1971 (from memory, **not checked**).

**Dimensions.** These are the 1997 figures, assumed the same in 1971 (**uncertain**).
- Globe: yellow, 275–335 mm across.
- Post bands: 275–335 mm deep; the lowest band is black and may be up to 1 m.
- Height: globe centre 2.1–3.1 m above the ground.
- Post: shaft 76 mm across, base 132–140 mm (Leicestershire standard drawing, 2024).
- Flash rate: 750 ms on, 750 ms off (a modern figure).
- The globe may be glass or polycarbonate; when the change came is **uncertain**.

**Photos.**
- Commons "Category:Belisha beacons", 67 files, including John Ball's 1960s Birmingham photos: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Belisha_beacons
- Blantyre Project, "Belisha Beacons 1967": https://blantyreproject.com/2017/03/belisha-beacons-1967/
- Geograph 5108422 (date not checked): https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/5108422

**Modelling.**
- Post: a 76 mm tube about 2.5 m tall, with 30 cm bands and black at the bottom.
- Globe: a 30 cm glowing amber sphere with a collar and a cap, flashing about 0.75 s on and 0.75 s off.
- Road: stripes, with zigzags each side.
- Wrong for 1990: LED halo rings, reflective sleeves, tactile paving (1990s; date **uncertain**).

## 5. Bus stop flag and pole

**Type in 1990.**
- The standard sign, TSRGD diagram 970, is rectangular; its current listed size is 450 × 375 mm.
- Under the National Bus Company, flags outside London were diagram 970 made in metal.
- After deregulation in October 1986, operators added their own flags, sometimes several on one pole (a "totem pole").
- West Yorkshire's Metro used its own logo on its flags.
- Tyne and Wear PTE used a blue and yellow "TW" logo until it became Nexus in 1996. Its buses became Busways in October 1986 (Beauty of Transport 2016; Wikipedia).
- For a fictional town: a flag of the diagram 970 kind.

**Dimensions.**
- Flag: 450 × 375 mm.
- Height to the top of the flag: about 2.5–3 m (**uncertain**).

**Colours.** The 1990 colours of diagram 970 were **not confirmed**; check the government's "UK traffic sign 970" SVG on Commons. The lettering is the Transport typeface and the bus symbol is Calvert's. Poles were steel or concrete, usually with a timetable case.

**Photos.** None dated found. Leads:
- https://thebeautyoftransport.com/2016/03/09/flying-the-flag-beautiful-and-not-so-beautiful-british-bus-stop-flags/
- https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Diagrams_of_bus_stop_signs

**Modelling.**
- A 60–75 mm tube about 3 m tall.
- A flag 450 × 375 mm at the top, at right angles to the kerb, with an optional second flag below.
- A shallow timetable box at eye height.
- Wrong for 1990: live display screens, Nexus branding, QR codes.

## 6. Street name plate

**Type.** Black capitals on a white plate, on terrace end walls or on two short posts. The Ministry of Transport's 1952 circular recommended Kindersley's lettering, and "Kindersley MOT" became dominant outside London (Beauty of Transport, 3 November 2021). Old cast-iron plates with raised letters also survived (**uncertain** how common).

**Dimensions (uncertain).**
- Plate: 20–25 cm tall, 60–120 cm long.
- Letters: 8–9 cm.
- Border: about 12.5 mm (a modern specification).

**Materials.** Cast aluminium, pressed steel or cast iron.

**Photos.** None dated found. Lead: https://thebeautyoftransport.com/2021/11/03/how-serifs-lost-the-road-war-but-won-the-streets/

**Modelling.**
- A thin plate about 25 × 90 × 1 cm, with a border and the name as a decal.
- Fixed at 2.2–2.5 m on the wall.
- Wrong for 1990: reflective sheeting, postcodes, sans-serif lettering, recycled-plastic boards.

## 7. Council litter bin (weakest)

**Type in 1990 (uncertain).** No dated source was found. Candidates:
- (a) An open-top steel or glass-fibre bin on a pole or lamp post, marked "LITTER".
- (b) A hooded glass-fibre bin such as Glasdon's Topsy. Glasdon has made plastic street furniture since 1959; the Topsy's first date was not found.
- (c) A round precast concrete bin with a pebbled finish and a metal liner.

Councils have used bright bin colours since the 1950s "Keep Britain Tidy" years (Historic England blog, 2022). Recommendation: (a) or (c).

**Dimensions (uncertain).** About 90–100 cm tall, 50–60 cm across, holding about 90 L.

**Photos.** None. A photo is needed before building.

**Modelling (c).** A hollow cylinder 55 cm across and 90 cm tall, rough grey pebbled surface, with a liner showing at the top. Wrong for 1990: split recycling bins, the "Topsy 2000", cast-iron "heritage" bins (**uncertain**).

## 8. Bollard (weak)

**Type (uncertain).**
- Old cast-iron "cannon" bollards at terrace corners, alongside plain precast concrete ones.
- A modern cannon reproduction stands 1223 mm above ground (Broxap). Older ones were often about 90 cm (**uncertain**).
- Black paint, sometimes with a white band; concrete left grey.

**Photos.** None dated.

**Modelling.**
- A tapered cylinder 25 cm at the foot, 90–120 cm tall, with rings and a half-sphere top.
- Wrong for 1990: reflective bands, stainless steel, gold paint.

## 9. Grit bin (weak on date)

**Type (uncertain).**
- A yellow glass-fibre bin with a hinged lid, marked "GRIT" or "SALT".
- Yellow is standard today; whether glass-fibre bins were already standard in 1990 is **uncertain**. Older ones were timber or concrete.
- Size: the Glasdon Nestor holds 90 L; council bins are about 200–400 L (**uncertain**). Roughly 60–80 cm tall, 70–100 cm wide, 50–70 cm deep.

**Photos.** None dated.

**Modelling.** A slightly tapered box with a domed or sloped lid and a lip, and raised lettering on the front.

## 10. Galvanised dustbin (the yard)

**Type.**
- A round galvanised steel dustbin to British Standard BS 792, with a lid and two handles.
- Wheeled bins were spreading from the mid-1980s. Many northern terraces still used dustbins or bags in 1990 (**uncertain** for any one town).

**Dimensions.** The modern 90 L equivalent is 575 mm tall, 450 mm across the top and 400 mm across the bottom. The 1990 BS 792 figures were not seen.

**Materials.** Grey galvanised steel with pressed horizontal ribs, a domed lid with a handle, dented and dull.

**Photos.** None dated.

**Modelling.**
- A frustum 575 mm tall, 450 mm at the top and 400 mm at the bottom.
- Two or three ribs, two side handles, a domed lid and a rolled rim.
- Wrong for 1990: wheelie bins with council logos (**uncertain**), plastic recycling boxes.
