The town's own news: one sample

Until now every story the street passed on was about Tom, so before he did anything the street had nothing to say, and over two hours the only stories anyone told were his. This is the first story of the town's own, to hear before any more are written.

The story

Hal and Rita had words in the pawn, and nobody knows what about.

It happens at Rita's pawn at eleven on Monday morning, when Hal calls there as he does every Monday. Everybody in the pawn then saw it: Rita and Hal themselves, Darren Milner, the pawn's back-room hand, its appraiser, its clerk, and the locksmith who works its counter in the mornings. From them it travels the way any story does, friend to friend, as their days cross; Hal and Rita keep it to themselves.

It names only people the street knows by name, says only what was seen, and leaves the why open: nobody in the town can say what the row was about, so no character can invent it.

How Tom hears it

Two neighbours, as he passes:

  • "Here, Hal and Rita had words in the pawn, and nobody knows what about." / "Never."
  • "Talk of the street, this. Hal and Rita had words in the pawn, and nobody knows what about." / "I'd not have thought it."
  • "Seems Hal and Rita had words in the pawn, and nobody knows what about." / "Well, it's none of my business."
  • "Hal and Rita had words in the pawn, and nobody knows what about, apparently." / "Who told you that?"

He makes out four tellings at most; after that it is the street's murmur.

What it does

Measured over two hours of play on the named cast's street (TownReach): he overhears it in every run, the first time about a minute in, four times in all, never the same words twice. It raises nobody's suspicion of him.

Why

The old game's four hints ran on the clock: at two game minutes a real second they came at 1, 5, 30 and 90 seconds, two of them overlapping, and a player who had not yet found the walking keys was told about the Ledger and a night job inside the first minute and a half (production/research/teaching-in-thirty-minutes/SUMMARY.md). The first session's own job is four steps: he moves, he is seen, it was written down, it came back. The hints follow those steps.

The six moments

Keys are shown as the player has them bound, in braces here. A spoken line is said in that person's approved voice when they are with him; the plain line always shows.

  • A new game, and he has not moved after four seconds. Plain: "{Move} walks, {Run} runs. People on this street notice who's about."
  • He is free to talk to somebody: in the first hour, when Sheila's walk-round ends at the office door, after the notice that the town answers in words written by an AI. Sheila: "That's Ron on the rank. Go and say hello. Everyone out there wants a look at you." Plain: "Walk up to anyone and press {Talk}. What you tell them, they remember." (Sheila's approved voice still drifts American in stretches; until a take of this line passes the accent gate, the plain line shows alone.)
  • Ron hands him the coat and the outfit's envelope, the first ask. Ron, as he hands it over: "If you go tonight, boss, wear that. In the dark nobody looks twice at a coat." Plain, with it: "{Coat} puts the coat on or takes it off: in the dark it makes you harder to recognise."
  • Somebody saw him do something the town will talk about. Plain: "Somebody saw that. What they make of it can go round."
  • He overheard a remark about himself. Plain: "That was about you. {Ledger} shows what you believe the street knows."
  • He opens the Ledger. Plain, at the top of its screen: "What you believe the street now knows about you."

Rules

  • Each shows once in a game, and the save keeps which have.
  • Walking first: in a new game nothing else shows until he has walked or been told how. After a load he has walked already.
  • Never two within twelve real seconds; a hint that falls due inside that waits its turn. Ron's handover and the Ledger's line show when they happen, since they are part of what is happening.
  • A hint about what just happened (4 and 5) is dropped if it has waited fifteen seconds, and the next time it happens it can teach; the talk hint waits up to forty-five.
  • The simulation decides the moments (the Core); the game shows the text and plays Sheila's and Ron's lines (the builder's, in their approved voices).

When DS Ellis turns up, and what she has

For Jafar's approval, 29 September (town list 6ar; the checklist's K7, A15.09 and A15.11). The first hour he approved says that on days 4 and 5, if the street has got loud about him, DS Carol Ellis turns up on Quay Street. Nothing decided when she comes or what she has. This follows the research on 1990 policing (production/research/police-response-1990/): most offences are never reported, a victim reports far more than a witness, a detective walks a street only after serious crime, and a charge needs a witness who names him. The street should always know more than she does.

Who goes to the police

  • A shopkeeper whose window is put in reports it, for the insurance. That brings a uniformed constable and a crime report, not a detective. Not Rita: the empire roster makes her the new crew's fence, and a fence does not ask the police into her shop, so her own window goes unreported.
  • Some never go at all, whatever they see or suffer, because their trade keeps them from it: Rita, the customs officer (the machine's stamp, the roster says), Hal, the pawn's back-room hand, the scrap dealer, and the outfit's man.
  • Somebody hurt or robbed reports it unless they know him well enough to settle it themselves, or are too frightened: a wounding or a robbery more readily than a push. The dead report nothing; whoever finds the body does.
  • A witness goes only for a crime a detective takes: a wounding or a robbery only unafraid and not on his side; a killing whenever not on his side, the brave because they can and the nervous because they crack. An ordinary witness, neither brave nor against him, says nothing about a wounding, as most witnesses said nothing.
  • A sighting that is no crime (a man with an envelope at the landing) is never reported. It is talk.
  • Anybody on a hook says nothing; anybody bought or frightened quiet says nothing, except about a body, which no money keeps.

Each person's own nerve and loyalty decide, so the same deed goes differently with different people there. The cast file does not yet give anybody their own yet: doing so moves bribes, debts and more besides, so its scope is on your page. Until then everybody starts ordinary and no witness goes to the police, unless the story cools them on him (Ada, stood up for her tea, is one).

She comes to Quay Street, each reason once: for a body; for a crime a detective takes, once somebody reports it; and, from day 4, for the street's talk once three people of his day world are passing talk of his nights round (heard, not seen).

Measured with the town's routines (TownReach --loud): on night one only four of the cast are out to see him, and whether the talk gets loud depends on which of them does. Plainly seen by Ron, who meets half the street, five to eight people are passing it round on days 3 to 5, so she comes on day 4; by Dusan, two; by the other two, one or none. A second night seen changes that only when Ron is one of the two who saw him; any pair without Ron stays at two or below. In the coat, one night seen by Ron reaches three only on day 3, before she can come for talk, and a second night with Ron holds it at three on day 4 or 5. So at three she comes in the first hour only if Ron saw him, on either night; at two, Dusan's sighting would bring her too.

What an arrest does

For Jafar's page, 29 September (town list 6bp; ROADMAP stage 3, an arrest reachable from live play). From the research (production/research/police-response-1990/CUSTODY-2026-09-29.md): in 1990 an arrest cost hours, not the game.

One question for you first

Today nothing can lead to an arrest. The friends' build has one crime, Rita's window; Rita never goes to the police (her trade), and a witness went to the police only for a crime a detective takes. So:

  • (A) A witness to the window can go to the police (recommended): only somebody who saw him plainly, is not afraid and has cooled on him (Ada, say, after he stood her up for her tea); at the town's middle tempers nobody does, so an arrest is rare and earned. Built this way meanwhile.
  • (B) Rita reports her own window, for the insurance, as most shopkeepers did; a constable asks round, and a witness who cooled on him names him.
  • (C) No arrest in the first build: only for violence, which it does not have yet.

What happens

  • Who comes: for a window, a uniformed constable, the morning after somebody gave a statement naming him; for a wounding, a robbery or a killing, DS Ellis on her visit, once she holds a statement.
  • The station: the custody sergeant books him in and keeps anything that may be evidence: the coat, if he wore it to the deed.
  • How long: as the Home Office's own study at the end of the 1980s found: a window about six hours (two if he owns up), a wounding eight, a robbery twelve.
  • How it ends: a window he owns up to, a caution and nothing more (and his coat back); otherwise charged, on the named statement, and bailed to the magistrates' next weekday sitting, so a Friday's charge waits for Monday (that date is my inference). Never taken twice for the same deed. Never a game end.
  • A killing is my choice, not the record's: held the twenty-two hours the most serious offences averaged, then bailed to come back in four weeks while they make their enquiries. In 1990 he would more likely have been kept for court; the game has no prison, so say if a killing should end differently.

The week's end: Sheila's question and his answer

For Jafar's page, 30 September (town list 6ca; the first hour you approved: "The week ends on day 7 ... Sheila, over the real book, 'So which is it going to be?'"; the outline: wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say, and the street learns the answer).

How it goes

  • When: day 7 is a Sunday, Sheila's day off, so she comes in to the office for it, at ten, and waits till twelve; once she has asked, she stays till six for his answer, "before the day's out". The first time he talks with her at the office from then on, she puts the question. If he never comes on the Sunday, she asks the next time he talks with her there.
  • With talk off it all still works: her lines here are fixed, not written by the model.
  • Over which book: Mickey's real book if she trusts him (the question above), the day-book if not.
  • His answer is two steps, as his no to Ron is, because it is for good: a line that sounds like one answer gets her plain question back, and only his plain yes to that is his answer. A line that mixes two answers, jokes, supposes or puts it off is no answer.
  • Refusing to say: said plainly ("None of your business"), or the day she asked ends unanswered. A put-off ("I'll let you know") is no answer.
  • The street learns it: she holds it first-hand, and anybody where she is then (after her hours, nobody else); how far it goes is her discretion and the town's talk.
  • Winding it down ends Mickey's arrangement that night, as her words say: Ron carries the word down to the landing and the outfit's talk has it, as it does a no. Taking it over never undoes a no he already gave Ron, and she never offers what is gone ("Mickey's arrangements are finished, and that doesn't change"). Your question below.
