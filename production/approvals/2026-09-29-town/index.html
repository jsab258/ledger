The first hour, on paper

For Jafar's approval (town list 6j; ROADMAP stage 4, "the first hour"). Under two pages, not a script. It follows the story outline on the 28 September page (arrival and Act I), canon, and three pieces of research: teaching a social system in thirty minutes, what players do when a world remembers them, and how stories travel between the named cast (measured 28 September).

What the first hour has to do

The research is plain about it. Remembering is quiet and forgetting is loud: a town that tracks you only tints its greetings unless something comes back and is said to your face. So the first hour is built on four steps, and the last is the one no hint can fake:

  • He moves. Walking, talking, the office, the street.
  • He is seen doing something that matters, or is known to have refused.
  • It is written down. The town holds it; his Ledger holds what he believes it holds.
  • It comes back, said to his face by somebody who was not there.

The Meridian Test asks that the world visibly know him at least once in the first thirty minutes. Among the forty named people as they live now, a plainly seen night reaches a second person in about six real minutes (the slowest tenth, nineteen); in the coat that makes him harder to recognise, it reaches anyone at all only about six times in ten within a week. Those figures are a best case: they assume he has met everyone and average sightings over every hour of the week. Only somebody who can tell it is him shows what they have heard, so the one who brings it back on day 2 has to be somebody he met on day

  • Measured with the five he meets (Sheila, Ron, Darren, Ada and June) and a

night-one sighting by whoever of the forty is out: one of them says it to his face by minute thirty in 13 of 13 cases, but 7 of those only at minute thirty itself, because the story only reaches those five in the evening, through the friends they meet then (they are about during the day; the story is not yet with them); in the coat, 6 of 13. So for the hour to hold, the first night's job should be seen by, or told to, somebody close to one of the day-one five.

The clock

The old game ran two game minutes to a real second, so a day was twelve real minutes; the new game has not set its clock yet. The times below assume the old rate and a nine o'clock start. At a slower rate the hour covers fewer days, and day 2 falls later: if it falls past minute thirty, "it comes back" misses the Meridian bar, so the rate is chosen with this hour in mind. The days are the likely order when the story is played straight; as the outline says, beats fire on states, not on the clock.

The hour

Minutes 0 to 7: day 1. Tom arrives with one suitcase and the letter, after the funeral he missed. Sheila Dunn is at Mickey's to meet him, shows him round, and stops at the one door she does not open. The first time he can speak to anyone, the game tells him, once, that the town answers in words written by an AI model and that a line can be reported. On the street: Ron Kirby at the rank calls him boss and watches him; Darren Milner is on the pavement, friendly and owing; Ada is at her window; June, Mickey's daughter, back for the funeral, wants nothing from the office and says so. Strangers call him the new owner; the people he has met use his name, because they know him now (Sheila's card has her keep him at "new management" until he earns it: see the question below).

Minutes 7 to 11: night one. The outfit's first ask comes, as Mickey's arrangements outlive him: a small job for the night, and a coat that makes him harder to recognise. He can refuse; refusing ends Mickey's arrangement, and does not fail the game.

Minutes 12 to 24: day 2, it comes back. Whatever he did with the ask, the outfit's people talk about it, so the night is a story either way. If he went and was seen, it reaches a second person within minutes; somebody who heard looks at him longer, and the first time Darren, or anyone he met yesterday who has heard, sees him, they say it to his face ("Funny hours you keep"). If he went unseen, or refused, it is the outfit's talk that comes back the same way ("Heard you told them no"). His Ledger shows what he believes the street now knows.

Minutes 24 to 36: day 3. Ada asks him in for tea on the evening the outfit's second ask falls: the week's first test of which life he keeps. Alison Sedman asks him about the warehouse fire, the story she will not drop.

Minutes 36 to 60: days 4 and 5. If the street has got loud about him, DS Carol Ellis turns up on Quay Street. What he did on those two nights keeps coming back, from more of the people he has met.

The week ends on day 7, a little under half an hour later: Sheila, over the real book, "So which is it going to be?"

How it teaches

Every hint fires the first time it can mean something, never on the clock: the first time he can talk, the first night job, the first time he is seen, the first remark he overhears about himself, the first time he opens the Ledger. The research found the old game's four hints all firing inside ninety seconds because they ran on the clock, two of them overlapping. Hints are the world's own words where they can be (Sheila on the rank, Ron on the coat), and a line of plain text only where the world has nobody to say it.

What is not in the first hour

The three rivals, beyond a name overheard. Act II. Any ending. Money beyond the takings and the first job's envelope. Nothing that needs a number to be understood. And not carried over from the old game: its day-5 scene with Ron, which breaks the content rule.

The town's own news: one sample

Until now every story the street passed on was about Tom, so before he did anything the street had nothing to say, and over two hours the only stories anyone told were his. This is the first story of the town's own, to hear before any more are written.

The story

Hal and Rita had words in the pawn, and nobody knows what about.

It happens at Rita's pawn at eleven on Monday morning, when Hal calls there as he does every Monday. Everybody in the pawn then saw it: Rita and Hal themselves, Darren Milner, the pawn's back-room hand, its appraiser, its clerk, and the locksmith who works its counter in the mornings. From them it travels the way any story does, friend to friend, as their days cross; Hal and Rita keep it to themselves.

It names only people the street knows by name, says only what was seen, and leaves the why open: nobody in the town can say what the row was about, so no character can invent it.

How Tom hears it

Two neighbours, as he passes:

  • "Here, Hal and Rita had words in the pawn, and nobody knows what about." / "Never."
  • "Talk of the street, this. Hal and Rita had words in the pawn, and nobody knows what about." / "I'd not have thought it."
  • "Seems Hal and Rita had words in the pawn, and nobody knows what about." / "Well, it's none of my business."
  • "Hal and Rita had words in the pawn, and nobody knows what about, apparently." / "Who told you that?"

He makes out four tellings at most; after that it is the street's murmur.

What it does

Measured over two hours of play on the named cast's street (TownReach): he overhears it in every run, the first time about a minute in, four times in all, never the same words twice. It raises nobody's suspicion of him.

The hints, each the first time it matters

For Jafar's approval, 29 September (town list 6y; the checklist's A04.07, "instructions shown when their actions become relevant"). The first hour he approved says every hint fires the first time it can mean something, never on the clock, in the world's own words where somebody can say it (Sheila, Ron on the coat), and a line of plain text only where nobody can.

Why

The old game's four hints ran on the clock: at two game minutes a real second they came at 1, 5, 30 and 90 seconds, two of them overlapping, and a player who had not yet found the walking keys was told about the Ledger and a night job inside the first minute and a half (production/research/teaching-in-thirty-minutes/SUMMARY.md). The first session's own job is four steps: he moves, he is seen, it was written down, it came back. The hints follow those steps.

The six moments

Keys are shown as the player has them bound, in braces here. A spoken line is said in that person's approved voice when they are with him; the plain line always shows.

  • A new game, and he has not moved after four seconds. Plain: "{Move} walks, {Run} runs. People on this street notice who's about."
  • He is free to talk to somebody: in the first hour, when Sheila's walk-round ends at the office door, after the notice that the town answers in words written by an AI. Sheila: "That's Ron on the rank. Go and say hello. Everyone out there wants a look at you." Plain: "Walk up to anyone and press {Talk}. What you tell them, they remember." (Sheila's approved voice still drifts American in stretches; until a take of this line passes the accent gate, the plain line shows alone.)
  • Ron hands him the coat and the outfit's envelope, the first ask. Ron, as he hands it over: "If you go tonight, boss, wear that. In the dark nobody looks twice at a coat." Plain, with it: "{Coat} puts the coat on or takes it off: in the dark it makes you harder to recognise."
  • Somebody saw him do something the town will talk about. Plain: "Somebody saw that. What they make of it can go round."
  • He overheard a remark about himself. Plain: "That was about you. {Ledger} shows what you believe the street knows."
  • He opens the Ledger. Plain, at the top of its screen: "What you believe the street now knows about you."

Rules

  • Each shows once in a game, and the save keeps which have.
  • Walking first: in a new game nothing else shows until he has walked or been told how. After a load he has walked already.
  • Never two within twelve real seconds; a hint that falls due inside that waits its turn. Ron's handover and the Ledger's line show when they happen, since they are part of what is happening.
  • A hint about what just happened (4 and 5) is dropped if it has waited fifteen seconds, and the next time it happens it can teach; the talk hint waits up to forty-five.
  • The simulation decides the moments (the Core); the game shows the text and plays Sheila's and Ron's lines (the builder's, in their approved voices).

When DS Ellis turns up, and what she has

For Jafar's approval, 29 September (town list 6ar; the checklist's K7, A15.09 and A15.11). The first hour he approved says that on days 4 and 5, if the street has got loud about him, DS Carol Ellis turns up on Quay Street. Nothing decided when she comes or what she has. This follows the research on 1990 policing (production/research/police-response-1990/): most offences are never reported, a victim reports far more than a witness, a detective walks a street only after serious crime, and a charge needs a witness who names him. The street should always know more than she does.

Who goes to the police

  • A shopkeeper whose window is put in reports it, for the insurance. That brings a uniformed constable and a crime report, not a detective. Not Rita: the empire roster makes her the new crew's fence, and a fence does not ask the police into her shop, so her own window goes unreported.
  • Some never go at all, whatever they see or suffer, because their trade keeps them from it: Rita, the customs officer (the machine's stamp, the roster says), Hal, the pawn's back-room hand, the scrap dealer, and the outfit's man.
  • Somebody hurt or robbed reports it unless they know him well enough to settle it themselves, or are too frightened: a wounding or a robbery more readily than a push. The dead report nothing; whoever finds the body does.
  • A witness goes only for a crime a detective takes: a wounding or a robbery only unafraid and not on his side; a killing whenever not on his side, the brave because they can and the nervous because they crack. An ordinary witness, neither brave nor against him, says nothing about a wounding, as most witnesses said nothing.
  • A sighting that is no crime (a man with an envelope at the landing) is never reported. It is talk.
  • Anybody on a hook says nothing; anybody bought or frightened quiet says nothing, except about a body, which no money keeps.

Each person's own nerve and loyalty decide, so the same deed goes differently with different people there. The cast file does not yet give anybody their own yet: doing so moves bribes, debts and more besides, so its scope is on your page. Until then everybody starts ordinary and no witness goes to the police, unless the story cools them on him (Ada, stood up for her tea, is one).

When she comes

Ellis is already on the warehouse fire, the outline's Act I thread, so her enquiries bring her through the Hook. She comes to Quay Street, each reason once: for a body; for a crime a detective takes, once somebody reports it; and, from day 4, for the street's talk once three people of his day world are passing talk of his nights round (heard, not seen).

Measured with the town's routines (TownReach --loud): on night one only four of the cast are out to see him, and whether the talk gets loud depends on which of them does. Plainly seen by Ron, who meets half the street, five to eight people are passing it round on days 3 to 5, so she comes on day 4; by Dusan, two; by the other two, one or none. A second night seen changes that only when Ron is one of the two who saw him; any pair without Ron stays at two or below. In the coat, one night seen by Ron reaches three only on day 3, before she can come for talk, and a second night with Ron holds it at three on day 4 or 5. So at three she comes in the first hour only if Ron saw him, on either night; at two, Dusan's sighting would bring her too.

What she can put to him

| She holds | She can | |---|---| | the street's talk | ask him where he was; nothing more | | statements from people who saw it but could not say who, however many | put the description to him | | a signed statement from someone who saw it was him | arrest: for a window, a constable's arrest (she does not come for damage); for a wounding, a robbery or a killing, hers; for a common assault, only a summons |

That follows the law of 1990 (production/research/police-response-1990/ARREST-2026-09-29.md): criminal damage was an arrestable offence even for a window, a common assault was not. The law also let an officer arrest on reasonable suspicion, talk included; she waits for a witness who names him, because that is what a charge needs, so that the street's talk alone never takes him. That is the game's choice, and yours to change.

A face somebody could pick out, short of knowing him, counts only as a description: no identification parade is modelled, which is a choice for you. In 1990 his silence could not be held against him; the caution's exact words are not yet found, so no line of hers quotes it.

Ada's tea

  • When: the evening of the outfit's second ask (the third day), for a man who has met her by then; she does not ask a stranger in. She asks him the first time she sees him that day, before nine: "There'll be a pot on at nine tonight, if you want it. I don't ask twice, mind." If she does not see him that day, she does not ask.
  • Coming means staying: to count as having come, he is there by half past nine and sits with her through half past ten, never away more than a few minutes. Dropping in and going is leaving early.
  • The test: the landing is open till one, but at the first hour's clock the walk from her step to the landing takes about two game hours, so a man who stays for his tea reaches the landing near one, if he hurries. And Ada keeps a window on Quay Street: if he goes down to the landing for the outfit that night, before his tea, after it or instead of it, she sees him go ("Mickey's nephew went off down towards the ferry, late, the night I'd asked him in for his tea"), and that is hers to tell like any sighting of his nights.
  • What it leaves: her regard for him. Sat with her, she thinks better of him, remembers it ("Mickey's nephew came for his tea and sat with me till gone half ten. There's more to him than they're saying."), and would no longer go to the police about him over something she saw. Left early, a little warmer, not enough for that. Stood up, cooler, and she remembers it ("I asked Mickey's nephew in for his tea. He never came. I'll not ask again."). A woman at a window who has not warmed to him is one who might go to DS Ellis. Never the game.
