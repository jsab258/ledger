Tom Nowak

The player. A principal of LEDGER (production/casting/CASTING.md); until 24 September the name was spelt Novak. Text only: face, clothes and any voice are the builder's, made to this sheet once it is approved.

Age
32 (born 1958)
Origin
Son of a Polish post-war settler and Mickey's sister. The family moved away from the port when he was small (the casting research's account, 7.3), so he is a stranger to everyone in the Hook, known only as Mickey's nephew by name (canon). Where he grew up and what he did there, canon does not say: see the question on the page.
Occupation
The new owner of Mickey's, the minicab office on Quay Street, with its half-dead outfit and book of uncollectable debts (canon). Arrives with one suitcase and a letter.
Build
About 5 ft 9 in (175 cm, the average man of 1993), lean; nothing about him says money.
Face
Clean-shaven (a shave read as trustworthy then), no tattoos, ordinary.
Hair
Short, dark brown, cut plainly: short back and sides.
Clothes, 1990
He arrives after the funeral, which he missed; his one good suit, a plain dark two-piece, is for the business of the inheritance. Day to day: jeans, a plain jumper or sweatshirt, leather shoes or trainers, a zip-front nylon or cotton jacket for the rain. One suitcase: he is seen in the same few things again and again, which the town will notice.
How he moves
A stranger's walk: head up (nobody in 1990 walks looking at a phone), reading shop boards and doorways, stopping where a local would not. The sheet does not make him a smoker (about three men in ten were).
Voice
The player's lines are typed, so Tom needs no voice. If he is ever to speak aloud he needs casting: of the approved men's voices one is 38 (Ron's) and the rest are 21 to 25, and none is his.

DS Carol Ellis

The detective. A principal of LEDGER (production/casting/CASTING.md); until 24 September she was Mara Ellis, and the old name survives only as an internal id (ellis). Text only: face, clothes and voice samples are the builder's.

Age
40 (born 1950)
Origin
A local woman who came up through the town's force. One of few women detectives then (the share of women in CID in 1990 is unsourced).
Occupation
Detective Sergeant, CID, in plain clothes. Which force Meridian's police belong to is not decided in canon, so the sheet names none. Canon: police are corruptible as individuals, never as a thesis; nothing on this sheet says which she is.
Build
About 5 ft 5 in, slim and upright; the average woman of 1993 was 5 ft 3 in.
Face
Minimal make-up; lines of tiredness rather than age.
Hair
Short and practical: a feathered bob, mid-brown.
Clothes, 1990
A navy or grey jacket and skirt, a plain blouse, tights, low court shoes or flat boots for walking, a shoulder bag, a belted raincoat. Skirt suits with modest shoulder pads were what working women wore.
How she moves
Brisk and economical. Writes in her notebook while she listens and lets silences run. Stands side-on in doorways, where she can see the room and the street. Procedure is her drama (Prime Suspect, 1991, is the reference for the procedure, not for her face).
Voice
Level and quick, a local accent, short questions with gaps left after them. Approved in July: p231 (a woman, 23, English, southern). Needs an older voice: 23 against 40, and southern against a local woman.
I'm not asking you to remember everything. I'm asking you to remember it the same way twice.
Write it down. Paper doesn't get tired and it doesn't change its mind.
You can talk to me here, or you can talk to me at the station. I'd rather here. So would you.

The story, in outline

For Jafar's approval, 28 September (town list, item 5). Under two pages, not a script. From canon, DECISIONS (D30, D33, D58), the July act drafts he approved (legacy/v1-era/act-drafts), the endings as built (ActThree.cs), the crime layer, and the research on authored stories in simulations and on British crime fiction. Where the July drafts assumed a pub, drink or dollars, this follows canon since: Mickey's is a minicab office.

The spine

A stranger inherits a position, not a business. Tom Nowak arrives with one suitcase and a letter; his uncle Mickey, dead three weeks, has left him Mickey's, a cab office on Quay Street in the Hook, a half-dead outfit and a book of debts nobody can collect. The town decides who he is by what it knows, and what it calls him says how far he has got: the new owner, Nowak, Tom, Tommy. Three questions in turn: what did you inherit; how big can it get before your two lives touch; which life was real.

Underneath, never explained: the dock labour scheme ended in July 1989 and more than half the registered dockers' jobs went. That is why the debts will not pay, why the outfit is half dead, and why a loud new crew can rise. The register is British noir, which runs on class, not greed: who may speak to whom, and how.

Arrival

He comes after the funeral, which he missed; June, Mickey's daughter, came back for it and is still in town, wanting nothing from the office. Sheila Dunn shows him round and stops at the one door she does not open; she promised Mickey to size him up. Ron Kirby, kept on since the docks let him go, calls him boss and watches him. Darren Milner is already on the pavement, friendly and owing. Ada watches from her window. By nightfall half the street has seen him and none of it knows him, and the first thing the town does is talk.

Act I: The Inheritance (the first week)

The most scripted act, as the research advises: the world is most predictable before the player changes it. Five threads, as approved in July: the outfit's claim (Mickey's arrangements outlive him; the first ask comes on night one, and refusing breaks his deal, not a quest); the warehouse fire, the founding rumour Alison Sedman will not drop and Ellis will not close; Mickey's real book, which Sheila shows when she trusts him or when Ellis's pressure forces her hand, with a page in Mickey's hand tying the fire to the outfit; the street deciding who he is, every act seen, retold and now shown; and someone worth being honest for, Alison (built) or Philip Danby (still to write). Ada's tea is the week's first test of which life he keeps. The week ends with Sheila, over the real book: "So which is it going to be?" Wind it down, take it over, or refuse to say; the street learns the answer.

Act II: The Squeeze

Opens when the business has grown. Each rival attacks a different ledger, and notices him only through what its own people see. Maureen Jensen, the Widow, and the dockside syndicate go after his people: poaching Ron, taxing his drivers. Geoffrey Agar and the old-money machine go after his clean money with paper: the office's private-hire licence, a solicitor's letter, an inspection. Danny Cammack's new crew go after his cover: noise on Quay Street that brings heat he did not earn. The Fixer sells true reads, a meeting, one truce. DS Carol Ellis joins the street's crimes to the warehouse fire: one case. One collision is guaranteed: an evening with his day life is interrupted by his night life at the door, and both worlds see each other. It ends at a sit-down with one head: accept, defy, or counter with what he knows.

Act III: The Ledger Comes Due (ruled in July: an audit)

A courteous letter naming a date: a VAT inspection by Customs and Excise, which ran VAT in 1990. The fare book has been lying since day one: too little laundered and the night money has no source, too much and a cab office on this street earned more than it could. The inspector brings one item each morning; Tom produces it or asks for it in writing. Sheila, if she trusts him, tells him where the lie holds. Ellis offers a trade: give her a rival, at the price that the street learns who talks; refusing her can still reach the best ending, but only through what the town believes. Someone in his crew may ask to take it over. On the last day he can reach only a few people, and whoever picks up is the campaign he played. Then the books open.

How the endings are reached (as built)

Never picked from a list: each is a state the world is in when the inspection closes, and when several fit, the stronger wins: Quiet, then Both, then the Straight Life, then the Kingdom. Quiet: he hands it to someone who can hold it (competence, loyalty, standing, no feud), never while he is hunted for a killing; three watch-only days show whether it holds. Both: the business survives, one friendship holds, the street does not hold his rackets as fact, Ellis's case is answerable, and the books both hold and make sense; rare, and by the July ruling not meant to be reached on a first playthrough (aimed at by the balance, not enforced). The Kingdom: the business survives and the books hold; he keeps what he built, and finds the people in it civil and no more unless one friend still counts him. The Straight Life: no business at the close, never built or sold up, and one friendship holds. Burn Both: none of these; what doing nothing produces. By D58 there is no redemption path, and every cost must be visible as it approaches, through Tom's own reading with its reasons (D33): a cost he could not see is a trap.

Rules the writing keeps

Beats fire on states, not on sequence; the only dates are those people in the story name (the week's end, the letter). No single beat can fail the run. The simulation decides who is alive, who knows what, who is where and how the law stands; the models only voice it. No drink, gambling or children; the town remembers everything, and now shows it.

Owed, already ordered

Act III's text in the Core still reads as a pub (the letter, the cellar, duty on stock, a cap on laundering set by bar takings): D58 made rewording it checkpoint work, and the cap must be re-derived for a cab office (fares against fuel, cars and drivers; VAT powers under the 1983 VAT Act, not the excise act).
